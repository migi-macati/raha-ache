Dala is a **fictional fisher and household claimant** introduced by name in Book III, Chapter 2, *The Red Wood Hearing*. Her son Pawi works with her. Their family rack has been damaged by a boat and its ropes; nets tear and fish escape. She identifies losses in a public hearing and corrects the keeper's tally when Pawi recalls that one basket was carried indoors before the accident. The correction reduces her own claim, but she insists that lost fish cannot simply be replaced by an offer to mend nets.

In Chapter 3 Dala and Pawi bring a net frame to the bank, where a grounded vessel obstructs their work. Dala prevents Pawi from entering unsafe water to help before the consequences are understood. Through Chapters 4–10, the route used for her catch and nets remains a practical limit on Ache's plans for steps, braces and landing access. A passage that serves boats may still cost her household a working path.

Dala's consent is specific to her own work and ground. She neither grants rights on behalf of every neighboring household nor accepts Ache's intervention as settlement of all her losses.

**Evidence boundary:** Dala, Pawi, their rack and the hearing are fictional. The novel does not establish an identifiable historical family, fishing-rights law or universal riverbank compensation procedure.
