## Historical context

Social life in sixteenth-century Luzon was structured by households, kinship, dependents, personal obligations and unequal status. Scott's *Barangay* describes distinctions conventionally rendered as **maginoo**, **maharlika**, **timawa**, and forms of **alipin** status, but categories differed across communities and colonial sources; they are **not a single immutable caste system**. The labels also cannot automatically be projected onto Brunei's court, whose institutions and titles followed different traditions.

Households could include relatives, clients, workers, debt dependents and enslaved people. Enslavement, debt bondage and captivity were consequential and coercive; avoid treating them as harmless household service.

**Story use:** a royal child's authority depends on people whose interests are not identical to his own. A decision affecting a dependent household can expose the limits of Ache's education. **Unverified:** specific childhood household size, servants, legal procedures, and rank titles in Ache's family.

**Research base:** William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (1994), especially social organization; [Project Gutenberg transcription of Morga's *Sucesos*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7001) for a later Spanish perspective requiring caution.

## Tagalog-specific status categories and conflict

Plasencia's 1589 [*Customs of the Tagalogs*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13701/13701-h/13701-h.htm) calls freeborn **maharlika** retainers with obligations to accompany their datu and at times row for him, while distinguishing **aliping namamahay** with homes, property and limited service obligations from the more restricted and saleable **aliping sa gigilid**. These were **not simply slaves in the same sense**. His account decries postconquest officials and claimants who classified namamahay dependents as gigilid and seized their children. This is **1589 later Tagalog evidence**, not a transcription of Ache's reign.

Scott's Tagalog chapters are a check on *maginoo* status and the inappropriate substitution of Visayan categories. Avoid flattening social life into timeless and rigid “noble/commoner/slave castes.” Service, marital position, inherited claims, debt, ransom, property and personal followings complicate rank.

**Book III story conflict:** a new written register turns a disputed obligation into a claim over a person's work or child. The case is **fictional** but illustrates a historically attested social distinction, and creates credible grievances against Ache's otherwise useful reforms.
