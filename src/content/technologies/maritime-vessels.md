## Historical anchors

Pigafetta describes **praus**, smaller boats and **junks** during the Brunei encounter of July 1521. These are broad European-rendered vessel labels, not a reliable inventory of standardized ship classes. A Chinese-style junk and a local plank-built craft could differ greatly in construction, rig, cargo capacity and crew.

The wider Austronesian maritime tradition included lashed-lug construction, sewn or edge-joined planking, outriggers on some vessels, and diverse sailing rigs. **Do not presume that every Bruneian warship used outriggers or that all vessels called praus were identical.** [Pierre-Yves Manguin, “The Southeast Asian Ship: An Historical Approach,” *Journal of Southeast Asian Studies* (1980)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-studies/article/abs/southeast-asian-ship-an-historical-approach/).

## Life and constraints aboard

Crew coordination depended on steering, lookout, sail handling, paddling or rowing where appropriate, repairs, water, food, sleeping space, discipline, and reliable pilots. Ships could be disabled by damaged steering gear, rigging, leaks, weather or sickness long before a decisive boarding action.

**Novel reconstruction:** assign a specific vessel a crew complement, provisions, dimensions, weapons and watch routine only as explicitly fictional design. Pigafetta's reported flotilla sizes do not establish Ache's exact Loue campaign force.

## Scene prompts

A pilot refuses an unsafe passage; the quartermaster argues over water; a commander sacrifices speed to keep a slower supply boat; an apprentice must distinguish the sounds of an ordinary working hull from a dangerous leak.

## Source caveat

The cited shipbuilding literature provides regional context, not an excavated plan of Ache's own vessel. Verify particular hull types against dated archaeological finds before fixing a design.
