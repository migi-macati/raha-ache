*Writer-facing development, contains possible future relationship spoilers. Ache's sexual and romantic attractions are **novel invention**: the surviving historical evidence does not establish his sexuality or private love life.*

## Canon decision

**In the fictional trilogy, Ache experiences attraction toward women and men.** This is an ongoing feature of his character and does not require an identity speech, fixed contemporary self-label, complete symmetry of attraction, a specific number of partners, or equal narrative attention to genders in every book.

The reader should recognize the pattern through sincere, differentiated feelings and choices. In internal narration Ache uses concepts and words plausible to his own life; the writer's archive can accurately call the **fictional characterization bisexual**, but dialogue should not insert the modern English label or modern coming-out scripts into circa-1500 conversations.

**Avoid:** bisexuality as a temporary “confusion” to be cured by marriage; constant infidelity; automatic love triangles; attraction that makes Ache disloyal by definition; claiming a historical rajah's orientation as verified.

### History and cultural distinctions

Early modern and early colonial Philippine accounts document a range of religious, gender and sexual practices through heavily filtered Spanish descriptions. **Gender-variant religious specialists are not proof that every community accepted every kind of intimacy, nor direct evidence of Ache's feelings.** Modern “gay,” “transgender,” “straight,” “bisexual” labels should not be treated as native administrative categories of his Maynila court.

Compare Carolyn Brewer, “[Baylan, Asog, Transvestism and Sodomy: Gender, Sexuality and the Sacred in Early Colonial Philippines](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/016dc513-4572-4a4e-89e5-d09475b75aa8)” (1999), with cultural histories and historically bounded Tagalog materials; direct evidence from Maynila c. 1500 is limited. Do not transpose Visayan **asog** to Tagalog usage unquestioningly or cast Ula's spiritual role as a generalized modern LGBT institution.

There may be social, political, family and religious constraints, but **their exact terms are situational research questions**, not a default narrative of universal acceptance or universal persecution. Preserve differences among Maynila, Brunei and later Spanish institutions without flattening Islam, local rites, and Christianity into monolithic sexual rules.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | Ache roughly **16–17** in the current canon; Sulad his peer | Awkward crushes, rivalries colored by attachment, wanting someone to choose to sit near him, teasing that lands badly, tentative and fully voluntary affectionate gestures, uncertainty over what feelings mean. | Sexualized description, eroticized bodies, sexual acts, adult-minor pairings, grooming or an adult mentor as romantic prospect. All Book I scenes should remain nonsexual. |
| **II** | An **adult** Ache in his Bruneian years after coming of age; verify the exact chronology for any planned encounter | Adult flirtation, meaningful reciprocity, love, an ended romance, a choice between intimacy and public duty, gentle affection, privately negotiated boundaries. Any sexual relationship remains between adults, entirely voluntary, with intimacy kept non-graphic/off-page. | A “sexual conquest” montage, coercion arising from rank, teacher–student exploitation, jealousy as the only relationship conflict, or using a partner to prove Ache's orientation. |
| **III** | Adult ruler, husband, later older man | A marriage that has its own imperfect companionship; meaningful old attraction and memory; romantic decisions that carry emotional and political consequences; mature tenderness or estrangement. | Equating marriage with heterosexual conversion, using male affection as a scandal-only device, reducing his wife to obstacle or assuming marriage and private longing always entail adultery. |

**Power matters.** Rank, paid service, captivity, teaching and political dependence can undermine real choice. If Ache is a commander/ruler and another person is his subordinate, the story must not treat their inability to refuse as romantic tension. For meaningful adult romantic partners, prefer peers with independent resources and a demonstrated ability to refuse him. Romantic refusal should be accepted without punishment.

## Chemistry is interaction, not explanation

A relationship feels alive when **two particular people change each other's behavior**:

- **Mutual attention:** one remembers a tiny habit the other thought nobody noticed.
- **Disagreement that sharpens attraction:** a peer wins an argument Ache expected to win; both remain attracted and angry.
- **Comfort:** silence while cleaning a boat, laughter at a private joke, an unremarkable meal made memorable by a companion.
- **Imperfect timing:** Ache attempts to be charming precisely when he should listen.
- **Restraint:** someone decides not to press an invitation, a touch, or a question.
- **Unequal feelings:** attraction need not be reciprocated and its absence does not make the other character cruel.
- **After-effects:** Ache misreads a compliment, is irritable the next morning, enjoys being seen, regrets having used an official title to end an awkward exchange.
- **Different tastes:** his interest in women need not feel identical to his interest in men; each individual relationship has its own emotional grammar.

**Don't** frame every friendship with a man as secretly erotic or every woman as a romantic possibility. Leave room for genuine platonic intimacy.

## Suggested relationship pathways (candidates, not forced pairings)

### Book I: Ache and Sulad — ambiguity with real agency

This is the richest **age-appropriate** thread because they are peers whose close friendship already has funny, defensive, and protective moments.

Possible subtext: after Sulad says he was afraid for Ache, Ache is more unsettled by that admission than by the admonishment about the sail; he notices when Sulad's easy smile doesn't return following the argument over his mother's cord; he may want Sulad's approval in a way he finds embarrassing. These observations can register as a *first crush, attachment or an emotional intimacy he cannot classify*. No presumption that Sulad reciprocates, and no sexual scene.

**What matters:** Sulad does not exist as Ache's automatic romantic reward. He has private wishes, a family, convictions and authority to say no. If a future chapter explores a confession or an affectionate gesture, both characters must have a clear choice, and it should create consequences beyond a tidy answer.

### Book I: possible attraction toward a girl Ache's own age

A peer in a harbor, household or festival setting can catch Ache's attention for her wit or competence, especially if she doesn't defer to his status. **Do not** use the rescued child from Chapter 1–2 as a crush, retroactively age up a child, or invent a disposable girl only to tick a representation checkbox.

**Better approach:** develop an existing appropriate-age peer or a new character with her own family, job, interests and later causal role. Until that role is ready, don't crowd the first three polished chapters with an arbitrary new meeting.

### Book II: Laila — attraction complicated by her independent future

Laila is an **adult** Bruneian political participant in any romantic Book II scene. Attraction can begin where she sees through Ache's court performance, but it must not stop at mutual cleverness. She may want to stay and govern in Brunei even when Ache wishes to return. A quiet evening, a carefully worded disagreement or a refused future promise can carry more chemistry than ceremonial flattery.

**Continuity warning:** whether fictional Laila is identical with Ache's historically attested unnamed cousin-wife is unresolved; do not silently assume so.

### Book II: Jalal — promising adult male-relationship candidate

Jalal has his **own Sulu family obligations, seafaring competence, humor and capacity to withdraw support**. That makes him a potentially interesting source of adult **mutual attraction** or an adult romance if the narrative confirms he is of age, independent of Ache's chain of command and **not a close relation**. Neither character's bisexuality or receptiveness follows automatically from one charged encounter.

Suggested escalation if chosen: playful rivalry -> an evening where a joke stops being a joke -> clear evidence of a voluntary, reciprocated affection -> a duty to Sulu that makes promises costly. An adult relationship may be tender and quietly intimate, but no explicit intimacy is required for the reader to believe it.

**Provisional, not established canon:** do not declare Jalal a romantic partner until the relationship is dramatized and it improves both character arcs. If the existing Jalal role proves too interwoven with patronage or age uncertainty, use a distinct adult peer instead.

### Book II: Safi — rivalry can be charged without becoming romantic

Safi's envy of Ache's access, and Ache's admiration of Safi's discipline, can produce an intense rivalry. It should not automatically become “enemies to lovers,” particularly while possible kinship, power and ages remain undetermined. Respect, jealousy, threat and attraction are not interchangeable, and Safi can remain a deeply consequential **platonic** rival.

### Book III: the wife, mature tenderness, and remembered possibilities

The historically reported Bruneian cousin-wife deserves a convincing inner life: preference, humor, frustrations, family and political authority. Their marriage can contain friendship, affection, argument, and attraction without requiring a perfect romantic ideal. Ache's attraction to men may remain part of his life and memory without implying a secret affair or erasing the wife's dignity.

A remembered adult relationship can become a poignant motif when both people choose separate obligations. Avoid dragging a youthful Sulad ambiguity through decades as a permanently unresolved “will they?” subplot if it prevents both men from living full lives.

## Sketch beats, not finished scenes

**Book I — quiet, age-appropriate ache of attention (Ache first person):** after arguing about his companion's inherited protective cord, Ache finds himself looking for a familiar joke that does not arrive. He's irritated by how much the silence matters. The feelings are *ambiguous*, with no physical sexualization.

**Book II — adult chemistry and political agency:** an adult peer catches Ache exaggerating his success in front of admirers. They argue cheerfully at first; the exchange turns serious when the other asks which crew paid for it. Ache notices he wants the conversation to continue even after losing. Later, the peer is free to decline both an invitation and Ache's political request.

**Book II — Laila's different chemistry:** Laila remembers Ache's favorite small ritual while challenging his plans for Brunei's resources. Her affection is authentic, and so is her refusal. A private conversation may end without reconciliation, while both still long for each other's company.

**Book III — memory without moralizing:** Ache sees an object associated with a past adult companion and remembers a promise he couldn't keep; his present family remains real, not a stand-in. The deeper ache is recognizing that his life contains choices he can't reverse.

## Editorial tests

1. Are the two people **actually individual adults** before any adult romantic intimacy? Book I's adolescent attachments stay nonsexual.
2. Can either person refuse the other's attention without retaliation, economic jeopardy or coercion by royal authority?
3. Does the scene tell us something **new** about both people, or is its sole point to confirm bisexual representation?
4. Would their goals still exist if they never became romantic partners?
5. Is bisexuality presented as a durable possibility for attraction, not confusion corrected by marriage or proof of promiscuity?
6. Does time pass naturally: early curiosity, adult experience, mature companionship and regret?
7. Is historical uncertainty properly disclosed in the archive, without stopping the chapter to lecture readers?
8. Is there **chemistry outside romance** as well—mentor friction, companion warmth, intellectual rivalry and family teasing?
