## Purpose and historical time

Research dossier for the existing first prose pass of Book I Chapter 3, “The Muslim Way.” Ache is approximately sixteen, before departure to Brunei and before 1521. His exact birth year, the landing's location, seasonal tide, local toll rules and his personal ritual practices are unverified. Keep these details fictional unless a dated source establishes them.

## Evidence and caution

- Aganduru Moriz (seventeenth century) retrospectively reports Ache's maternal connection to Brunei, his mother's rule and Tondo's encroachment. This does not document the chapter's trader, toll dispute, ritual elder or prayer.
- A 1572 Spanish *Conquest of Luzon* account describes coastal communities influenced by Bruneian Islamic teaching, while depicting their religious knowledge unevenly. Its missionary/colonial viewpoint and its distance from Ache's youth require caution. [Blair–Robertson, vol. III](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).
- Diego de Herrera's 1570 letter likewise questions how thoroughly Luzon's communities followed Islam. Treat his testimony as a contested outsider assessment, not a measure of Ache's faith. [Blair–Robertson, vol. XXXIV](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/47927/pg47927-images.html).
- Juan de Plasencia's Tagalog relations are dated 21 October 1589. They document ritual practices and the term *catolonan* in the English translation; they cannot verify Ula's particular red-thread rite decades earlier. [NCCA sourcebook](https://ncca.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/PHILIPPINE-HISTORY-SOURCE-BOOK-FINAL-SEP022021.pdf).
- Ethan Hawkley discusses China/Islam networks and interpreters in sixteenth-century Manila; evidence mostly concerns later decades. [World History Connected](https://journals.gmu.edu/whc/article/download/4400/2378?inline=1).
- Nick Joaquin's arguments about cultural transformation and “smallness” are interpretive provocations, **not primary evidence** of precolonial behavior. Do not make Ache think in modern national categories or presume indigenous crafts were inferior. [Labiste's 2024 critical study](https://www.plarideljournal.org/article/the-philippines-as-tool-forged-nation-revisiting-nick-joaquins-use-of-marshall-mcluhans-medium-theory-to-reconstruct-colonial-history/).

## Manuscript audit and scene plan

1. **Net repairs and Ula:** preserve her practical expertise, healing obligation and independence. Her red thread is invention. Avoid uncritically calling a Tagalog elder *babaylan*; verify *catolonan* before using it in prose.
2. **Hamid's detained ship:** tide, poles, cargo and damaged net are useful physical constraints. Exact fee rules and standardized customs administration are invention. Make crew work visible.
3. **Mother's counsel:** resolve a concrete continuity issue. Chapter 2's narrow marked cloth was kept among Ache's father's possessions; Chapter 3's narrow patterned cloth is said to come from Siripada via Hamid. Distinguish the two objects or reconcile provenance.
4. **Witnessed settlement:** specify whether the steward makes tally marks, writes a text, or gathers oral testimony. Avoid a modern paper-ledger bureaucracy. The fisher household and Ula must choose to help Ache for reasons of their own.
5. **Bruneian encounter:** Ache's partial comprehension of his mother's conversation in Malay is plausible but not documented. His morning prayer is likewise plausible fictional characterization; do not assert precise recitation or ritual chronology without evidence.
6. **Three blue poles:** preserve the mystery. Chapter 4 must explain who installed them, their claimed authority and the material consequences.

## Dramatic and character criteria

Ache's goal is to unload one vessel without provoking a fight. He succeeds provisionally because people outside his household testify, not because rank guarantees obedience. Ula protects her community's interests; Hamid tests Ache's competence; Sulad remains willing to fight; his mother models conditional accommodation. Faith must shape a concrete personal choice, or the chapter title overpromises.

## River-crossing geography and navigation audit — 8 October 2026, late pass

### A. What the evidence establishes, and when

**1570 voyage as narrated in the anonymous 20 April 1572 *Conquest of the Island of Luzon*:** Local interpreters/pilots warned Martín de Goiti's party that the coast approaching Menilla was dangerous and that **high tide was needed to enter the river** (Blair and Robertson, vol. III, printed pp. 72–73). The expedition sent men to sound the river mouth; its vessels later **crossed a bar at the entrance**, and the observer described a populated riverside settlement fronted by a palisade and Chinese trading vessels nearby (pp. 73–75). These are unusually useful *specific* observations for shallow-water navigation, anchoring, shore access, and the political importance of pilots. [Full translation with page markers](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13616/13616-h/13616-h.htm), narrative near the headings and printed pp. 72–75.

The same account mentions vessels carrying rice and salted fish in Manila Bay (pp. 71–72) and, later, the river as a route to upriver Cainta (the latter expedition occurred in 1571; see vol. III, around printed pp. 141–143). This supports a riverine-commercial setting, **not** Hamid's precise cargo, a sixteenth-century fixed tariff, or the exact layout of the fictional crossing. The 1572 text is a colonial expedition narrative, not an eyewitness account of Ache at age sixteen; its rhetoric, allegations and numbers are partisan and must not be reproduced as neutral fact.

**Modern physical analogues, not sixteenth-century measurements:** Paronda, David and Apodaca (2019), “River flow pattern and heavy metals concentrations in Pasig River, Philippines as affected by varying seasons and astronomical tides,” *IOP Conference Series: Earth and Environmental Science* 344, 012049, [doi:10.1088/1755-1315/344/1/012049](https://doi.org/10.1088/1755-1315/344/1/012049), observed high-tide upstream backflow in the dry season and stronger seaward flow in the wet season at low tide. Herrera and Naito (2024), “Hydrodynamic Investigation of Laguna Lake,” *Proceedings of IAHS* 386, 95–102, [doi:10.5194/piahs-386-95-2024](https://doi.org/10.5194/piahs-386-95-2024), models interaction among lake level, winds, sea level and river discharge. Modern dams, dredging, reclamation, water abstraction, pollution and altered bathymetry mean **none** of these studies establishes an exact 1500s current, salinity, river width, landing, shoreline or sailing schedule.

**Safe inference:** a loaded boat's draft, an exposed muddy landing, shifting tidal water levels, local pilot knowledge, poles and maneuvering room can create urgent constraints. **Unsafe inference:** a known sixteenth-century tide table, modern coordinates for the crossing, guaranteed reverse flow on a chosen day, or the assumption that every boat had to await high tide at every inland landing.

### C. Physical staging, to guide a targeted prose revision

**Spatial model (fictional, deliberately not georeferenced):** Hamid has passed the bay entrance with local guidance and is held at a **Maynila-bank landing** on a navigable reach near the contested north-bank approach. A shallow muddy shelf limits how close a laden hull can come; crew members hold position with poles and a stern line. The plank is not yet securely landed. Smaller craft can move between banks while Hamid's deeper-laden hull cannot easily shift berths. This provides a concrete reason for urgency without requiring a second historically attested customs station.

**Sequence and causality:**
1. Before Ache arrives, crew members have tried to hold the vessel clear of mud while a collector blocks unloading. Show who is on the line, who is on the pole, and how much of the hull's side is exposed.
2. Hamid knows from the boatmen that the falling water will leave the laden ship farther from usable bank; **this is a scene-specific judgment**, not a measured tide forecast. If the vessel has already cleared the bar, do not conflate entrance depth with the landing's shallows.
3. Ula knows exactly which workers removed the broken stakes after Chapter 1's accident. Their unpaid or uncredited work exposes the collector's unsupported claim to have maintained the channel.
4. Ache chooses to summon those witnesses, including people outside his household. The steward can *recite the competing claims before witnesses and mark a simple tally* if needed; avoid unspecified documentary writing. The collector concedes unloading **pending adjudication**, without surrendering Tondo's larger claim.
5. On the next visit, the three blue-marked poles are **not** net-repair stakes. They are the physical evidence of a new jurisdictional assertion that Chapter 4 must explain. Show their placement relative to the landing so the reader understands why they matter.

**Language/voice:** A knowledgeable boatman would distinguish grounding, depth, slack in a mooring line, and loss of a landing window. Avoid a modern harbor master's bureaucratic diction and technical exposition from Ache. Let Ache learn by observing a pole bite mud, a crewman's shouted correction, and the captain's irritation.

- **Critical:** replace “told our steward to write down” unless a plausible physical medium and its social function are established. Witnessed oral agreement is the safer default; the Chapter 2 proposal for paired tallies can remain a fictional political device.
- **Important:** make the faith theme change Ache's choice about whom to trust or protect, not simply his interior summary of plural practices. Avoid inventing formal theological positions for Ula.
- **Important:** do not locate the scene using today's Intramuros streets, modern bridge locations, or the 1734 coastline as though they were a contemporary survey.
- **Unresolved:** month/season, exact fictional berth and draft, Hamid's vessel type, material and provenance of the toll tally, and how Chapter 4 explains the blue poles.

## Scott cross-check: commercial control, not a modern customs regime (9 October 2026)

**Secondary synthesis checked:** William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (Ateneo de Manila University Press, 1994), Chapter 12, “Tagalog Society and Religion,” especially the discussion of chiefs' privileges, market access and arbitration (roughly printed pp. 221–223; confirm exact page in a clean copy before quoting). Scott describes chiefly restrictions on fishing areas, fees at strategic waterways, and upstream chiefs affecting Manila prices by delaying goods. These are **comparative Tagalog practices**, not evidence that the invented southern landing had a standardized north/south tariff. Text access for checking: https://studylib.net/doc/26210542/barangay---sixteenth-century-philippine-culture-and-society ; publisher bibliographic record: https://unipress.ateneo.edu/product/barangay-sixteenth-century-philippine-culture-and-society-1 .

**Primary account, two distinct evidentiary layers:** The *Relation of the Discovery of the Island of Luzon* (1570 expedition, preserved in Blair and Robertson, *The Philippine Islands*, vol. III, printed pp. 75 and 81) reports that Chinese merchants complained that Manila's rulers forcibly retained ship helms and goods without payment. Later in the **same** Spanish expedition narrative, the author reports that Goiti ordered the return of sails and helms after the fighting. This is internal corroboration that navigational gear was being held, **not independent confirmation** of the merchants' full allegation or of its motive. https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13616/13616-h/13616-h.htm . The narrator's self-presentation of Spanish protection of merchants is politically interested: the same narrative records Spanish seizure of Chinese goods elsewhere, and a later letter by Guido de Lavezares deplores Spanish plunder of Chinese junks (vol. III, printed pp. 264–265). Do not make Spanish benevolence the unquestioned baseline.

**Separate retrospective claim:** Scott also quotes a **1665 statement attributed to Lakandula's descendant Juan Macapagal** describing duties and anchorage fees paid by Chinese ships and removal of sails/rudders. This is an independent *later testimony* about ancestral rights, but its century-long distance and family-political interest require caution; it is not an eyewitness record of Ache's childhood. Distinguish it from the 1570 merchants' complaint rather than collapsing both into a proven formal tariff system. See Scott, Chapter 12, discussion of Tondo/Manila paramount chiefs (around printed pp. 220–222); page to recheck in publisher edition.

**Decision for Chapter 3 and handoff to Chapter 4:** Keep the captain's northern tally and disputed southern charge explicitly fictional. Ache wins provisional unloading through witnesses and bargaining; he has not established the legal status of either fee. In Chapter 4, make the concrete stakes observable: who moors at the marked poles, who is exempt, whose crews provide maintenance, who can delay goods, and which households choose one chief's protection over another. The novel should show personal allegiance and material dependence before announcing abstract sovereignty. No prose change is needed solely because this evidence was found.

**Outstanding:** Verify Scott's precise printed pages and original source citation for the 1665 testimony from a clean edition; investigate whether Chinese-language or other independent merchant testimony exists. Avoid presenting that testimony as found until located. Also inspect the chronological relationship between Ache's fictional childhood and the 1570/1665 evidence before adding details.
