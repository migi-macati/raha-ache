## Purpose and time discipline

**Editorial finding:** Ache's Maynila should never resemble an isolated place waiting for the Spanish to connect it with the world. By his lifetime, Luzon, Brunei, Sulu and parts of the Visayas were involved in older, shifting maritime circuits of goods, news, diplomacy, labor, religion and armed power. But there is no evidence that every foreign civilization with an artifact in the archipelago maintained a resident community in Maynila.

This dossier distinguishes **(1) objects made elsewhere, (2) merchants who actually traveled, (3) immigrant or expatriate residents, (4) concepts transmitted through intermediaries, (5) claims by powerful states, and (6) direct political rule**. The evidence for one is not evidence for all six.

Three chronological layers must never be collapsed:

- **Early history, c. 700–1400:** Srivijaya, the tenth-century Laguna copperplate, Chinese ceramics, imported religious forms, early Chinese descriptions of Philippine ports, the fourteenth-century Majapahit court and Sulu/China missions. They establish depth, not uninterrupted government or trade for Ache.
- **Ache's plausible youth and Brunei career, around 1500–1521:** merchants, court diplomats, Malay/Bruneian intermediaries, Asian texts and goods, the rise of Malacca and its conquest by Portugal in 1511, the Brunei-related voyage reports of 1521. Direct Luzon–Brunei–Malacca circulation is visible in Tomé Pires's *Suma Oriental*, composed c. 1512–1515.
- **Ache's later life, especially 1565–1572:** Spanish expansion, Japanese merchants encountered at Maynila in 1570, increased documentary visibility and contestation over what it meant to rule or trade. Large formal Chinese and Japanese settlements recorded later are **not automatically part of Ache's youth**.

The text is a research synthesis with explicitly identified limits, not a demonstration that all details were personally witnessed by named figures.

## 1. Chinese worlds: commerce, objects, diplomacy, and constraints

### Material evidence

The National Museum reports Chinese high-fired stoneware and porcelain extending from the late Tang era through later centuries and from numerous archaeological sites, including Santa Ana in Manila, Calatagan, Butuan and elsewhere. Imported ceramic vessels were practical goods, prestige displays and burial objects—not a uniform sign of citizenship, settlement or religion. Vietnamese and Thai ware is also part of the collection. [National Museum, Stoneware and Porcelain](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/archaeology/stoneware-and-porcelainware/).

Scenes: a locally owned Chinese jar used for storing water, a bowl reserved for distinguished guests, a repaired dish passed between households, a trader disputing the value of wares. Avoid the assumption that all blue-and-white porcelain was current at any point in Ache's childhood; consult dating by type.

### Contemporary records of exchange

Song/Yuan accounts such as Zhao Rukuo's *Zhu Fan Zhi* (c. 1225) and Wang Dayuan's *Daoyi Zhilüe* (fourteenth century) describe maritime trade localities interpreted as lying in or near the Philippine archipelago. The precise identification of *Ma-i* and some other place-names remains disputed. Chinese texts often use court-centered diplomatic language—tribute, submission, barbarian land—that must be treated as a foreign documentary genre, not accepted as territorial control.

Ming records also mention Luzon and other regional delegations. Scott's [*Filipinos in China before 1500*](https://ia800104.us.archive.org/31/items/FilipinosInChinaBefore1500/Filipinos%20in%20China%20before%201500.pdf) discusses the 1405 combined Luzon and Java mission, and Pangasinan missions in the years immediately after. These **precede Ache**; a later Luzon ruler is not automatically descended from a named Ming-court envoy.

The rise of Ming restrictions on unlicensed maritime commerce and the practical movement of coastal merchants generate useful conflicts between commercial interests and state policy. A Ming maritime prohibition is not proof that ships ceased sailing or that all traders represented the Ming state.

### Presence in Maynila

For **Ache's youth**: Chinese merchants and crews are historically plausible; imported Chinese goods are overwhelmingly defensible. Treat permanent residence, the scale of any Chinese quarter, particular dialects, and the names of individual wholesalers as fictional reconstructions unless established from sources. Southern Chinese ports, especially Fujian, are important in the regional network, but no Chinaman merchant character should be made a representative ambassador of an imperial China by default.

For **1570–72**: commercial traffic becomes better documented alongside the Spanish. Still avoid backdating the later Spanish-period *Parián*, galleon-export system or seventeenth-century communities into Book I. A locally established commercial broker may have many obligations beyond one monarch's policy.

**Story conflicts:** Tondo versus Maynila over access to importers; duties versus gift exchange; merchant credit versus a patron's power to detain cargo; Chinese restrictions versus merchants willing to evade them.

## 2. The Indian subcontinent: real influence, often indirect contact

### Materials documented around 1512–1515

Tomé Pires reports Bornean traders taking **cloth from the “Kling”/Indian coastal trade and Bengal** and **glass beads from Cambay/Gujarat**, alongside Chinese-made metal goods, through Malacca into regional island markets. These goods could reach a Luzon buyer via **Brunei and Malacca**, even if the buyer never encountered the Indian manufacturers. [Pires, *Suma Oriental*, vol. I, pp. 133–134](https://archive.org/stream/McGillLibrary-136385-182/136385_djvu.txt).

This is a more secure choice for immersion than arbitrarily inventing a resident Gujarati merchant family in Maynila: Ache can wear or bargain for Indian textile without knowing the exporter, and the broker can narrate the layered journey of the goods.

### Older Indic forms

Sanskrit-derived terms, Indic-related scripts and Hindu-Buddhist artistic ideas entered maritime Southeast Asia through prolonged and selective local adoption. The [900 CE Laguna Copperplate Inscription](https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol70/iss2/2/) embodies a cosmopolitan **Old Malay, Indic-script, Javanese-connected** documentary setting, not a demonstrable colony of Indian administrators.

The Agusan gold image shows a related Hindu-Buddhist artistic horizon, though its exact identity is contested; even a securely identified religious image would not establish that all Tagalogs shared the depicted practice. [Cultural Center of the Philippines, Agusan gold image](https://epa.culturalcenter.gov.ph/3/82/2191/).

**Story conflicts:** a skilled scribe or elder inherited a ritualized title or debt formula from foreign-derived traditions, but adapts it to local circumstances. No “Hindu empire of Manila” without evidence.

## 3. Srivijaya: an earlier maritime inheritance, not Ache's overlord

Srivijaya centered on Sumatra and connected ports in the early and middle first millennium's later centuries. The [National Museum](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2022/09/20/the-9th-to-10th-century-archaeological-evidence-of-maritime-relations-between-the-philippines-and-the-islands-of-southeast-asia/) presents Srivijayan trader activity in the Philippines around the eighth–eleventh centuries as a **possibility**, not proof of annexation. Discussions of ancient Indonesian networks have long mixed plausible cultural transmission with empire-size assumptions.

Lourdes Rausa-Gomez's [“Sri Vijaya and Madjapahit” (1967)](https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol15/iss1/4/) is indispensable to the debate; a later [Bangko Sentral / *Ginto* synthesis](https://www.bsp.gov.ph/Media_And_Research/Publications/Ginto.pdf) observes a lack of firm epigraphic proof of either empire's government of the Philippines.

In Book I, a trader or historian may recall an older Sumatran tradition through legends or a prestige object, but **a living Srivijayan ambassador in 1500 is historically misplaced**. Its legacy should appear through institutions, religious terminology, artistic choices, or the movement of heirlooms across centuries, without specific provenance invented as fact.

## 4. Majapahit and Java: past imperial prestige, ongoing trade

Majapahit's fourteenth-century *Nagarakretagama* is a Javanese royal praise-poem describing the reach and grandeur of its court. Some readings associate named places with the southern Philippine world. Such readings do not independently establish a **1365 Majapahit conquest of Manila**, precise borders, garrisons or direct tax collection there. Place-name identifications such as Saludang/Selurong should be examined individually and cannot be treated as a proven identification with Maynila merely because a later similar name exists.

From an Ache-era perspective, **Java remains real and significant even as Majapahit's old imperial order declines or changes**. Java is not a vanished civilization. Traders, marriage brokers, mercenaries, religious teachers, shipbuilders and regional rivals operate in shifting states and ports.

The European report of the 1521 Loue campaign describes the opposing allegiance as belonging to “Java Major.” This is evidence of a **European report of rival political allegiance**, not decisive identification of a Majapahit royal government or an actual Javanese garrison at Loue. See [Brunei relationship research](/raha-ache/research/brunei-relationship/).

**Story conflicts:** a Bruneian official interprets a Loue port's allegiance as rebellion; a Javanese-linked trader calls it commercial protection; Loue residents defend a different account. All can believe themselves lawful.

## 5. Malays, Bruneians, Sulu, and connected Southeast Asian crews

This is the **strongest arena for named, physically present foreign actors** in the trilogy. The category “Malay” also needs care: port languages, ancestry, commercial practice, religious affiliation and political allegiance do not necessarily correspond.

### Historical witness: Pires

Between 1512 and 1515, Pires reports that the *Luções* took goods to **Brunei and then Malacca**, alongside gold, food, wax and honey, and lived or traded in Malay-region ports. He mentions roughly **500 Luções in Minjam** (modern locality identification should be verified), including wealthy merchants. Pires's numerical estimate and view of polity structure should not be converted into an Ache-era population census. [Pires, *Suma Oriental*, vol. I, pp. 133–134](https://archive.org/stream/McGillLibrary-136385-182/136385_djvu.txt).

**Major implication:** the voyage is bidirectional. Ache can meet Luzon traders **already abroad**, without himself being the first Luzon person to see a Bornean or Malaccan market. Foreign merchants can be born in Southeast Asia or have lived in multiple ports; a household may be mixed-language and mixed-kin.

### Brunei

Brunei is a political and trading center shaped by the growth of Muslim networks and by Portuguese seizure of Malacca in 1511. Dadi Darmadi's [study](https://studiaislamika.ppimcensis.or.id/index.php/studia-islamika/article/view/1390) describes how the resulting power realignment affected Brunei's commercial and Islamic influence.

Ache's reported Bruneian royal kinship and military service cannot establish that Brunei continuously administered Maynila as a bureaucratic province. No uniform “Bruneian occupation” should be retrofitted into Book I's local succession crisis.

### Sulu, Mindanao, Moluccas, and other ports

The world of shipping also runs through **Sulu, eastern Mindanao and the Moluccas**, with islanders, coastal polities, visiting traders, itinerant religious scholars and sea peoples. Cesar Adib Majul's [study of Sulu genealogy](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arch_0044-8613_1981_num_22_1_1677) warns against reading tarsilas as straightforward annals; modern archaeological synthesis likewise shows diverse communities and sources of political identity. [Sea Nomads, Sultans, and Raiders (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11457-025-09481-3).

The Philippines were not a culturally undifferentiated port complex. Sulu communities do not exist merely to supply Ache with recruits or adventures. Local people decide whose access is allowed, which goods move, whether a religious teacher is welcomed, or whether a captain is called an ally or raider.

**Story conflicts:** competing safe-conducts and tribute arrangements; marriage as diplomacy; shifting maritime passage; cargo and credit; distinctions between religious identity and political obligation.

## 6. Arabs and Persians: people, exchange and Islam are distinct questions

Islamic ideas reached Sulu, Mindanao, Brunei and some Luzon ruling circles through **overlapping networks** over generations. Traditions name Arab or Persian missionaries, Sufi teachers and ancestral figures; their historical chronology and origin are not equally secure. Some carriers were Malay, Javanese or Chinese Muslims. Majul's [“Islamic and Arab Cultural Influences in the South of the Philippines”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-southeast-asian-history/article/abs/islamic-and-arab-cultural-influences-in-the-south-of-the-philippines/7C404F49AB5930A3C3F2F857B6BA402A) and [Sulu-genealogy analysis](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arch_0044-8613_1981_num_22_1_1677) are useful starting points.

The Philippine archaeological record also contains **Persian or other West Asian ceramics**, but because trade routes are multistage their arrival does not establish that a Persian merchant personally sold the pot at the excavated site. [NCCA, Ceramic Age](https://ncca.gov.ph/about-culture-and-arts/culture-profile/prehistory-of-the-philippines/the-ceramic-age/).

For Book II, make Muslim scholarly practice, legal interpretations, ritual habits, and courtly patronage **internally debated**, rather than depicting Islam as a single foreign package. A Bruneian Muslim, a visiting jurist, a Sulu captain and a Tagalog royal household can disagree about practice, authority and duty.

**Do not equate “Moro” in a later Spanish report with Arab ethnicity**; it is a Spanish classification with an Iberian history. Do not equate Ache's Muslim identity with an all-Muslim polity.

## 7. Japan: stronger evidence around 1570, tighter controls earlier

Ubaldo Iaccarino's [2014 article, pp. 155–158](https://www.ostasien-verlag.de/zeitschriften/crossroads/cr/pdf/CR_10_2013_155-194_Iaccarino.pdf) reconstructs Japanese maritime trade with north and west Luzon and argues voyages preceded 1565. Most significantly for the book's endpoint, he cites a Spanish archival record for **Japanese merchants found in Maynila when Martín de Goiti arrived in May 1570**, including a Christian merchant called **Pablo (Paulo)**. This is a concrete, historically grounded opportunity for **Book III Bonus Chapter 11**: the city's people at the Spanish arrival are not all Tagalog or Spanish.

But the evidence for permanent Japanese communities on a large scale, red-seal expeditions, Japan–Manila formal diplomacy and Japan's Christian merchant networks becomes far stronger **after the 1570s**, especially the 1580s–1600s. Do not transplant that world into Ache's teenage years or use the stereotype that every Japanese merchant is a sword-bearing samurai. Japan was politically fragmented; merchants had varied ethnic, religious and regional interests.

**Earlier years:** suggest news or goods carried by multiple intermediaries, or a cautiously fictional itinerant Japanese sailor in northwestern Luzon if the plot warrants it. Do not put a documented Japanese town in Maynila circa 1500.

**1570–72:** Japanese visitors and potentially their knowledge of Portugal, Christianity and firearms can offer a counterpoint to the arriving Castilians—but an individually documented visitor must not receive invented motives disguised as biographical fact.

## 8. Ryukyu: the route is better established than a Manila colony

Shunzō Sakamaki's [“Ryukyu and Southeast Asia”](https://rhinoresourcecenter.com/wp-content/uploads/2006/12/1175862509.pdf) reconstructs Ryukyu's important trade links, and scholarship on early modern East Asian maritime history identifies Ryukyu as an interregional transshipment system. [Cambridge, “Oceans of History, Seas of Change”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-asian-studies/article/oceans-of-history-seas-of-change-recent-revisionist-writing-in-western-languages-about-china-and-east-asian-maritime-history-during-the-period-15001630/60BF038D98F14B102E04E9F28F0BF6E8).

The direct **paper trail of Ryukyuan state correspondence with Luzon is not equally strong**. Scott notes its apparent absence in the surviving records of Okinawan maritime trade; disappearance of records and merchant voyages outside state records remain possible explanations.

For the novel, let goods and route intelligence pass through **Fujian–Ryukyu–Japan–Luzon** networks without claiming that a Ryukyuan expedition to Ache's Maynila is documented. A specific Ryukyuan-speaking character would be a stated fictional composite.

## 9. Siam, Vietnam/Champa, South China Sea and the mainland shores

Thai and Vietnamese ceramics occur in excavated Philippine tradeware. The [National Museum's catalog](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/archaeology/stoneware-and-porcelainware/) is a first source for objects, with exact dates needing type-specific checks.

This is a **different level of claim** from asserting a resident Siamese envoy, a Cham mosque or Vietnamese quarter in Ache's Maynila. Commercial goods and secondhand news are strong ways to imply the wider geography. Some artisans and pilots might have traveled through these ports, but individual cases need evidence or a clear fiction marker.

The wider route ecology changes the price and availability of cloth, ceramics, spices, aromatic woods, salt, gold, metals and provisions. War, monsoon failure, debt or a new customs impost at a port far away can matter before the ruler in Maynila even learns its name.

## 10. Europeans: late newcomers to an already inhabited trading world

### 1511–1515: Portuguese conquest and commercial intelligence

Portugal's seizure of **Malacca in 1511** disrupted older trade arrangements. Pires, writing there in **1512–1515**, knew about Luzon by report; this is **evidence Europeans had commercial knowledge of Luzon before Magellan**, not proof that Pires or a Portuguese squadron reached Maynila. [Pires, *Suma Oriental*](https://archive.org/stream/McGillLibrary-136385-182/136385_djvu.txt).

Ache can hear of armed foreigners taking Malacca **from an Asian merchant** while still in Brunei. The report could be mistaken or partial and should affect prices, protection arrangements, fear, opportunism and court factions.

### 1521: Castilian-funded expedition with a multinational crew

The Magellan/Elcano expedition was financed under the Castilian crown, included Portuguese and other Europeans, and came through the Pacific into Philippine waters. The encounter with the unnamed royal Luzon commander serving Brunei is preserved in expedition testimony, with identity and details needing comparison among witnesses. [The archive's 1521 evidence audit](/raha-ache/research/brunei-relationship/).

Ache's defeat and capture are not a first meeting between an isolated islander and the concept of “Europe”; he could already have heard reports from Malacca. What is new is **the ships, force, practical behavior and personal stakes of direct encounter**.

### After 1521: further encounters, Spanish settlement, Maynila

Further European voyages entered the wider archipelago in the decades after 1521. By **1565**, Legazpi established a Castilian foothold in Cebu; **1570–1571** brought the struggle over Maynila; Ache's life closes in the documented 1572 context. On exact events, use the project's primary-source records for 1570–72 rather than a panoramic “arrival of Europe” montage.

Avoid the common anachronism of importing the later Manila–Acapulco galleon city's social, institutional and economic arrangements into the **pre-1571 port**. The city had a world before it became a colonial capital.

## 11. Eight concrete setting and character tests

1. **A crowded Maynila waterfront:** boats carrying goods from distinct ports; some foreigners physically present; most “foreign” objects already passed through several Asian owners.
2. **An older traded object:** a relic of tenth–fourteenth-century exchanges appears in a family's memory, but without a narrator certifying a continuous empire.
3. **The Chinese broker's dilemma:** a captain says a cargo is already promised to Tondo; Ache's mother wants guaranteed deliveries; the trader fears political retaliation.
4. **A Brunei trader's Malacca news:** Portuguese seizure in 1511 turns distant imperial rivalry into a local price and alliance problem.
5. **A cloth's route:** Indian-cotton material bought by a Malaccan intermediary reaches Borneo and then Luzon; the fabric tells a more precise story than an invented “Indian colony.”
6. **Loue's divided allegiance:** Brunei calls its demands legitimate, Java-linked merchants say another compact holds, and local residents refuse both readings.
7. **Japanese merchant in 1570:** the documented presence of merchants—including the reported Christian Pablo—shows multiple overlapping Asian/European worlds in Maynila before the Spanish victory.
8. **The record and the memory:** a future Spanish chronicler records one set of alliances; a Chinese commercial note or Bruneian genealogical claim preserves another. Neither may be a transparent window onto Ache.

**Narrative constraint:** a first-person narrator can misunderstand foreigners; the story bible cannot silently share the error.

## 13. Research questions, sorted by urgency

**Before revising Book I Chapters 1–3:** consult Scott's Tagalog-specific discussion of Chinese merchants, cargo, jewelry, imported wares and coast-to-interior movement. Verify which ceramic and textile examples fit *Maynila specifically* and c. 1500.

**Before revising Book I Chapter 10 and Book II Chapters 1–2:** review Pires's *Suma Oriental* pp. 133–134 against original Portuguese spellings; distinguish Borneans, Luções, Malacca merchants and Indian-made goods. Review Pigafetta's Brunei descriptions without projecting them backward into every Bruneian decade.

**Before Book II Loue Chapters 6–8:** check *Java Major* in Pigafetta and alternate editions of expedition testimony; study Javanese merchant roles and the changing post-Majapahit order. Avoid citing the *Nagarakretagama* as proof of a Luzon garrison or Loue administration.

**Before Book III bonus 11:** check the original Goiti 1570 document cited by Iaccarino (AGI Patronato 24, r. 17) for the number, identity and location of Japanese merchants and the report about Pablo. Audit presence of Chinese traders with the 1570–71 relations independently.

**Before attributing personal ancestry:** verify the Arab/Persian/Sulu genealogies and their dates. A written tarsila may preserve social legitimacy without guaranteeing the exact route or ethnic identity of a named early teacher.

**Before publishing a historical map:** map connections as **trade routes, reported encounters, archaeological sites and asserted political influence**, using different lines or symbols. Do not draw Srivijaya or Majapahit borders over Luzon without disputed-status labeling.

**Further research:** maritime Chinese written sources (Zhao Rukuo, Wang Dayuan, Ming Shilu); Spanish primary records on Japanese merchants in 1570; Agoo/Cagayan/Japan routes; Ryukyuan documentation; India-to-Malacca fabrics and Gujarati bead trade; dating of Manila-area religious materials.

**Source discipline:** these are distinct levels of attestation. References to seventeenth-century artifacts or settlements cannot be silently backdated; later written sources are not firsthand evidence of a tenth-century court; foreign royal claims do not prove direct sovereignty; Pires's commercial intelligence is contemporary but secondhand for Luzon; the specific Japanese merchant appearance is supported through a modern scholar's citation of a 1570 archival record still to be inspected directly.
