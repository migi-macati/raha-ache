## The historically defensible central picture

**Maynila was not a remote settlement waiting for the Spaniards to introduce exchange, foreign residents, manufacturing, or political organization.** Its larger river and bay environment participated in Asian circuits. By **1570** an anonymous Spanish expedition account explicitly describes Chinese and Japanese **residents** in Maynila, visiting Chinese shipping, locally controlled river defenses, artillery, buildings, imported goods, and a substantial store of metal and gun-casting equipment. **This is the strongest local first-contact snapshot**, but it is not a passport to move every detail unchanged back to Ache's probable youth around **1500**.

The fictional reconstruction of early Maynila should therefore rely on **three distinct types of evidence**:

1. **Earlier local archaeology:** Santa Ana's eleventh–fourteenth-century burial and habitation evidence of locally used Chinese wares, earthenware, animal bones, shellfish, beads, iron debris and cooking technology. This precedes Ache and is **upriver**, not a detailed excavation of Ache's bay-mouth house.
2. **Contemporary external commercial intelligence:** Tomé Pires's *Suma Oriental*, composed **1512–1515**, documents **Luções** participating in the Luzon–Borneo–Malacca commercial sphere. He **did not personally inspect Ache's Maynila** and cannot supply a street plan or list of its residents.
3. **Later direct observations or interviews:** the **1570 Spanish relation**, **1571–72 records** and **Plasencia's 1589** Tagalog ethnography. These yield detailed textures and social arrangements but belong to **different times and authors' purposes**.

The guiding revision is: **show physical systems before explaining networks**. Who brings rice; who can move a boat; whose net occupies a fishing place; who stores a visitor's cargo; who can veto a departure; how servants work; who knows a foreign language; which debts remain after a prince sails away?

**Terminology:** the *Luções* of Portuguese commercial writing, *Moro* of Spanish polemic, and later *Tagalog* social categories are not exact synonyms. A trader from the wider Luzon region need not be a resident or citizen of Ache's Maynila.

## II. Entering by water: river, tides, passage and boundaries

The 1570 relation presents **navigation as an exercise in local expertise**. An interpreter and his brother, natives of Maynila, counsel the foreign expedition not to anchor at the town immediately. They say it is necessary to wait for **high tide to enter the Pasig**. The party sends people to **sound the river mouth**, waits for a meeting, and travels along a **thickly settled coast** before observing the palisade and armed gate at the town. From another anchorage it describes cultivated slopes. [1570 voyage, pp. 72–75](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

### What a reader should experience

An early-day opening should foreground changing **water level**, loaded draft, rowing and sails, sandbars, shore calls and the judgment of pilots. The soundscape can include splashing paddles, cargo jars knocking, fishermen calling over nets, rigging and the instructions shouted by people who know which landing is navigable at which tide. These are **dramatic reconstructions**, not verbatim descriptions of a 1500 harbor.

The important distinction: **a river bank is not a continuous undifferentiated public quay**. Different households or patrons may use and defend particular fishery and landing rights. Plasencia (1589) reports villages where chiefs controlled **fishing places and certain sections of river markets** and could demand payment from outsiders. Applying this principle to a specified Maynila landing circa 1500 is plausible **fiction, not a documented tariff**.

### Scene engines

- A tide makes a foreign cargo vessel wait. The captain holds someone responsible for spoiled perishables.
- A household objects when a court messenger orders its fishing stakes cleared for a visiting crew.
- A pilot becomes temporarily more authoritative than a young prince: technical ability makes formal rank insufficient.
- A distant threat appears as a disrupted route or absent vessel, not only as a herald's dramatic proclamation.

**Setting correction:** avoid a precise stone-walled Intramuros streetscape, a modern deepwater port or an uninterrupted causeway. The **palisade** is witnessed **in 1570**, not verified as an identical fixture in 1500.

## III. Houses and households: different ways of belonging

### Physical and domestic life

The 1570 source reports **Sulayman's house** as large, holding gold/money, iron, copper, porcelain, cotton, blankets, wax and vats of alcohol. It describes a separate nearby **storehouse** with iron and copper, existing and unfinished guns, and casting molds. These are **observations and reports after a destructive assault**: some contents were seen amid ruins; other details were relayed by witnesses. The writer may exaggerate the scale or frame events to justify violence. [1570 relation, pp. 82–83](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

The archeological Santa Ana material preserves earlier **locally made cooking earthenware and a stove**, imported vessels used in local contexts, and food refuse (shells, animal bones). It supports a **lived domestic economy** but cannot specify the exact kitchen of Ache's mother. [National Museum, Earthenware](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/archaeology/earthenware/); [NCCA, Ceramic Age](https://ncca.gov.ph/about-culture-and-arts/culture-profile/prehistory-of-the-philippines/the-ceramic-age/).

### Labor and inequality

Plasencia's **1589** account distinguishes freeborn retainers, **aliping namamahay** with households and possessions but real service obligations, and **aliping sa gigilid**, often more severely dependent and saleable. These are not interchangeable; some obligations pass to descendants. A prince's household necessarily contains people who prepare provisions, row, maintain vessels, collect payments and do chores, yet their exact 1500 ranks must be historically checked. [Plasencia, *Customs*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13701/13701-h/13701-h.htm).

**Narrative implication:** Ache's mother governs more than a decorated hall. Decisions redistribute labor, provisions, debts, rights and punishments. A worker may obey while resenting the prince, and a resident may belong to a household through ties more complicated than an anachronistic wage-contract.

### Guardrails

Do not portray the entire settlement as uniformly prosperous because a ruler had porcelain; grave goods and houses can reveal **inequality**. Do not use household servants as interchangeable extras or euphemize enslavement. Do not label every imported vessel as a luxury: context determines value.

## IV. The market is a network of contested permissions

The 1570 relation reports **four Chinese vessels** anchored near the town, their crews offering gifts to Spaniards and **complaining** that the Maynila rulers had taken ships' **helms/rudders and some goods without payment**. This may reveal a coercive local practice or a biased complaint; it does not establish a universally enforced right of Maynila rulers to seize any foreign ship. The same document describes Chinese traders selling imported goods and buying **wax**. [1570 relation, pp. 75, 83](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

A market transaction can have multiple stages:

1. Cargo waits on the right water or tide window.
2. A pilot or broker obtains a landing or shore meeting.
3. Someone asserts passage, fishing, storage or market rights.
4. Traders bargain over goods, credit, weights, reputation or reciprocal gifts.
5. Rowers, porters, dependents and households absorb delays or losses.
6. A gift, unpaid debt or insult alters the political bargain between patrons.

**All six steps are a plausible dramatic model, not a recovered Maynila legal code.** Specific documents, tally boards and dual-clerk systems in existing Chapter 2 remain **novel-canon inventions** unless an Ache-era witness proves them.

### Goods by evidence layer

| Item | Historical basis | Do not assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chinese ceramics | Santa Ana earlier archaeology; 1570 household and vessels | Each jar was made in a single Chinese kiln at a known year or bought from an ethnic Chinese vendor |
| Local earthenware and cooking pots | Santa Ana archaeological collections | All kitchens in 1500 used exactly the same stove as c. 1200 |
| Rice, salted fish, hens, provisions | Boats and gifts in 1570 voyage relation | A modern-style permanent market with identical offerings every day |
| Wax and honey | Pires c. 1512–15; wax bought by Chinese in 1570 | Every shipment came from Maynila itself or all suppliers were free households |
| Cotton and cloth | Pires trade via Borneo/Malacca; 1570 house and Chinese goods | Every fabric's manufacturer was personally present at Maynila |
| Iron, copper, cannon, molds | 1570 storehouses/workshops | The precise foundry plan or cannon dimensions in Ache's childhood |
| Gold and silver | Pires and Spanish trading reports; Plasencia's land/payment notes | A fixed official exchange rate across Maynila's entire regional network |

For **Book I Chapter 1**, a young prince who has never priced a damaged imported jar, compensated a fishery owner, or considered someone else's missed tide is more convincing than one delivering a lecture about silk routes. The currently published chapter already has a usable cargo accident and compensation hearing. Expand material specificity in later revision only where it sharpens causal consequences.

## V. People and languages: the residents are not simply “foreigners”

### The 1570 resident count

The Spanish relation says Maynila contained **forty married Chinese and twenty Japanese**. It further mentions **Chinese wives** of Chinese men who had married and settled in town, seized during the assault. This is substantial **residential** evidence in a directly attested period, exceeding the weaker formula “a Japanese trader happened to be visiting” previously used in our archive. It also reveals how war and captivity could affect **noncombatant foreigners**. The writer reports a Japanese **Pablo** who identified himself as Christian; his alleged role among Maynila's bombardiers is only **“people say.”** [1570 relation, p. 82](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

The numbers are **a reported count**, not a verified census. They do **not** establish a Japanese merchant ward, Chinese ethnic monopoly, Cantonese as a universal local language, or the same household numbers in **1500**.

### A named Maynila intermediary: Mehomete

The source gives a vivid example of individual mobility: **Mehomete/Mahomete**, a **Maynila-born supplier** who came to the Spanish base in **Cebu** to sell food, converted with his wife and a son before the 1570 expedition, traveled back as an interpreter, and worked through a brother and other kin in Maynila. His brother is reported to be married there and returned to his own house as part of the negotiations. [1570 relation, pp. 72–79](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

He is a particularly rich model for a **Book III bonus-chapter secondary first-person viewpoint** or for the moral complexity of the encounter: is he protecting family, profiting, bridging worlds, choosing a new religion, or serving an invader? **We cannot know his motives from the relation.** If included, his internal life must be clearly fictional.

### Earlier Maynila and language

Before 1521, traders from Luzon operated in Brunei and Malacca (Pires). Tagalog or other local languages were used among households; **Malay** served trading and courtly networks in the wider region, and some brokers could manage more than one language. We lack a precise list of what Ache, Sulad, his mother, or any 1500 resident could speak or write.

A multilingual scene should portray **uneven competence**: greetings versus numbers, kin terms versus precise prices, polished court formulas versus shouted nautical instructions. A powerful person may be unusually dependent on an interpreter. **Do not make all Asians automatically mutually intelligible or universally fluent in Malay.**

## VI. Belief and belonging

Ache's Muslim formation plausibly connects him to Bruneian court and maritime networks, but it is **novel reconstruction of a specific household**. Evidence about Muslim rulers in **1570** cannot establish identical observance among all south-bank households half a century earlier. The local region could contain practitioners of ancestral ritual, emerging Islamic practices and people whose lived affiliations crossed simple categories.

Imported objects are not automatic religious evidence. Santa Ana burial assemblages reflect funerary practice in an **earlier century** and must not be directly equated with an alleged 1500 Muslim court, or with a reconstructed kingdom of Namayan.

**Scene method:** an Islamic prayer, a voyage blessing, a mother's protective charm, a trade promise and a funerary obligation can create competing obligations. Different characters should have reasons to take them seriously; no voice exists solely to deliver an encyclopedia paragraph.

## VII. The physical sensory palette: safe when tethered to processes

This section lists **proposed scene textures**, not literal period descriptions.

- **Sight:** river-current lines, low-draft outrigger boats, larger trading hulls farther downriver, braided lines, nearby garden/shore work, local earthenware beside imported glazed jars, soot from household cooking.
- **Sound:** splashing paddles, boat crews calling distances, shifting cargo, hawkers and workers bargaining, gongs or drums **where context is evidenced**, blades against metal in the workshop, different languages heard in fragments.
- **Smell:** wood smoke, tidal mud, fish being cleaned or dried, rope and wet timbers, heated metal, cooking grain.
- **Touch:** rough lashes, smooth traded ceramic, hot clay stove, damp cloth, coin/ornament, a rope biting an inexperienced hand.
- **Movement:** a tide-bound vessel waits while shore crews labor; a political message crosses the river; a woman carries a grievance through kin before it reaches the princely hall; a child knows which boat belongs to a regular customer.

Careful with **exoticizing sensory clichés**: the opening should look busy because of actual work, not because random perfume, dragons, foreign banners, bronze bells and dancing priests appear together. The scene's drama determines which details are noticed.

## VIII. What this does to the current manuscript

The first two existing chapters already prioritize lived causality: a child is rescued, fishery stakes are removed, a cargo boat is damaged, the owner and porter demand compensation, and Ache must learn what command costs. **Preserve the plot**, including Sulad's criticism and the mother's political education.

### Chapter 1: The Boy Prince

Existing harbor steward, cargo, fisher, porter, and tide expectations are plausible **fictional mechanisms**. The line about the cargo's mixed jars and cloth is safe without declaring every jar to be imported. During revision, add **at most one or two discriminating details** (e.g., a glaze, wax-marked bundle, specific injury to the net or crew member) if they change the action. Do **not** add a historical exposition paragraph or named Chinese/Japanese residents around 1500 merely because they are documented in 1570.

### Chapter 2: Family and Community

The compensation hearing captures the productive tension between household store ownership and princely claims. The **joint tally clerk proposed by Lakandula's messenger** is a **deliberate novel invention**, not a source-attested sixteenth-century institution. The political motive is credible as fiction: whoever controls counting vessels may eventually claim the right to charge or veto them. If rewritten, give an affected trader or crew member a **distinct interest** rather than repeating Ache's internal argument.

### Chapter 3: The Muslim Way

The scene with a Malay-speaking trader presently dramatizes uneven language competence, a courtly connection and the social role of an older local religious figure. This fits the research. Preserve **Ula's autonomy** and Sulad's family ritual as meaningful, not props contrasting with a superior court religion. Remove the current novel's explicit “Chapter Two” meta-joke, which breaks first-person immersion; this is an editorial correction, not historical reconstruction.

### Chapters 4–10

- **4 Encroachment:** competing records and rights reveal how commercial power can be asserted without occupying every shore.
- **5 Fight or Flight:** flight loses dependents, trade guarantees and obligations, not simply status.
- **6–8 Voyage and bargaining:** each new community has its own local arrangements; Ache's Maynila privilege is irrelevant abroad.
- **9 Storm:** knowledge of boats and supplies matters more than aristocratic birth.
- **10 Brunei:** encounter expatriate Luzon traders and port people who have years of experience with Malacca; there is no “first discovery of the Asian world.”

### Book III bonus 11

Keep the foreign household numbers, **Mehomete** and **Pablo** in the character/scene planning pool. Their choices in the 1570 conflict can complicate the apparent binary of “Maynila versus Spain”: some lived as locals, some sought alternative protection, some suffered captivity. Their beliefs and motives remain historical gaps to be written as fiction only if chosen. The 1570 report that Chinese complained of seized rudders should be told as **contested testimony**, not as a certain indictment or exoneration of Sulayman.

## X. Research tasks for the next pass

1. Compare the English 1570 relation against the Spanish manuscript or a vetted scholarly transcription, especially the **forty married Chinese** phrasing, **twenty Japanese** figure, the **ship rudders**, and **Pablo's alleged artillery role**. Identify author, manuscript date and transmission.
2. Check Scott's **Tagalog-specific chapters 11–12, pp. 189–242**, for contemporaneous customs and how he assigns witnesses to practices. Avoid borrowing Visayan details by default.
3. Distinguish **Santa Ana/Sapà** archaeological locale from Maynila at the river mouth; review *Glorious Port City Manila* (2016) for maps of excavated deposits, pottery typologies and habitation context. Do not invent Namayan's exact borders.
4. Obtain specific pre-1570 information on **Chinese residents** versus visiting seasonal shipping and mixed households; investigate any Ming or private commercial records.
5. Trace **local manuscript, oral and material evidence for boat construction, fishing technology, currencies, writing, weights, cooking and clothing** before naming specifics in Ache's childhood.
6. Decide whether a secondary narrative viewpoint in Book III's bonus sequence (Mehomete, Pablo or a Chinese resident wife) would illuminate history or merely consume space. Treat identifiable historical figures with restraint.
7. Audit Chapter 2's fictional **records and joint clerks** as a possible anachronism: retain if framed as a proposed local innovation, not a common bureaucratic form.
8. Check the season/wind assumptions around Ache's proposed route to Brunei without assuming that every month would offer safe passage.
