## Series function

**New continuing subplot for Book III, Chapters 4–10, with payoff and reversal in bonus Chapters 11–12.** Foreign trade, writing/translation, the status of dependents, and the rights of local communities are woven into Ache's **existing** institution-building plot. They do not replace the main story, the Book III final coalition crisis, or the two historical bonus chapters.

**Working label:** *The Broken Compact* (development title, not necessarily an eventual chapter title).

**Dramatic question:** When Ache makes the harbor safer by replacing private favors with enforceable rules, who has the power to define a promise—and who becomes bound by it?

### A three-party dispute with a fourth constituency

1. **Ache's central institutions:** predictable dues, shipment rights, naval security, records and translated treaties.
2. **Tondo captains and local shore-holders:** custom, patronage, local autonomy and control of fishing/landing rights.
3. **Asian merchants and brokers:** reliable access and safe passage, but wary of seizure, enforced exclusive contracts, double demands and rulers' armed power.
4. **Locally dependent workers and families:** rowers, artificers, retainers, enslaved people, namamahay householders and kin whose labor and status are counted in ledgers but whose consent is not always sought.

There is no automatic moral winner. Traders can exploit workers; rulers can protect merchants while dispossessing families; local autonomy can preserve protections or shelter entrenched servitude. Historical evidence permits these as **fictional themes**; it does **not** document a particular rebellion orchestrated by Ache.

## Two real people: characterization without false biography

### Mehomete: upgraded to major late-Book-III supporting figure

**Historical anchor:** the 1570 relation identifies a Maynila-born provisions trader/interpreter who visited Spanish Cebu, had wife and son converted, and returned with Goiti, mediated by a brother and local kin. **No firsthand account attests his life before that period**. [Primary source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

**Provisional narrative appointment:** the principal **commercial-and-language broker** in the late Book III **core**, originally conceived as an anonymous interpreter in the Harbour Council, is now **Mehomete** *if and only if* the current Chapter 6–10 chronology can be reconciled with a plausible adulthood and his subsequent activity in Spanish Cebu. His presence earlier than **1570**, court patronage, vocabulary, motivations and relationships are **explicit novel inventions**, not historical discovery.

**Where he carries the plot:** in Chapters 6–10 he teaches Ache that an agreement cannot be neutral if different parties describe its terms in different languages. As a merchant he values stable rules; as an intermediary he sees when documents become tools of coercion. He might advise changes that infuriate the old captains and traders alike. To keep him distinct from the existing cast, he replaces a **generic proposed interpreter/broker function**, not Sulad, Sulayman, Lakandula, Ache's wife, Panday Pira or any historically important person.

**Unresolved fictional decision:** why he later appears in Cebu and accepts baptism should **not be determined now** as an inevitable betrayal; preserve multiple possibilities and his familial agency. By 1570 his role as Spanish interpreter creates a powerful **reversal**: Ache may recognize his court's former brokerage techniques operating for a new power. This is fiction built around a documented endpoint.

**POV:** book III remains Ache-dominant; one concise Mehomete first-person passage in Bonus 11 is possible, not mandatory, to show how language and family survival collide.

**Fallback if core chronology remains too early:** create a separate **fictional** older broker in Chapters 6–10, and keep Mehomete major in Bonus 11. Do not pretend that a known Mehomete lived in the 1520s or 1530s.

### Pablo: major supporting figure in the 1570 crisis, not an invented century-spanning companion

**Historical anchor:** the Spanish 1570 account says a Japanese resident told the expedition that he was Christian and named **Pablo**. It attributes service as one of Maynila's gunners to what “people say”; **this is hearsay**. His age, career, trade status, conversion and ultimate fate are unknown. [Primary source](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html).

**Narrative appointment:** **major supporting presence in Bonus Chapter 11**. Pablo bridges Maynila's resident Japanese community and unfamiliar Spaniards, with the tension between a professed shared religion and potentially different political loyalties. If a scene places him near the cannon, present this as **novel hypothesis**, not confirmed employment. He need not be a samurai, Portuguese agent, colonial collaborator or guaranteed Tagalog speaker.

**Possible fictional arc:** first negotiating safety for resident families and property, then choosing whether to remain, negotiate or flee when the settlement burns; a brief scene after the burning and an optional Bonus 12 aftermath can give him a consequential ending. Do not present his ultimate survival or travel as historical unless verified.

**POV:** one short first-person section can reveal what an Asian Christian *resident of Maynila* sees that Ache cannot. The entire chapter should not be transferred away from Ache to two new protagonists.

### No retroactive substitutions

The existing core relationships remain: Ache–Sulad (chosen loyalty), Ache–Sulayman (succession), Ache–Lakandula (dual authority), Ache–wife (Brunei/local negotiation), Panday Pira (technical production). Mehomete **may replace the unnamed broker/translator**, and Pablo **may replace an anonymous Japanese visitor** in bonus material. Neither replaces Sulad or major existing historical characters.

## Required material conflict: status, not simply merchant wealth

A ship has been delayed by a disputed fishery, and a supplier demands redress. A court scribe registers a **namamahay** family's inherited labor obligations in a way that would allow an official to remove a dependent child. An older witness protests; a captain claims the record is now the law. This is **fictional**, informed by Plasencia's (1589) distinction between *namamahay* and *sa gigilid*. In the drama the stakes are home, inheritance, personal freedom and the supply of manpower, not merely a change to a tax rate.

The conflict is a test of whether a ruler can **correct his institutions when they turn an injustice into an efficient system**. No fictional case should be represented on the archive as an attested 16th-century Maynila lawsuit.

## Scene blueprint: an enforceable promise

**POV:** Ache, first person. **Place:** harbor hearing in an intentionally invented Book III court.

**Immediate objective:** adjudicate a detained vessel whose foreign owner claims protection under a trade agreement.

**Opposition:** a local claimant argues that the captain owes an inherited labor payment; a broker cites the treaty; a household head says the children being seized are **namamahay** dependents with a right to remain home.

**Turning point:** the court record renders a conditional oral oath as permanent subjection. Ache initially assumes the difference is merely scribal.

**Cost:** the ruler's “fair” system is now faster at dispossessing people than arbitrary bargaining was. The shipowner may nevertheless want the harsh outcome, revealing traders' divided moral interests.

**Outcome:** Ache orders contested records reopened and appeals heard. Captains accuse him of weakening the new rules; the freed family does not necessarily become loyal to him. A grievance feeds Chapter 9.

**Payoff:** in 1570, the Spaniards report a treaty of friendship. Mehomete can witness another encounter in which words of protection, submission and allegiance carry unequal expectations. There is no invented exact historical dialogue.

## Publication / canon discipline

- The **subplot is novel fiction**, not a discovered historical movement or specific real lawsuit.
- **Mehomete appears in historical evidence for 1570, not earlier.** Any appearances in core Chapters 6–10 are a speculative backstory, subject to matching the novel's chronology; the actual year range still needs to be set.
- **Pablo's cannon role is hearsay**, and the fate of either man after the 1570 account is unknown.
- Strong historical themes should alter **choices and stakes**, not crowd each chapter with historical lecture material.
- Preserve Book III's core ten-chapter completion and the Spanish / Ache-death bonus chapters.
