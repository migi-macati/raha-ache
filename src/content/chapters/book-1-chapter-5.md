The merchant had brought his baskets down before the sun reached the roofs.

I knew them by the dark reed tied across the handles. Yesterday he had said he would take them to Tondo's landing. I had spent most of the night telling myself that he might change his mind.

He had not.

His porters carried the baskets past our tally boards and down to a small boat. There was no Tondo crew waiting for him. He would have to find one when he crossed, if he found one at all.

Our steward was going with them.

He had a clean cloth folded beneath one arm and a little bundle of food in his hand. My mother had sent him to learn what the Tondo organizer would ask for regular crews on our shore. The steward looked at the baskets, then at me, as though he expected me to be pleased that the work had begun.

"Will you agree to his price?" I asked.

"I will hear it."

"And if he asks to count every man who takes a paddle?"

"I will hear that too."

"You could say no."

"Then your mother would have sent someone who was not supposed to hear him."

He stepped into the boat. One of the porters pushed it clear with his pole. The merchant sat with the baskets at his feet, keeping his hand on the nearest binding.

The boat crossed toward Tondo, small and ordinary. No banner. No escort. Nothing I could point to and call an invasion.

I hated how easily it went.


A young rower stood farther along the bank, near the place where yesterday's crew had cast off without Sima's cargo. I recognized the organizer's sister's son. He had one hand wrapped around a paddle shaft, though there was no boat beside him.

His uncle came out from behind the store sheds.

"I have asked you twice," the rower said.

"I know."

"You told me she would pay."

"She paid me."

"Then give me my share."

His uncle looked toward the boats. "Not here."

"Where?"

"After the next work."

The young man held the paddle across his body. He did not raise it. For a moment I thought he might. Then he set the blade on the ground, hard enough to make the wet sand jump.

The organizer went past him without another word.

I could have called the man back. Yesterday I had tried to settle a quarrel before I understood it. Today I understood enough to know that Sima's payment and the rower's wages were different things.

I still wanted to call him back.

The young man saw me watching.

"Did your mother put that on her board?" he asked.

"No."

"Then perhaps she will not know it happened."

He lifted the paddle and walked toward the fish sheds. I had no answer that would have kept him there.

* * *

Bulkan was beside the damaged outrigger when I found him. He had one foot against the hull while a carpenter tested a new fastening. The carpenter pressed, listened to the wood, and pressed again.

"That boat will not be ready today," Bulkan said without looking up.

"I did not come for that boat."

"Good. I would have had to disappoint you."

"I want men at the landing."

"There are men at the landing."

"Men who answer to us."

Bulkan stood. He had been my teacher long enough to know when I was trying to turn a question into an order.


"How many?" he asked.

"Enough that Tondo's crews stop acting as though this is their shore."

He glanced toward the water. "How many is enough?"

"Two boats. A dozen men."

"Doing what?"

"Watching."

"Watching whom?"

"The men who come to take our work."

Bulkan ran his thumb along the edge of the fastening. The carpenter waited for him to move.

"Yesterday the rowers came because a household hired them," he said. "Would you have your men stop the next crew from coming?"

"Not if they are paid fairly."

"Who will decide that at the water?"

"I will."

"And when you are not there?"

I looked at the hull. A narrow strip of new wood had been fitted along the side, pale against the old planking. The carpenter had done good work. He was waiting to finish it.

"I want them to know we are not helpless," I said.

Bulkan moved his foot so the carpenter could work.

"I can put a boat near the landing," he said. "A man can tell us what he sees. If someone tries to take a vessel by force, that is another matter. But if your men stand there all day while the crews work, they will want food. They will want to know who sent them. And the first time a Tondo rower laughs at them, you will have to decide whether the laughter is worth a fight."

"I would decide."

"I believe you."

He said it so readily that I looked at him.

"I have seen you decide quickly," he continued. "I have also seen the boat afterward."

The carpenter struck the fastening into place. I felt the blow through the ground.

Bulkan had once made me repeat a turn until my hands blistered. I had cursed him for it and then, the next day, found myself making the turn correctly without thinking. I wanted him to be wrong now. I wanted him to be an old man who had forgotten what courage was for.

"Would you stand with me?" I asked.

"If your mother sends me."

"And if she will not?"

He looked at me for a long time.

"I would still come if someone were drowning."

That was not what I had asked.

* * *

The boat was drawn above the tide line. A length of net hung between two posts beside it, and Sulad's uncle sat beneath the shade with a heap of torn mesh in his lap.

Sulad was separating sound cord from frayed cord. Each piece he saved went into a little coil by his knee.

"I need the boat," I said.

"Everyone needs a boat," his uncle answered.

"For three days. Perhaps less."

"Then I need three days of fish."

"I can pay."

"With what?"

I told him about the merchant crossing to Tondo, about the steward learning the organizer's terms, about crews who might soon find it easier to work for the other shore. Sulad's uncle kept working while I spoke.

"You want us to sit at the landing," he said at last.

"To offer the merchants another choice."

"And while we wait, who brings in our catch?"

I had come prepared to argue about Tondo. I had not prepared an answer about fish.

"The stakes are damaged," I said. "You may lose tonight's catch anyway."

"Then we need the hours before the water turns, not a prince telling us how many days we can spare."

Sulad looked up sharply. His uncle was not looking at him.

"I could bring food," I said.

"From your own house?"

"From the household stores."

"Has your mother said yes?"

"Not yet."

His uncle picked up a section of net whose knots had come loose in a row. He spread it across his knees and waited for Sulad to pass him more cord.

"When she says yes, you can ask me again."

"Just one boat," I said.

"One boat is the one we have."

I looked at Sulad. He did not offer to persuade his uncle.

"You know what is happening at the landing," I said.

"I know what is happening here," he replied.

He held up a length of cord that had been cut too short to join anything. It fell from his fingers into the waste pile.

"I thought you would want to help."

"With what?"

"With Maynila."

"Am I somewhere else?"

I had meant to say something brave. Instead I stood watching his uncle work through the torn mesh, knot by knot, until the silence became worse than any answer.

"Can I do anything?" I asked.

Sulad moved the sound cord away from me.

"You can hold this edge where I can reach it."

I took the net between my hands. Wet fiber rubbed against the tender place on my thumb. He threaded the cord through the opening, pulled it snug, and moved to the next. We worked without speaking for a while.

Once, when I loosened my grip, the mesh sagged.

"Hold it there," he said, bringing my hand back to the place he wanted it.

His fingers were cold from the water. I felt them for only a moment. The small cord he wore for protection was still hidden beneath his cloth.

I kept the net steady.

At last his uncle examined the repaired stretch and laid it with the pieces ready to be hung.

"I could carry a message," he said. "One crossing, if the water is fair. After the net is finished, and if someone pays for the work."

"Only one?"

"One is what I am offering."

"I need more than a message."

"Then you must ask someone who has more to give."

I thanked him. The boat had not moved, and I had gained no crew. But I knew exactly what his offer would cost, which was more than I had known when I arrived.

On the path back I heard Sulad call after me.

"Do not tell your mother I agreed to leave."

"I won't."

"Or that my uncle did."

"I won't."

He waited until I looked at him.

"Ask us when you know what you can offer."

I went home with the smell of wet fiber on my hands and a promise I had made before I knew whether I could keep it.

* * *

The steward had not returned. His place at the tally board was empty, and the day's marks ended halfway down the wood.

My mother was in the storehouse, watching two women turn out a basket of grain. One held it steady while the other searched the bottom with her fingers. They were looking for damp. When the basket was lifted again, a little pale dust fell through the weave.

"Leave that one open," my mother said. "It will spoil faster if we pretend it is dry."

I waited until the women had carried the basket aside.

"I need food for a boat."

"Which boat?"

"One from the fishing bank."

"Whose?"

"Not mine."

She looked at me then. "I had understood that much."

"Sulad's uncle will carry a message across. He wants food for the work."

"To Tondo?"

"Perhaps farther, if we find someone who knows the way."

"Someone you have not found."

"Not yet."

She brushed grain dust from her palm. "How much did you promise him?"

"I said I would ask."

"That is not what I asked."

I looked at the open basket. "I told him I could bring food from our stores."

"And he agreed?"

"He asked whether you had given it to me."

She waited.

"I said no."

"Then he knows the answer before you do."

I felt heat rising in my face. "He offered a crossing. That is more than anyone else has offered me today."

"Your teacher offered to watch the landing."

"One boat. Watching."

"That is what you asked him to do."

"I asked him for men who would stand there."

"To stop the merchant who crossed this morning?"

"No."

"To stop the men he might hire?"

"If they mean to take our work."

She set her hand on the rim of the basket. "A man taking wages for carrying another man's goods is not stealing your shore."

"I know the difference."

"Then tell me whom your men would stop."

I could see Bulkan's carpenter waiting beside the damaged hull. I could hear the young rower's paddle strike the sand.

"The organizer keeps what he owes his own sister's son," I said. "We know it, and the merchant still crosses to hire his people."

"That debt is not yours to erase."

"Nor his to hide."

"No." She let the word stand. "The steward will ask about wages as well as boats. I sent him because the terms matter. He has not come back. Neither of us knows what Tondo will demand."

"And if their price is too high?"

"Then we decide what we can pay."

"And if every merchant goes to them while we decide?"

"Then I will have been too slow."

She said it without looking away. I had wanted her to deny the danger. Her admitting it made the room feel smaller.

"I can go south," I said.

"With Sulad's uncle's fishing boat?"

"With a captain who has been there. I met him upriver."

"When?"

I told her. I told her he spoke Malay and had seen the Brunei court, that he knew men who traded there. I did not tell her he had promised to take me. He had promised nothing.

"Where is he now?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Then you cannot go with him."

"I can find him."

"How?"

That was the question I had been keeping for last. A messenger could ask along the river. A merchant might know which vessels were moving south. Every person who helped would know what I intended, and each could choose whom to tell.

"I have to try," I said.

She picked up a handful of grain and let it run back into the basket.

"You speak as though trying costs only you."

"It is my mother's kin I would ask."

"My kin," she said. "And your grandfather's household is not a place where you arrive empty-handed and make demands because you know the names of the dead."

I looked at her hands. There was grain in the lines of her fingers.

"I thought you would want me to go."

"I want you alive. I want this landing to belong to the people who use it. I want the men who carry our goods to be paid. None of those wants makes the others disappear."

She called one of the women back and told her to set aside enough dry grain for a single paid crossing, should the steward's terms make one necessary. Nothing was to be taken until she gave the word.

"That is all?" I asked.

"That is what I can authorize without promising another household's supper."

"And the boat south?"

"Find the captain. Learn what he asks. Then come to me with something more than a wish."

I wanted to say I would leave whether she allowed it or not. Instead I watched the woman cover the grain. She tied the basket shut carefully, as if it were already going somewhere.

My mother turned back to the tally board.

"You may go," she said.

I had come to ask for food. I left knowing the first thing I would need to find was someone willing to carry my words.

* * *

Ula was sorting shells on a mat when I found her. The small ones lay in one pile, the broken ones in another. She did not look up until I had stepped around the mat twice.

"If you are going to make the light move," she said, "sit down."

I sat.

"Have you come to ask what your mother thinks?"

"I know what she thinks."

"Then why do you look as though you have lost an argument?"

"I have."

"That is a useful beginning."

I told her about the captain we had met upriver. She remembered him. She remembered, too, that he had laughed when I spoke Malay too quickly and answered me faster than I could understand.

"I need to find him," I said.

"Do you know his name?"

I hesitated. I had been so intent on what he knew of Brunei that I had never asked for the name by which his own men called him.

Ula stopped sorting.

"His ship came from the west," I said. "The headman knew him."

"Many ships come from the west. More go away."

"I could send someone to the headman."

"You could."

"Would you?"

She picked up a shell, found a crack along its edge, and set it with the broken ones.

"What would the message say?"

"That Ache of Maynila wishes to speak with the captain who came upriver."

"And what does Ache of Maynila offer him for his trouble?"

"I can pay."

"With what?"

I thought of the grain my mother had ordered tied shut. It had been set aside for one paid crossing, not for every favor I might need on the way to Brunei.

"Not from the stores," I said.

"Good. You have learned something since morning."

"I have silver."

"You told Sulad that last time."

"I have some left."

"Then count it before you promise it."

I pulled the little pieces from my cloth and laid them beside her shells. She did not touch them. She asked how much of it I was prepared to lose if the captain could not be found.

"All of it," I said.

"Then you have not counted carefully enough."

I gathered the pieces again, leaving one on the mat.

"For a message," I said. "Not a passage."

Ula nodded. "That is a price I can ask someone to consider."

"Who?"

"A woman whose brother carries goods upriver. She may be here tomorrow. Or she may have left this morning. I will not promise what she has not agreed to."

I leaned forward. "If she knows where the captain is—"

"Then she will tell you what she knows. She will not know what he will do next."

Outside, a boatman called to someone on the bank. I knew the voice from our landing, though I could not see his face.

"Do not say I am looking for Brunei," I told her.

Ula raised her eyes.

"Why?"

"Because Tondo would hear."

"Tondo hears things because people speak. So does Maynila."

"If the wrong man knows—"

"Then perhaps he will tell your mother before you do."

"She knows."

"That you wish to go. Does she know you are already spending silver to find a captain?"

I had not thought of the difference.

Ula pushed the unbroken shells into a neat line. "I will ask whether the woman can carry your message. I will not tell her it is a summons from a ruler. It is a request from a young man who met a captain and failed to learn his name."

"She will laugh."

"Perhaps. A laugh is cheaper than a lie."

I stood, then stopped. "Will you tell my mother?"

"She sent you to me once because she thought I would teach you to listen. You have come back asking me to keep a secret. That is not the same thing."

"Will you?"

"I have not decided."

It was the worst answer she could have given me, because it was the one she had a right to give.

At the door I heard her begin sorting again.

"Ache," she called.

I turned.

"If the captain agrees to speak, ask where he is going before you ask him to take you there."

I went back toward the river with less silver than I had brought and no nearer to a ship. But someone might now carry my question upriver. For the first time, that seemed like an action rather than a wish.


* * *

By evening the fishing bank smelled of wet fiber and smoke. Nets hung from a frame to dry. A small fire burned beyond them, where Sulad's uncle was turning fish on a split stick.

Sulad saw me before his uncle did.

"You came back," he said.

"I said I would."

"You said you would bring food."

"I asked my mother."

"And?"

"She may pay for one crossing. If there is a crossing worth paying for."

His uncle turned the fish. "Then there is no work yet."

"No."

"Good. I can eat before anyone asks me to row."

He said it without anger. I wished he had sounded angry. It would have given me something to answer.

Sulad walked with me beyond the drying nets. The river had begun to darken, and lights were appearing on the opposite bank.

"I may leave Maynila," I said.

"May?"

"I am trying to find the captain we met upriver."

"The one who made you speak Malay like a child?"

"That one."

Sulad smiled, then stopped. "For Brunei?"

"To ask my grandfather for help."

"Help with Tondo?"

"Help enough that Tondo will have to listen."

"Did your mother send you?"

"She told me to find the captain first."

"That is not the same answer."

"No."

We stood where the tide had left a strip of damp sand. Behind us his uncle called for another piece of wood for the fire. Sulad did not move.

"Will you ask me to come?" he said.

I had meant to ask. I had practiced the words on the walk from Ula's house, words about trust and the dangers of traveling with strangers. I had not practiced what I would say if he asked first.

"Would you?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you want me there or want someone to do what you say."

I felt the sting of it. "I want you."

He looked away toward the boats.

"I mean I want you with me," I said.

"I know what you mean."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Then he picked up a pebble and rolled it between his fingers.

"My uncle needs the boat. The net is not finished. If I leave, someone else must do my share. You cannot promise him grain and call that consent."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I came here this morning and asked for three days of work as though you had nothing else to do. I know now."

He let the pebble fall.

"If there is a passage," he said, "I will speak to him myself. And I will decide whether to go."

"I would not stop you."

"You could make it difficult."

That was true. I had been taught to give orders so often that I sometimes heard a refusal as an insult before I heard it as an answer.

"I will not ask him in your name," I said.

"Good."

"Will you tell me what you decide?"

"When there is something to decide."

We walked back to the fire. His uncle had eaten one fish and left the other where it would stay warm.

"I have silver," I told him.

"So I have heard."

"Not much. Enough to pay for a message, perhaps a little more. If you carry one, I will pay you from my own things."

"After I hear where it must go."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will find someone else."

He took the fish from the stick and offered it to Sulad, who broke off a piece and handed the rest to me.

I had not expected to be fed by the household whose labor I had spent the day trying to borrow.

On the way home, I passed the landing. The steward had returned. He stood beside my mother with a board in his hands, his face pinched from a day on the water.

I stopped where they could not yet see me.

"The organizer will name the crews," the steward was saying. "He wants the merchants to come to him before they ask anyone else. He says he can find men quickly."

"And their pay?" my mother asked.

"He says that can be settled for each journey."

"The first crew has not been paid," she said.

"He says the woman paid him."

"That was not my question."

The steward lowered the board.

I waited for my mother to order him back across the river, or to accept the bargain because we had no better one. She did neither.

"Write down exactly what he asked," she said. "Tomorrow we will ask the merchants what they would pay for a crew of their own choosing."

I could have stepped forward and told her I had found another answer. I had not. Ula had offered to ask a woman whose brother sometimes carried goods upriver. The piece of silver I had left beside her shells might still be there tomorrow. No one had agreed to carry my words.

I went inside and counted what remained. I counted it again, this time setting aside what I would need if a captain could be found. It was less than I had imagined when I first spoke of Brunei.

Outside, someone dragged a boat farther up the bank. The wood scraped against the stones and stopped. I thought of Sulad's uncle turning his fish over the fire, and of the young rower lifting his paddle without his wages.

I would seek a passage to Brunei. Tonight there was no boat to take me.
