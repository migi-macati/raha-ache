The porter waited for me where the path turned away from the hall. He had one foot on the higher ground and the other in the mud, as though he could still decide to go home.

"Is the man who sent you far?" I asked.

"His boat is not."

"That was not what I asked."

"He will want to know why you came without the wood."

I looked back toward the landing. The new channel stake stood out from the bank. Beyond it a narrow boat was coming upriver with its bow low in the water. Its steerswoman called once, and the men at the forward end brought their poles inside the hull. They were making for the lower steps.

"The wood for the post has not been chosen," I said.

"Then tell him. He thinks you chose it when you moved the other one."

I had not moved the other one. I had helped decide where it should stand. Already those were becoming the same thing in other people's mouths.

The porter led me along the upper path. The tide had risen over the low ground where Dala sometimes set her frame. Pawi stood ankle-deep beside her, holding the repaired net against the current. The new cord pulled tight, then loosened. Dala lifted one corner to keep it from catching on a root.

"Did it hold?" I called.

"It held once," she said. "Come back after it has held twice."

She had no time for the work I was going to ask of her.

At the upper landing, two vessels occupied the space that could safely hold one. The smaller was tied to the old post. Its crew had brought jars of oil and three rolls of woven cloth from the other bank, and the women who had received the goods were carrying them up the steps. The larger boat waited outside it with its bow angled toward the current. It carried covered baskets and bundles of cut wood, lashed so close together that the men could hardly find a place to set their feet.

The larger boat's owner stood on the bank with his cloth drawn up above his knees. He was the man who had objected to our new marker yesterday. I recognized the fold in his shoulder cloth before I saw his face.

"You came," he said.

"The porter said you wanted to speak."

"I wanted the lower post ready."

"It cannot be ready until the bank is braced."

He pointed to the new sounding stake.

"You drove that yesterday."

"To show the deeper water."

"Wood stands in the water. A boat needs wood to hold to. I am asking what difference you intend me to see."

A woman on the larger boat answered before I could. She had a broad paddle under one arm and a length of line wound around the other. The line had left a red mark where it crossed her skin.

"That one is in the channel," she said. "Tie us to it and the first pull will turn the bow across the way."

"Then use two lines," the owner said.

"To what?"

He looked at the bank. The nearest tree leaned over the mud, its roots partly exposed by the water. Farther up stood the post holding the smaller vessel. There was no other sound place within the reach of her line.

"Take the tree," he said.

She shook her head.

"The roots are already coming out."

The owner looked at me as if I had put the roots there.

"Your keeper said there would be a post."

"She said we needed one."

"Do you mean to keep explaining the need until my cargo spoils?"

The covered baskets were dry. I could not tell what lay inside them. One had a wet mark spreading across its lower weave. The owner saw me looking.

"Salted fish," he said. "If the water reaches it, you can eat it yourself."

The woman with the paddle leaned over the side and pressed a pole into the mud. She lifted it, moved it a little farther out and tried again.

"We can wait here while the tide is high," she said. "When it turns, the stern will swing toward the bank. I will not hold her against the current all afternoon."

"Then unload," the owner said.

"Where?"

The smaller boat's crew had left only a narrow place on the steps. A woman carrying an oil jar was coming up it with both hands under the vessel's round body. She stopped when she saw us and waited for someone to move. Nobody did.

I stepped aside. She passed, breathing through her mouth. The jar was heavy enough that her arms trembled.

"We could shift the smaller boat," I said.

"Ask its owner," said the woman with the paddle.

I looked for him. He was on the bank arguing with a buyer about a jar that had lost its stopper. The buyer had spread a cloth on the ground and was showing him the dark patch left by the oil.

The porter stood beside me. I had forgotten him.

"Will you pay me now?" he asked.

"Your owner sent you."

"He told me you would pay when you came."

The owner of the larger boat heard him.

"I told him to find the man who promised a post."

"I promised to ask about one," I said.

"Then you have asked. Pay him."

The porter did not look pleased that the owner had spoken for him. He turned to me.

"I walked here and back to your hall. You could have stayed there if you had wanted. I would still have walked."

He was right about that. I had no food with me and no cloth that he could use. The only thing I could offer was another journey to the hall, where Amaya would ask whose stores I intended to open.

"Come with me when we finish," I said.

"I am not following you for the rest of the day."

He went to the shade of the tree and sat with his knees drawn up. I watched him settle himself. He had decided to wait, but he would not work while he waited.

The woman with the paddle called from the boat.

"If you are finished deciding who sent whom, the stern is moving."

The boat had shifted. Its bow still pointed toward the steps, but the current was pressing its outer side. One of the men held a pole against the bottom, his shoulder bent so far forward that his head nearly touched his knees. Another had put a line around the exposed tree root despite the woman's warning.

"Take that off," she told him.

"The owner said—"

"Take it off."

He obeyed. The root lifted a little when the line came free. Mud fell from beneath it into the water.

I climbed down to the steps and called to the smaller boat's owner. He came slowly, wiping his hands on a piece of cloth already dark with oil.

"Can you move your boat upstream until this one unloads?"

"With my jars on the steps?"

"Only the ones that are left aboard."

"And where will the people carrying them stand?"

I looked at the bank. There was a narrow shelf of firm ground beyond the hall path. It was clear except for a rack on which Dala had laid a broken piece of netting to dry.

"We could use that shelf," I said.

The smaller boat's owner followed my gaze.

"Whose rack?"

"Dala's."

"Ask her."

I knew he was right. I also knew the tide would not wait for me to walk down to her fishing place and back. I was thinking of telling him to move the rack and asking her afterward.

Then I saw Pawi coming up the path carrying the wet end of the repaired frame. Dala followed, supporting the other side. They had finished their second test. She stopped when she saw us all looking at her shelf.

"What?" she asked.

"Could we move your rack for one tide?" I said.

"To put whose goods there?"

"The oil jars. This boat needs the upper post."

She set the frame down carefully.

"I have fish drying on the rack."

"Could it be moved?"

"Yes. By me. After I have somewhere else to put it."

She looked at the loaded vessel. The man holding the pole had begun to shake. His companion went to help him.

"That boat should not be here waiting for me to clear my fish," she said. "Who told it the lower steps were ready?"

The owner opened his mouth. The woman with the paddle answered him.

"No one. I told him to wait at the bend until the small boat finished. He said the new stake meant the work was done."

The owner turned toward her.

"I said the channel was marked."

"You said we could unload."

I had not been there when he said it. I could see from his face that he would have preferred I had.

Dala knelt beside her frame and checked the knots along its edge. Pawi stood close enough to hear every word.

"I will carry the dry fish to my sister's place," she said. "It will take me until the water reaches that mark." She pointed to a stain on the step. "If the jars are still there when I return, they are not going onto my rack."

"Will your sister take the fish?" I asked.

"I will ask her."

It was a small permission, and it was the only one I had.

The smaller boat's owner called to his crew to finish carrying the jars. Two men climbed aboard to lift the last of them. The woman who had been carrying oil set down her load and told them not to rush the stopper loose again. The buyer held out the stained cloth as evidence of what rushing had already cost.

The larger boat's steerswoman watched the space open.

"Now," she said. "Move the smaller boat ahead by its own line. Leave the post for us."

The smaller boat's owner looked at the loaded vessel.

"How long?"

"Until we have the baskets off. The wood can wait aboard."

"The wood will make you heavy."

"Then I will take the fish first."

She did not ask the owner of her cargo to decide the order. He had hired her to bring it to the landing. She knew what the boat could bear.

The smaller boat edged away from the post. Its men took the line forward along the bank and held it from above the steps. The larger vessel moved in. For a moment its bow was close enough to the smaller boat that I thought the two would strike. The steerswoman put her paddle into the water and turned the stern just clear. One of her men dropped a loop over the upper post.

"Do not pull yet," she told him.

He waited until she had brought the bow around. Then he tightened the line. The boat touched the steps with a soft blow that sounded worse than it was.

The owner breathed out.

"See?" he said. "There was room."

"There was room because another boat moved," the steerswoman said. "It will have to move again when it wants its post back."

The first basket came ashore. Its underside was damp but the fish inside had been wrapped well. The woman carrying it set it on a mat and opened the cover. The owner counted the bundles. The steerswoman counted the baskets still in the hull. They were not counting the same thing, and for once neither tried to make the other stop.

The porter rose from beneath the tree.

"I have waited," he said.

The owner turned to him.

"Then carry one of those baskets."

"I was sent for the post," the porter answered. "You have men to carry fish."

The owner looked offended. I thought he would insist. Instead he told the men from the boat to keep unloading. He had already lost too much of the tide to argue with another man who knew exactly what he had agreed to do.

I went with the porter to the hall. Amaya was spreading the morning's washed cloth across a rope. The keeper sat beneath the eaves with the strips from yesterday's sounding. The old marker lay beside her, dry now and pale where it had been under water.

"The upper post is holding a loaded boat," I told them.

"At what tide?" the keeper asked.

"This one."

"And when it falls?"

"The steerswoman will move before then."

"She had better."

I asked whether the landing's share could pay the porter for bringing me to the owner.

"What did he carry for the landing?" the keeper asked.

"He carried the owner's message."

"Then ask the owner to pay him."

"He sent him to me."

"And you followed him. That does not make the message ours."

The porter stood beside me. He was no longer asking for his pay. He was listening to hear whether I would ask someone else to bear it.

Amaya took down one of the cloths before it had dried. She folded it and held it out to me.

"Mine," she said. "From the household work, not the landing's count. If he accepts it, you owe me the cloth."

The porter took it and rubbed the weave between his fingers. He asked whether it had been used. Amaya said it had, and showed him the worn place along one edge. He considered it for a moment, then nodded.

"It will do."

He folded it small and put it inside his own cloth. He left without thanking me. I had done nothing for which he needed to thank me.

The keeper spread the sounding strips across her knees.

"Who will choose the wood for the lower post?" she asked.

"I will ask the man who drove the stake to inspect the bank."

"Who will release him from his other work?"

"I do not know."

"Then ask that before you tell the owners when the post will stand."

Amaya hung the remaining cloths. She had not asked why the porter needed payment. She had seen that he did.

I went back down to the landing before the tide turned. The larger boat was nearly empty of fish. Its bundles of wood remained tied in the hull. The steerswoman had kept them there, although the owner had wanted them unloaded as well. The smaller vessel still waited above the steps, its crew watching the water.

The owner met me on the path.

"How many tides?" he asked.

"For the lower post?"

"For a place where I can bring a boat without making two crews move out of each other's way."

"I will bring the keeper and the man who knows the bank to look at it after the next low water. Then we can ask what wood and help it needs."

"Ask whom?"

"The people who have it."

He looked at the timber still aboard his vessel.

"Some of them have already given wood for your passage."

"I know."

"Then you will hear the same answer you heard yesterday."

He was not threatening me now. He was telling me how the next meeting would go.

"I will come when the water is low," I said. "You can come too."

"I will. If there is still no place for my boat by the third morning, I will take the next load across the river."

I wanted to tell him that the landing would lose more than his load if he did. I wanted him to understand that the post was meant for everyone. Instead I looked at the upper berth, where his steerswoman was already telling the men to loosen the line.

"Tell me before you take it," I said.

He gave a short laugh.

"You will see me go."

He walked toward his boat. The woman with the paddle had turned it outward, away from the new sounding stake. She waited until the smaller vessel was clear before she took her line from the post. The cargo was ashore. The berth was empty again, but only until the next boat came.

I stood where the lower post should have been. The mud gave under my heel. A narrow channel of water opened around it and closed when I moved my foot.

The keeper had said we needed bracing. I had repeated the word to the owner as though I knew what it required. When the man who had driven the stake came to look, I would have to ask him where the wood could stand. Then I would have to ask who owned it.

* * *

At low water the mud was firm enough to hold the footprints of the people who had come to look at it. That did not mean it would hold a post.

The keeper had brought the sounding strips from the hall. The man who had driven yesterday's stake came behind her carrying a narrow pole. He set it against the bank and pressed until the lower end disappeared. He pulled it free, moved three steps along the edge, and tried again.

"Where is the wood?" he asked.

"We have to find out whose it is," I said.

"Then I have come to look at mud."

He pushed the pole in a third time. I could see the change in his shoulders when it struck something firmer. He marked the depth with his thumb and held it up for the keeper. She measured it against the cuts on one of her strips.

"Could a post stand there?" I asked.

"A post can stand anywhere until a boat pulls it over. Who will brace it? And from which side?"

I pointed toward the higher bank.

"That path belongs to people," the keeper said. "You cannot drive a brace through it without asking them."

The man with the pole looked at me. He had been taken away from another task to come here. He had not agreed to build anything.

"Can you come back after the next tide?" I asked.

"Ask the owner whose work I left. I am due at his boat before the sun is high."

He gave the pole back to the keeper and went toward the steps. I watched him pass the new sounding stake without touching it. He knew what it was for.

Above the hall, timber lay beneath a roof of leaves. I had seen the pile from the path and thought of it as wood we had. From closer up, the lengths were different. Two were straight and heavy, with pale cuts at their ends. One was split down the side. The shorter pieces were laid crosswise on stones to keep them clear of the wet ground. A length of cord tied around the largest beam bore a knot I recognized from the hall's rafters.

Amaya was there before me. She had a basket of old thatching at her feet. When she saw where I was looking, she took the cord in her hand.

"The roof," she said.

"I have not asked yet."

"You brought the keeper and a man with a measuring strip. That is how you ask now."

The keeper stopped beside the pile but did not touch it.

"The lower landing needs a post," I said. "The bank is soft. We may need wood to hold it from behind."

"And the hall needs a roof," Amaya said.

I looked up. From the path the roof seemed whole. Beneath the eaves a brown stain ran down one upright. The mat beside it was curled where rain had reached the weave.

"How much of this is for the roof?"

"All that I have marked. Some of the shorter pieces are for the sleeping platform. You know where the water came in last time."

"I thought those had already been put aside."

"They have. That is why they are here."

She pointed to the split length.

"That one may be no good for either task. The others are not waiting for you to find them a purpose."

I wanted to tell her that if boats could not unload safely, there would be less food to keep under the roof. She had been keeping that food dry while I was away. I was certain she had already considered the argument.

"Could we take only one?" I asked.

"Which one?"

I looked at the pile. I had no answer.

"Ask the person who will set the post," she said. "Then ask the people who brought the beam. I will not give up a sound length because you like its shape."

The keeper made a cut on a fresh strip.

"What is that?" Amaya asked.

"Wood requested. Not wood granted."

"Good."

I had come intending to settle a shortage. Now the shortage had names attached to it, and I had not spoken to half of them.

At the foot of the path Dala had spread her net on the dry side of the bank. Pawi was feeding a thin cord through the frame. The new knots were still darker than the old. A woman stood with them holding a basket of fish against her hip.

"My sister," Dala said when I approached. "She took the dry fish yesterday because the oil jars needed the shelf."

Her sister looked at me without shifting the basket.

"Dala said you would bring the rack back before evening," she said.

"I asked whether it could be moved."

"She moved it. That is why I carried the fish."

I could hear the difference. Dala had made the choice, and her sister had borne part of its cost.

"Where do you want the rack?" I asked.

"Where it was," Dala said. "Unless you intend to put a wall there."

"We are looking at a post."

"Then why did your man tell Pawi there would be a fence?"

"Which man?"

"The one carrying the keeper's strips. He said the landing needed to be shut on one side so people would stop setting goods in the mud."

The keeper had stayed near the timber. I looked toward her, then back at Dala. I had mentioned a sheltered place for cargo when the owner complained. Someone had made it into a fence before I knew where the first post would go.

"I have not chosen a fence," I said.

"That will be news to the people already measuring my path."

Pawi held the net frame up. A knot near its edge loosened under its own weight. Dala took it from him and tightened it again.

"Has it held in the water?" I asked.

"Twice when we held it. It has not held a catch. You are asking me to put it where a boat may strike it, and you are asking before I know whether it will work."

"I did not mean to take the place."

"Then say where the post goes without taking it."

I looked from the net to the soft bank. The easiest line for a brace ran across the strip of shore where she had stood all morning. I had seen it at low tide and called it empty ground.

Dala's sister set her basket down.

"I have a place higher up," she said. "Dala can put fish there on the days she needs to. It is farther from the water. I will not carry every basket for her."

"I did not ask you to," Dala said.

"I know. I am telling him."

Pawi had begun to retie the loose knot. He kept his eyes on his hands.

"Will the landing pay for another rack?" I asked.

Dala's sister laughed once.

"With the wood you have not found?"

I could not answer. The keeper came down the path, carrying her strips.

"If we move a rack for landing work," she said, "we can witness what was moved and who will replace it. We cannot promise a new rack before anyone agrees to supply it."

Dala stood.

"I will show you the place when the water is high. You cannot see where the boats turn from here."

She took the net from Pawi and walked toward the steps. Her sister followed with the fish. I had wanted a straight line for a brace. Dala had made me wait to see what the line crossed.

The timber supplier arrived near midday, after the man who had driven the stake had returned to his other work. His name was Madan. He came with a young woman carrying a cord wound around a short piece of wood. She held the cord out to him whenever he asked, but did not let him take it away.

Madan had sent two of the lengths beneath Amaya's shelter. I knew that much because I had seen him help unload them weeks ago. I had not asked why they were still there.

"Amaya said you want the beam for the roof," he told me.

"She said it is pledged."

"It is."

"Could another beam be brought for the roof if this one is used at the landing?"

"From where?"

"From your next load."

"My next load is not yours."

The young woman unwound the cord and showed me three knots spaced along it. Madan told her to hold the ends apart.

"One for the hall," he said. "One for my sister's boat, which needs a new crosspiece. One for a house above the bend. We have not cut the third yet."

"Is the first the one under Amaya's roof?"

"It is the one she chose. We agreed on its length before I brought it. She gave grain for the men who carried it and cloth for the work at the bank. Do you want me to take it back and return those things?"

"No."

"Then do not tell her that I have given it to you."

I looked at the young woman. She was keeping the knots apart as if the order mattered.

"Who holds the second claim?" I asked.

"My sister," Madan said. "She is away with the boat. This is her daughter. Ask her whether her mother has said the crosspiece can wait."

The young woman looked at him before she answered.

"She said it must be ready before the next long trip. She did not say a man from the hall could have it first."

I had expected a supplier to bargain with me. The wood had been bargained for before I arrived.

"Could you cut another length?" I asked Madan.

"I could. I have men who know where to cut and how to bring it down. They have work already. What will they eat while they do yours?"

"The landing has a share from the boats that unload."

"Does the keeper say it can be spent on this?"

The keeper, who had followed us up the path, answered before I could.

"I have counted what came in. I have not promised it."

Madan nodded.

"Then you have not bought anything."

I could feel the cargo owner's third morning approaching. I wanted to make Madan an offer large enough to end the talk. I did not know how much grain the landing could spare, or what work would be left undone if his men came here.

"What would you need to consider it?" I asked.

Madan held out his hand for the cord. His niece did not give it to him.

"Tell him the rest," he said.

She looked directly at me.

"If you take the men for the post, my mother's boat will wait. If the next trip is late, the people who have promised goods to her will ask us why. We will not tell them the river took the wood."

Madan let his hand fall.

"I can show you two short pieces that were left from a repair," he said. "They belong to me, and I have not promised them. I do not know whether they will serve your post. I will bring them to the bank tomorrow if someone who knows the work is there to judge them. I will not have them cut or driven before we agree what they are worth."

"Will you come if I ask?"

"I have just said I will come. Do you hear a promise of a post in that?"

"No."

"Good. And if I say the pieces are no use, or the work is too dear, will you still let my boats use the steps?"

The keeper looked at me. So did Madan's niece. I knew why he had asked in front of witnesses. I had been restored to the hall, and men had begun to speak of my requests as though they were orders. If Madan refused, he wanted the refusal to belong to him without costing his family its trade.

"Yes," I said. "The passage remains open under the agreement. The upper berth is used as the keeper directs while the lower one is unsafe. Your answer about wood will not change that."

"Tell the owner with the salted fish," Madan said.

"He is here?"

"He is at the steps telling people he will go across the river tomorrow if you cannot build for him."

"That is his choice."

"It is. But he has been saying you can take the hall's beams and the service of any man who owes you help. I wanted to hear what you would say before he heard it for me."

I looked at the keeper's strips. The marks on them recorded things that had been seen or agreed. None recorded that I could take Madan's men.

"People have duties to households," I said, and stopped. I could not tell him which duty he owed me. The fact that I had heard of such service did not make him mine to call.

Madan waited.

"I will not call your men away from their work on my word alone," I said. "If someone owes help for the hall or landing, the people concerned must say what that help is. We can hear it then."

He considered me for a moment.

"Then I will bring the two pieces. Not the beam under the roof."

His niece rewound the cord. She kept the knot for her mother's boat visible on the outside.

After they left, the keeper made two more cuts on a strip. I asked her what they meant.

"Two pieces offered for inspection. No wood transferred."

"And the owner's deadline?"

"Still his."

The sun had reached the far side of the hall. Amaya came down from the shelter carrying the cloth she had used to cover the split beam. She saw me looking at it.

"You owe me one cloth," she said.

"I remember."

"Do not put it on the landing's account."

"I will not."

She passed me without asking whether I had found the post. She had heard Madan's offer. It was enough for today, though not for the owner who wanted his fish unloaded tomorrow.

I went down to the bank again. Dala had set the frame in the water and was holding it while Pawi watched the knots. Her sister stood above them, guarding the basket from the rising tide. From where I stood I could see the narrow place a brace might take, and the place the net needed when the water moved inward. The two did not fit together as easily as they had in my mind.

Across the steps, the cargo owner was directing a man to gather the empty baskets from yesterday. He saw me and called out.

"Did you find my wood?"

"I found wood someone may bring for the maker to inspect tomorrow."

"That is not what I asked."

"No."

"Will the post be ready by the third morning?"

"I cannot promise that."

He looked toward the upper berth, where another vessel had already tied up. For a moment I thought he would call me a child and go. Instead he pointed to the two boats.

"Then the next load goes across. You can tell the people who buy it why."

He returned to his baskets. I could have followed him and promised the hall's beams, or asked Madan's niece to persuade her mother to wait. Both would have given me an answer I could take back to the keeper. Neither would have given the river a sound place to hold a boat.

I stood at the edge until the rising water covered the prints of the morning's inspection. When Madan came tomorrow, the person who understood the bank would have to be there. I had a supplier's offer, a roof that still needed repair, a fishing place I could not take, and an owner who had already chosen another shore.

* * *

Madan came at the falling tide with the two pieces of wood balanced across the shoulders of men who had not been at the landing yesterday. His niece walked ahead of them. She carried the knotted measuring cord, though neither piece was long enough to reach the knot she kept for her mother's boat.

The cargo owner's next load had already gone to the far bank.

I had watched it pass at dawn, the steerswoman taking the deeper line around our marker without looking toward the steps. The owner had stood beside the baskets while they were carried aboard. He had not come to ask whether I had found the wood.

Madan set the pieces on the dry part of the bank.

"Where is the person who will judge them?"

"Coming," I said.

"Then we wait. I will not have a boy drive one into the mud and call it accepted."

His niece put a stone under the thinner length to keep its cut end out of the water. The men who had carried it stayed where they were. They had another load to collect upriver, Madan told me, and could wait only until the tide began to return.

"Will they help set the post?" I asked.

"They carried my wood here. That is what I asked them to do."

I had made the same mistake twice, and they had heard it both times.

The maker arrived with a child carrying a shallow basket of tools. She was older than I had expected, with one arm marked by a pale line from wrist to elbow. She stopped above the wet ground and looked first at the path, then at the upper post, then at the sounding stake.

"Who told you I make posts?" she asked.

"The keeper said you had repaired the upper steps."

"I repaired two steps. A step takes the weight of a foot. A post takes the pull of a boat. Which work do you want?"

"The second."

"Then show me the boat."

I had none to show her. The last loaded vessel had gone across the river, and the narrow boat at the upper berth was tied to the sound post already standing there.

The maker looked at Madan's pieces.

"Those are for a post?"

"For you to inspect," his niece said. "My uncle has not sold them."

The maker smiled at her.

"Then we understand one another."

She called herself Raya. The child was her sister's son, and she sent him to ask the keeper for the strip recording the depth of the bank. He returned with the keeper herself. She carried the strip and the cord used for the sounding.

Raya held the strip beside the first length of wood. She turned the wood over with her foot, found a split near one end, and asked Madan where it had been stored.

"Under a roof since the last repair," he said.

"With that end resting on the ground?"

"Some of the time."

She ran a thumb along the split. Then she lifted the other piece and laid it across the first.

"These might hold a small step above the mud if there were something sound beneath them. Neither will reach firm ground and leave enough above water to take a loaded boat."

Madan folded his arms.

"I did not say they would."

"No," Raya said. "Someone else hoped."

She looked at me.

"Did you?"

"Yes."

"Then we have saved you the trouble of putting them in and pulling them out."

I wanted her to say what else might serve. Instead she walked down to the lower bank. At the water's edge she pressed her heel into the mud and left it there while she counted a few breaths. When she lifted it, water seeped into the mark. She tried again farther up. The ground there held her weight, but a narrow path ran between the hall and Dala's working place.

"Who uses that path?"

"Households above the steps. Dala brings her catch across it."

"Can I put a brace through it?"

"No one has agreed to that."

"Good. Then don't draw your brace there."

She sent the child to the upper post with a cord. He looped it loosely around the wood and brought the free end down toward us. Raya took the end and pulled until the cord straightened. She did not put her whole weight into it.

"Imagine the boat here," she said. "The water carries it down. Its line pulls that way. If you plant a post in this mud without holding it from higher ground, it will lean. When the boat pulls again, it may come out."

"The sounding stake held," I said.

"Nothing pulled a loaded boat against it."

The keeper said, "That was the point of the sounding."

Raya let the cord fall slack.

"Who has the right to keep the line clear above the bank?"

I looked toward Dala. She had set her frame in the water and was watching Pawi lift one corner to free a knot. Her sister was there too, arranging fish on a mat beyond the reach of the tide.

"Ask her," I said.

"Then I will. I will not set a brace where she will have to cut it away to work."

She walked to Dala before I could call her over. I followed at a distance close enough to hear.

Raya showed Dala where the pull would run if a boat were tied at the lower berth. Dala pointed to the place where she spread her net at low water. Raya moved two steps farther upstream.

"Here?"

"A boat turns there," Dala said. "You would put the brace under its bow."

"Then here?"

"That is where Pawi stands when the current is strong."

Raya looked at the boy. He did not raise his head.

"Can you stand elsewhere?"

"I can," he said. "But I cannot see the lower knot from there."

Dala held the net higher. A small fish had caught near the edge. She freed it with two fingers and dropped it into the basket.

"Come when the water is higher," she told Raya. "The turn looks different. I will show you where the line comes closest."

"Will you show me?" Raya asked. "Or have you promised me the place?"

"Only the first."

"Then we understand one another too."

Raya returned to the timber. Madan's men had begun shifting their feet. The sun was high enough that the ground beneath the wood had dried.

"I can come back at the higher water," Raya told me. "I can tell you where a post might hold. I cannot build it from these two pieces."

"What would you need?"

"A longer sound length for the post. Wood to hold it from the bank. Cord that can take a pull. People who can carry and set the lengths. Someone who knows how the boats turn at both tides."

"The steerswoman knows."

"Is she yours to call?"

"No."

"Then ask her. And I will want the man who knows the ground above the path, because a brace cannot end in another place that gives way."

The keeper made marks on her strip as Raya spoke.

"How long?" I asked.

"How long to find the wood? Or how long to set it after the wood comes? Those are different questions."

"After."

"If the ground is sound where we need it, perhaps two days of work. If it is not, longer. I will not promise a number before I have seen it under water."

"And what do you ask?"

"Food while we work. Help to lift the wood. The use of a boat for one tide to test the line. And something of mine to take home when the work is done."

"Cloth?"

"Cloth might do. Ask me what kind before you bring it. My sister's roof needs a covering before the next long rain."

Amaya's roof needed one too. I could already hear her asking which cloth I meant to promise.

"Will you take the landing's share?"

"Does the keeper have enough to give?"

The keeper answered, "For one inspection, perhaps. Not for two days and everyone who must eat."

Raya nodded.

"Then you are asking people to lend work to a place that has not paid for it. Some may. I have a child to feed and a sister who has lent me his help today."

The child, who had been listening without looking at us, said, "She told me to come home before dark."

Raya glanced at him.

"And I intend to keep that promise."

Madan stepped forward.

"The two pieces?"

"Not for the post," Raya said. "They may serve a step or a crosspiece later. They are yours until you agree otherwise."

He told his men to lift them again. His niece stopped him before they could.

"Let her mark what she saw," she said.

Madan looked at the keeper.

"That I brought them, yes. Not that she took them."

The keeper cut two short marks on a fresh strip and showed it to them both.

Raya had begun gathering her tools when the cargo owner came down the upper path. He had seen Madan's men and assumed the work had started. Behind him walked the steerswoman who had brought his fish yesterday.

"I sent the next load across," he said to me. "I told you I would."

"I saw it."

"And now you have a maker."

"She has inspected the bank."

"Then take the long pole from my boat. It is straight, and she can use it today."

He pointed to the steerswoman.

"Bring it."

She did not move.

"Which pole?" she asked.

"The long one beneath the stern mat."

"That is mine."

"I paid for the passage."

"You paid me to carry your fish. You did not buy my pole."

"It is wood. We will give it back when the post is standing."

"If you drive it into the mud, how will you give it back?"

He turned to Raya.

"Will it serve?"

Raya looked at the steerswoman.

"May I see it?"

"No," the woman said. "I need it to steer the next load."

Raya turned back to the owner.

"Then I have nothing to inspect."

He stared at me, waiting for me to tell the woman to obey. He had been certain enough of his promise to bring her before the maker, but he had never asked her.

"You cannot keep a landing," he said, "if every person who brings you a piece of wood can take it away again."

"She has not brought it," I said.

"I have offered it."

"You offered something she owns."

The steerswoman looked at me for the first time since arriving. I could not tell whether she approved. She still had the next load to carry, and I had not made her work easier.

The owner lowered his voice.

"Would your father have waited for permission to set one post?"

I thought of the broken red wood inside the hall. My father's name had brought me back to the bench, but it could not lengthen Madan's pieces or turn a steering pole into mine.

"I do not know what he would have done with this bank," I said.

"Then perhaps he would have known whom to ask."

He left by the upper path. The steerswoman stayed.

"If there is a test," she told Raya, "I may bring a boat when the tide is right. You will ask me first, and I will decide what it can carry."

Raya said, "That is what I wanted."

The woman looked at me.

"Do not tell him I promised."

"I won't."

She went after the owner, leaving the long pole where it belonged.

Sulad appeared on the steps before the tide had turned. He had been working at another landing and carried a coil of line over one shoulder. I had sent no one for him. He stopped when he saw Raya's marks in the mud.

"You are trying to hold a boat there?" he asked.

"We are trying to find out whether we can."

He watched the current move around the sounding stake.

"The water will turn her stern toward Dala's place when it rises."

"Raya wants someone to show her."

"Then ask the woman who brought the fish. She has already held a loaded hull here."

"I have."

"Good."

I asked whether he could help carry the timber when we found it.

"For how long?"

"Perhaps two days."

"I have agreed to take another boat downriver tomorrow. After that I can give you a tide, if the boat returns when it should."

"Would you take a share of what the landing receives?"

"Only if the keeper says it is there. I will not wait through three tides for a share you have already promised to someone else."

The keeper heard him and said nothing. She had not promised him anything.

"What would you take instead?" I asked.

"Food for the day, and a length of good line when the work is finished. Not Dala's cord. Not the line I am carrying now."

I nodded.

"Then we will ask again when you return."

"Ask me," he said. "Do not send a man to tell me I have been counted."

He continued up the path to the boat that had hired him. Raya watched him go.

"He knows the difference between a line that belongs to him and one that does not," she said.

"So does everyone today."

"Then we have found one thing that will hold."

I nearly smiled. She did not.

The keeper brought her strip to the hall while the tide was still low. She had recorded Madan's two pieces as inspected and refused for the mooring post, Raya's conditions as a proposal, the steerswoman's offer to discuss a test, and Sulad's possible return. None of the marks said the work had begun.

Amaya was repairing the wet edge of a sleeping mat. She listened while the keeper read the account aloud.

"How much food?" she asked.

"Not settled," the keeper said.

"Whose cloth?"

"Not settled."

"Then the hall has not given either."

"No."

Amaya pulled the needle through the mat.

"Good. You still owe me one."

"I know," I said.

Outside, Madan's men carried the two short lengths back along the path. The wood had come to the bank and gone away again without becoming ours. Across the river the cargo owner's baskets were being opened beneath another roof. I could see the movement of people, though not what they were saying.

Raya had promised to return at high water to look at the line of the bank. Dala had promised only to show her where the boats turned. The steerswoman had promised nothing beyond a conversation. I had no post, no timber for one, and no right to spend the hall's food.

I went to the lower steps before the water covered them. Out in the channel, the sounding stake stood where the witnesses had placed it. The current parted around its narrow sides. There was no line tied to it.

* * *

At the higher water the bank looked smaller.

The path beside Dala's nets was still there, but the dry strip on which Raya had stood in the morning had narrowed to the width of a man's foot. Water ran into the prints she had left. When I put my heel near one of them, the mud opened around it and did not close.

Dala was already waiting. She had moved her frame above the place where she usually worked, though the lower edge still lay in the water. Pawi held a corner while her sister untangled a cord that had dried stiff after the last repair.

"You said you would show her," I said.

"I said she should come when the water was higher."

"She is coming."

"Then you can wait with me."

Across the river, the cargo owner's baskets were being loaded again. I could not tell whether they were the same baskets I had watched yesterday. It was easier to count what we had lost than to know how long we could afford to lose it.

Raya came with her sister's son and a woman carrying a narrow board. The board was dark along one edge and worn smooth in the middle. Raya took it from the woman before they reached the steps.

"That belongs to the keeper," she said. "She says it was taken off the upper landing after the last repair. She will let us use it to see where a line might lie. She has not given it away."

"Does it hold weight?" I asked.

"It held feet once. We are not putting a boat on it."

The keeper followed, carrying the board's old tally strip. She had made a fresh mark for its temporary use. She showed me the marks as though I might otherwise mistake the board for something I had won.

"Raya has asked for one inspection meal," she said. "I can provide that from the landing's share. I have not counted the men who might build."

"And the wood?"

"Still belongs to Madan."

He appeared while we were speaking. His niece was beside him, carrying the cord with the knot for her mother's boat. The two short pieces had not come with them.

"Raya sent for us," Madan said. "She said she wanted to see what the water would do before she asked again about wood."

"I do," Raya answered. "I will not buy a piece that cannot serve. And I will not make a promise about the ground while I have seen it only dry."

Madan's niece looked at the keeper's board.

"Whose is that?"

"The landing's," the keeper said. "It comes back when we finish."

"Then why are we here?"

"Because your uncle offered to sell wood, and I may be able to tell him which wood would be worth bringing," Raya said. "You need not stay."

The niece looked toward the river.

"My mother will ask what we saw."

"Then stay and tell her what you saw. Do not tell her we bought anything."

The steerswoman arrived from the upper steps without the cargo owner. She had brought her own boat, low and empty except for a water jar and two paddles. She had left the long steering pole in the stern where she could reach it.

"I have one turn of the tide," she said. "After that I take a load downriver. If you want me to hold the boat here, tell me where. If you want it tied to your stake, I am going home."

Raya pointed past the stake toward the deeper line.

"Bring her alongside the lower bank, but do not put anyone ashore. I want to see how the bow comes in when the water rises."

"That I can do."

Dala lifted her frame clear of the water.

"Before you bring her, look here."

She walked Raya along the path, stopping twice to show where the current struck the mud. At the first place, the water had already eaten beneath a mat of roots. At the second, a narrow stream ran back from the steps and across the ground on which Pawi usually stood.

"When the boat turns, her bow comes closer than you think," Dala said. "Last time it caught the frame. I have put it here for your inspection. I will move it back when you finish."

Raya watched the little stream. She pressed the end of the keeper's board into the mud well above it and lifted it out. Water filled the mark.

"We cannot brace across this path," she said.

"I told you yesterday," Dala answered.

"You told me where you worked. Today you have shown me why the ground will not hold."

Dala did not seem pleased to have been proved right. She had moved her work and lost part of the morning for the proof.

Raya turned to me.

"Have you brought anyone to help?"

"I have not called Sulad. He is away with the boat that hired him."

"Good. We need no one to lift timber today. My nephew can carry the board, and the keeper can mark what she sees. The steerswoman controls her boat. No one else is to put a hand on its line."

Madan folded his arms.

"Then you are not testing a post."

"No. I am testing whether the place you want to put a post will stay where it is when the water and the boat move together. If it will not, a long piece of your wood will not make it stay."

The keeper gave the board to Raya's nephew. He carried it to the higher edge of the bank, where Raya told him to lay it flat. It reached from the firmer path to a patch of mud beside the lower steps. Raya put a stone on one end and made him step back.

"Do not stand on it," she told him. "I want to see what the water does underneath."

The steerswoman brought the boat in slowly. At first the bow seemed to follow the line of the steps. Then the current took the stern. She pushed against it with her pole, held the hull straight for a few breaths, and let the bow move where the water wanted it to go.

The bow came nearer to Dala's working place than I had expected.

"There," Dala said.

"I see it," Raya answered.

The steerswoman corrected the turn and held the boat off the bank.

"Can you do that with a loaded hull?" I called.

"Not for long. A full boat will want to keep moving. That is why I told the owner not to tie it to the sounding stake."

The keeper marked the place on her strip. Raya asked the steerswoman to repeat the approach once, without coming nearer. She did. This time the water was a little higher. A small wave from the bow crossed the low path and reached the end of the board.

The stone moved.

I thought the boy had touched it, but his hands were at his sides. The board settled into the mud at its lower end, then lifted a finger's breadth when the water ran back. Raya took one step toward it.

"Leave it," the steerswoman called. "The water is still coming."

Raya stopped. The next wave ran under the board. The upper end slid sideways, taking the stone with it. The stone dropped into the narrow stream Dala had shown us. Mud broke away beneath the roots, and the stream widened.

Pawi had already lifted the frame. He tried to move it farther up the path, but one corner caught on a root. Dala caught the other corner before the current took it.

"Let go of that end," she told him.

"I can free it."

"Let go."

He did. The frame twisted in her hands. The old cord at the repaired corner snapped. One side fell into the water and struck the edge of the board. The frame opened. Fish that had been caught in its lower pocket spilled through the gap and went with the current.

Dala went after the frame, not the fish. She caught it by the sound side and pulled it against her knee. Her sister took Pawi by the shoulder and brought him back to the higher path.

Raya raised her hand toward the steerswoman.

"Take the boat out."

The woman had already begun to turn. She pushed away from the bank and took the deeper line without asking me.

I went down toward Dala. She held the frame in both hands. The broken corner hung between them.

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Pawi?"

He shook his head. His sister examined his fingers anyway.

I looked at the fish disappearing in the disturbed water.

"How many?"

"Do not count them for me," Dala said.

I closed my mouth.

Raya lifted the keeper's board from the mud. Its edge had split where it struck the frame. She set it on the higher ground and stood beside it without touching Dala's net.

"The board was meant to show us the ground," she said. "I should have put it farther from the stream."

"You put it where I told you the water went," Dala said.

"I put it above where you showed me. I did not expect the mud to leave so quickly."

"Then now you know."

Raya nodded.

The keeper bent to examine the broken board. She made a mark for it on a separate strip.

"That is the landing's loss," she said. "The net is not."

Dala laid the frame on the path and pointed to the cord that had snapped.

"This was the repair we made after the boat grounded. Pawi spent two mornings on it. My sister brought the cord from her own work. I moved the frame so you could look at the water. If I had been fishing in my usual place, it would not have been there when the board came down."

Raya said, "I asked you to move it."

"I know."

"I will mend what I can."

"With whose cord?"

Raya had no answer ready.

I looked toward the hall. The cloths Amaya had been drying were still under its roof. I could have told Dala we would bring one and cut it into strips. I had already promised a cloth I did not own to the porter and owed Amaya for it.

"The landing must answer for this," I said.

The keeper straightened.

"With what? We have one inspection meal counted, and now a broken board. The people who gave the landing its share did not give you the hall's roof cloth."

"I know."

"Then tell Dala what you can actually give."

Dala watched me. I had thought that admitting a debt would be enough to show I had heard her. It was not enough to mend the frame.

"I will go to Amaya and ask whether she can spare cord," I said. "If she cannot, I will ask the households who use this landing what they can contribute. I will not say you have been paid until you have."

"And the fish?"

"I cannot bring them back."

"No," she said. "You cannot."

Pawi picked up the snapped corner. He examined the old knot and showed it to his aunt.

"Some of this can be tied again."

"Not now," Dala said. "Put it down."

He did. His hands were shaking, though he had not been hurt.

Madan's niece had watched from the higher path. She approached the keeper and asked to see the marks.

"Will you write that my uncle's wood was not used?"

"It was not here," the keeper said.

"And that the boat was empty?"

"The woman brought it empty."

"And that Dala moved her frame because Raya asked?"

The keeper looked at Dala.

"Is that what you want witnessed?"

"Yes."

The keeper made another mark. Madan's niece studied it until she was satisfied.

"Then my mother will know why we did not sell the two pieces."

Madan called her back. He had been quiet since the frame broke. I thought he might offer one of the short pieces for its repair, but he did not. The pieces had been his to sell, and they were still his.

Raya asked the steerswoman to come ashore. She brought the boat to the upper berth when it cleared and tied to the sound post. She stepped out carrying her pole.

"Did I come too close?" she asked Dala.

"No," Dala said. "You did what Raya asked. The bank came apart."

The woman looked at the new cut in the mud.

"It will come apart again. The next full boat will pull harder than mine did. Do not ask me to test it with cargo."

"I won't," I said.

She turned to Raya.

"You wanted one tide. You have had it."

"Thank you."

"Do not count that as a promise for another."

She took her jar and returned to her boat. She had lost time on her own work to show us a danger we could not have paid her to discover. I wondered whether the owner across the river would call that a service to him. He had not sent her.

Raya sat on the higher step. Her nephew brought her the basket of tools and waited for her to speak.

"We stop here," she said.

"We have stopped," I answered.

"No. You have stopped this trial. I mean no one is to drive a post into the lower bank or use that board to carry a load until we find firmer ground and a different place for the pull. If someone comes tomorrow with a long piece of wood, send them to me before they cut it."

The keeper said, "I can mark that."

"Mark that I said it," Raya replied. "And mark that I am not taking the work until I have seen the new place."

I wanted the mark to say the post would be ready soon. It would say instead that we had found another place where it could not stand.

Dala lifted the broken frame with her sister. Pawi carried the loose corner behind them. She stopped before leaving.

"Who will come when we mend this?" she asked me.

"I will."

"To watch?"

I thought of how often I had stood beside other people's work and called the standing a contribution.

"To carry what you ask me to carry," I said. "If you want me there."

She considered it.

"Come at low water. We will see what is worth saving."

She left without promising me forgiveness.

The keeper held up the tally strips: one for the board, one for the inspection meal, one for the net and the lost fishing time. She kept the strips apart.

"If the cargo owner asks whether the lower berth is open," she said, "what shall I tell him?"

"That it is closed."

"And if he asks when it will open?"

"That we do not know."

Raya stood and gathered her tools. She had been paid for an inspection, and she had found something we had not wanted to know. I could not ask her to make the result smaller.

Across the river, a loaded boat left the other landing. Its bow turned cleanly into the current. Our sounding stake stood beyond the broken edge of the bank, still showing the deeper channel. Nothing was tied to it.

I had wanted a post that would keep boats from leaving us. By the time the water fell, we had lost a board, a net's repair, a morning of fishing, and another answer I could have given the owners. The one thing I could still decide was that no boat would be made to trust the lower berth tomorrow.

* * *

The owner came to the hall with three empty baskets.

He set them in front of the keeper before anyone had asked him to sit. The baskets had been washed, but one still smelled of the salted fish he had taken across the river.

"One for the wood," he said. "One for the people who set it. One for the people who will watch the passage after it stands."

The keeper did not touch them.

"What have you brought to put in them?"

"A way to fill them."

He looked at me. I had sent for the people whose work or trade depended on the steps, but I had not sent for him. He had come because the lower berth was closed, and because the people who brought cargo to his boats had begun asking why they should use our bank at all.

"The passage is open," he said. "Every boat that uses it takes something from the people who keep it safe. Let each leave a little when it passes. Then you will not have to beg for every piece of wood."

"The landing share is paid by those who unload here," the keeper said.

"Then give the share another name."

"The name does not change who owes it."

He put a hand on the first basket.

"Will you tell me that the boats from the far bank do not use the marker?"

"They do."

"And the people who use it should give nothing?"

"They have agreed to the sounding and the witnesses. They have not agreed to pay for your lower post."

The owner turned to the doorway, where the far-bank representative had just entered. He was the man who had watched the replacement of the channel stake. He had brought no boat today. His feet were dry.

"Then tell him to bring a basket."

The representative looked at the three baskets, then at the keeper's strips hung behind her.

"Which one is for passing without landing?"

"All three," the owner said.

"Then none is mine."

The owner laughed.

"Your people will be glad of the work when their boats strike the bank."

"My people will be glad if the stake stays where the witnesses put it. We already agreed who will warn a boat and who will hear a claim. You are asking me to agree again to something I refused."

He stood near the doorway. He had not come to speak for every boat from Tondo, and he did not pretend to.

Madan entered with his niece. The two men who had carried his wood yesterday were absent. The niece kept her mother's measuring cord looped around her wrist, the knot visible.

The owner saw them and lifted the second basket.

"Here is a man with wood and people to carry it. He has helped the landing before. He can help again."

Madan's niece answered first.

"The men are carrying my mother's boat pieces today."

"They owe service to their house," the owner said.

"Yes. To our house."

Madan sat beside her.

"Tell me which service I owe to your baskets."

The owner looked toward me.

"Ask Ache. His father did not need to bargain for every man who held a piece of timber."

I had heard my father's name used to demand boats, a hearing, and obedience. The man spoke as though the dead could still lift a post.

"I cannot tell Madan what his household owes without hearing the people who hold that claim," I said.

"You have a place in the hall. Use it."

"I am using it to hear him."

The owner pushed the baskets aside. One tipped over. It rolled toward the doorway until Pawi stopped it with his foot.

Dala came in behind him carrying the broken corner of her frame. Her sister held the rest. The cord hung loose where it had snapped, and the edge that had struck the board was cracked.

"That is from the work you stopped," the owner said.

"From the work we tried," Dala answered.

"Then you have seen what happens when you do not finish it."

"We saw what happened when someone put a board on a bank that would not hold."

Raya came in after them. She had been at her sister's house since the tide fell. Her nephew was not with her.

"That was my board to place," she said. "The keeper lent it. I set it where I thought the ground would stay. It moved. I will not ask anyone to set a post there."

The owner pointed at her.

"Then choose a place that will hold."

"I have. But I have not asked the people who use it."

He stared at her.

"You had another place all along?"

"I had another place to look. I did not know it would serve until the water showed me where the bank gave way."

"Then why are we speaking about baskets?"

"Because the other place belongs to other work."

She turned to Dala's sister.

"May I show them the higher steps?"

Dala's sister hesitated. She dried fish and woven mats on the upper path when the tide was high. Her racks were not there today because she had carried the broken frame to the hall.

"You may show them," she said. "You may not move my rack without asking."

Raya nodded.

The owner stood.

"Then we will all go and ask."

"No," the keeper said. "First tell us what you mean to collect. I will not carry these baskets to the steps and have someone say the people standing there agreed to fill them."

The owner looked at her, then at the representative by the door.

"A small payment from every passing boat. A day's help from each house that uses the landing. Wood from those who have it."

"Which houses?" Madan asked.

"The ones who use it."

"How often?"

"Until the work is finished."

"Who decides when that is?"

The owner did not answer.

The keeper took down the strip from the witnessed sounding. She laid it beside the baskets and read the agreement aloud. A boat that unloaded at Maynila owed the established landing share. A boat that passed through owed no new charge. A complaint could be heard by witnesses from both banks, and no one could secretly move the marker while it was disputed.

The representative repeated his household's assent to those terms.

"I can tell the people who sent me that Maynila has kept them," he said. "I cannot tell them they owe work on a bank where they do not land."

The owner folded his arms.

"Then keep your agreement and watch your boats go elsewhere."

"Some already have," I said.

He looked at me sharply. He had expected me to deny it.

"I know what you lose," he said. "I have lost it too."

For the first time that morning, I believed he had come because he wanted the landing to work. The baskets were his answer to a loss he could not keep paying for. I still could not fill them with other people's obligations.

Raya lifted the broken corner of Dala's frame and examined the crack.

"Before we talk about the next piece of wood," she said, "who will mend this?"

"I have offered to carry what Dala asks," I said.

"Carrying will not replace the cord."

Dala took the frame back.

"I want the keeper to keep my claim apart from the post," she said. "If I agree to let Raya look at the higher steps, I do not want someone saying I have been paid for the fish."

The keeper took a new strip.

"Tell me what you lost."

Dala named the cord her sister had supplied, the fish that escaped, and the morning she and Pawi had spent away from the water. Pawi corrected her on the cord: two old lengths could be reused, but the snapped piece could not. She let him speak.

"Do not mark a number of fish," she said. "No one counted them before they went."

The keeper marked the loss without pretending to know its size.

I asked whether the landing's share could buy new cord.

"Not yet," she said. "The meal for the inspection has been counted. The board must be repaired or replaced. If you spend the next share before it arrives, tell the people who use the steps what you have done."

The owner looked at the strip.

"Will you keep a separate account for every fish that swims away?"

"For the people who were asked to move their work," the keeper answered.

He shook his head but said no more.

We went to the upper steps after the sun had moved beyond the hall. Raya walked ahead, carrying no tools. She showed us a narrow shelf of firmer ground above the old post. It was not wide enough for two cargo crews to work side by side. Behind it stood the drying rack belonging to Dala's sister. A worn board lay across the top step, and one edge had begun to split.

"Here," Raya said. "I can replace the bad step and give a small boat a place to set its load above the water. The old post can still hold one boat at a time. I will not build another post below until someone finds sound ground and agrees on the place."

The owner looked down at the water.

"That will not take my larger boat."

"No," Raya said.

"Then it is not the work I asked for."

"No."

He waited for me to overrule her. I did not.

Dala's sister measured the space between the rack and the step with her own feet.

"If the work takes more than one low tide, where will I dry my fish?"

"Can you move the rack for one day?" Raya asked.

"Where?"

Dala pointed to a place farther along the path.

"Only if Pawi and I carry it, and only if the people using the steps keep their baskets out of that place."

Pawi looked at the rack.

"We will need help with the heavy end."

"I can help," I said.

Dala's sister looked at me.

"One low tide. If the work is not done, my rack goes back."

Raya agreed. She turned to Madan.

"Your short pieces might serve beneath the new step. I will inspect them again where the ground is firm. Will you sell them for that work?"

Madan looked at his niece.

"They are mine," he said, "but the men who carried them have other work. We will need someone else to bring them."

The niece said, "My mother has a boat returning at the next low water. It can carry the pieces if there is room after her own load. I will ask her. I cannot promise it for her."

"What would you take for the wood?" the keeper asked.

Madan named a length of woven cloth, the kind his household used to wrap goods on wet journeys. It was more than I had hoped, but he had not offered the wood for free.

The owner of the smaller boat was unloading oil jars at the upper post. He had stopped to listen. His boat was the one that had blocked the berth when the salted fish arrived.

"I have cloth," he said. "If the step is sound, my jars will not have to be carried over that broken edge again. I will give one length for the two pieces, if Raya says they can be used."

"Whose cloth?" Amaya asked.

She had come from the hall after hearing where we had gone.

"Mine," the smaller-boat owner said. "From the load I brought today. I can show the keeper which roll."

"And who feeds the people who do the work?" she asked.

He glanced toward the hall.

"Not from my roof stores," Amaya said.

Raya looked at him.

"I need food for myself and whoever lifts the wood. One low tide's work, if the ground is as I saw it. If it is not, I stop."

The smaller-boat owner considered his jars. He said he could supply a meal for Raya and one helper. Madan said he would not supply the helper. Dala's sister would move her rack but would not leave her fish to build the step.

I asked Raya whether I could lift with her.

"You can carry," she said. "You cannot take the place of someone who knows where to put the weight."

"Then tell me where to carry."

"First find the wood. Then we will see."

The keeper made a separate strip for each offer. Madan's wood was conditional on Raya's inspection and the cloth being shown. The niece would ask her mother about transport; her mother's answer had not yet been given. The smaller-boat owner offered cloth and one meal. Dala's sister allowed her rack to move for one low tide, with her own people present. Raya would decide whether the step was safe to repair and could stop the work.

The salted-fish owner watched the marks being made.

"You have bought a step," he said. "I asked for a landing."

"We have not bought it yet," the keeper answered.

He looked toward his baskets across the river.

"Then I will keep taking my loads there."

"That is your choice," I said.

"And when the people who pay you ask why their cargo waits while you mend a place for smaller boats?"

"I will tell them which people offered wood and food, and which did not."

His face changed. I had wanted the words to be fair. They sounded like a challenge.

"I offered a way for everyone to pay," he said.

"You offered to collect from people who had refused."

"I will not put my name on your strip."

He walked away before the keeper could answer. I watched him go. I had lost the help of a man who could bring more cargo than the rest of us together, and I had no certainty that the smaller work would make him return.

Amaya remained beside me.

"You have promised to carry wood," she said.

"Yes."

"And you still owe me a cloth."

"I know."

"Do not pay me with a day you have already given someone else."

I looked at her. She was not smiling.

"I will find a way to repay it," I said.

"Then do that before you call another promise settled."

Below us, the keeper took down the old tally for the broken step and laid it beside the new offers. The two strips did not yet make a finished agreement. Madan's niece still had to ask her mother; Raya still had to judge the pieces; the owner still had to show the cloth. Dala's frame was broken, and no one had paid for its loss.

The tide had begun to fall. The higher step emerged slowly, showing the split edge beneath the wet surface. It was smaller work than the post I had promised the cargo owner. I could see the place where it might be done, and the people who might do it. For once I could also see the things that would stop us before anyone lifted a tool.

* * *

Madan's niece came to the hall before the water had fallen enough to uncover the upper step.

She carried no wood. She had the measuring cord in her hand and a question for the keeper.

"Has the cloth been shown?"

"Not yet," the keeper said.

"Then my mother has not sent her boat."

I had been waiting for her since dawn. I had imagined the pieces arriving with the first load, the work beginning as soon as the bank dried. Her mother's boat had another cargo to carry. The niece would not ask the crew to change its journey for a promise no one had paid.

"Will she bring them if the cloth is here?" I asked.

"She says there may be room after her own goods. She wants to see what is being taken, and she will not wait while men argue about its price."

"Can you tell her we agree?"

"Do we?"

The keeper looked at me. I had no cloth to show.

The owner of the smaller boat arrived a little later with one of his crew and a roll wrapped against the damp. He set it on the bench beside the keeper, opened the covering, and unrolled a length of cloth wide enough for two people to hold between them.

"This is mine," he said. "I brought it on the boat yesterday. I will give it for Madan's wood if Raya accepts the wood for the step."

Madan was there to examine it. He rubbed the edge, pulled at one loose thread, and asked whether it had been stored wet. The owner showed him the dry inner folds.

"That will do," Madan said.

"Then take it," the owner answered.

"Not until the maker has seen the pieces."

They looked at Raya. She was waiting outside the hall with her nephew. She had brought her own tools and a strip of cord, but no food.

"Bring the pieces to the higher step," she said. "I will judge them there. If they are unsound, they go back. I will not have you tell the owner you bought a post from me."

Madan's niece went to fetch her mother. The cloth remained on the bench under the keeper's eye.

We waited through the last of the falling tide. The keeper counted the food offered for the day's work. The smaller-boat owner had brought cooked rice and fish for Raya and one helper, enough for the meal he had promised. Raya's nephew had eaten at home; he was there to carry her basket and fetch water, not to be counted as the promised helper.

"Who will lift the timber?" she asked.

"I will," I said.

"And who will hold it while you move?"

The smaller-boat owner pointed to the crewman who had brought the food.

"He can help until our jars are ready to load."

Raya looked at the man.

"Did he agree?"

"I did," the man said. "For the morning. The owner has paid me for the trip and for this work. If it takes longer, ask me again."

Raya nodded.

"Then there are two of you. Do not lift until I tell you where."

Dala's sister came down the path carrying a mat. She had removed the fish from her drying rack and laid them farther from the steps. Pawi followed with the rack's smaller end on his shoulder. Dala carried the other end. They stopped when they saw me.

"You said you would help move it," Dala's sister said.

"I did."

She pointed to the heavier end.

"Take that from Dala. She has the frame to mend."

I put my shoulder beneath the rack. It was heavier than it had looked yesterday. The wood pressed into my neck, and the lower crosspiece caught against my knee when we turned. Pawi walked ahead and called where the ground was firm. Dala's sister chose the new place. I set the rack down where she told me, not where it was easiest for me to leave it.

"One low tide," she said.

"I remember."

"Then remember where it goes back."

She returned to her fish. Dala stayed beside the broken frame, working the old knots free with Pawi when he could spare a hand.

Madan's niece came back with her mother's boat as the steps emerged. Her mother stood at the stern, watching the crew lower the two short pieces to the bank.

"I have brought these after my own load," she said. "I have not lent you the boat for the day."

"You have done what was asked," the keeper answered.

"Then mark that the pieces have been delivered for inspection. If they go back, I will take them when I return. If they stay, Madan can tell me who will carry the cloth home."

The keeper made the mark. The mother waited until Raya had turned both pieces and pressed at the old split. She watched the maker test the cut ends against the sound edge of the step.

"Neither will serve as a post," Raya said.

"We know," Madan's niece answered.

"But the sound length can support the new step if it lies above the water and if we keep the load small. The split end of the other piece must be cut away. It may still hold the edge where people put a foot, not the pull of a boat."

Madan asked whether she would accept them on those terms.

"Yes. For this repair. Not for the lower berth."

He told the keeper to mark the sale. Only then did he take the cloth. His niece rewound her measuring cord and went with her mother to finish the boat's journey. She had not promised another.

The first part of the work was taking the old step apart.

The edge lifted more easily than I expected. Beneath it the ground was firm on one side and hollow on the other. Raya made us stop while she tested the place with her own foot. She told the crewman to clear the loose mud and asked me to carry the sound piece to the higher end. When I reached for the other piece, she caught my wrist.

"That one still has the split."

"I was going to put it beside the first."

"Then you were going to hide the part I need to cut away."

I put it down.

She showed me the line she wanted removed. The crewman held the timber while she worked. The sound part was shorter afterward, but she could place it where it would take only the weight of a foot. She refused to use it where the larger boat might strike.

We worked while the water fell. I carried wood and stones, then carried them back when Raya found that I had put them where they would make the edge uneven. The crewman said little. He had his own boat to return to, and each mistake I made kept him longer from it.

At one point I asked whether we should put a rail along the step so the jars would not slip.

"With whose wood?" Raya asked.

I looked at the offcut she had set aside.

"That."

"It is split."

"Could it hold a rail?"

"It might hold a hand until someone leaned against it. Then it would teach them to fall."

She left it on the ground. I did not ask again.

By the time the tide turned, the old upper post still stood where it had stood in the morning. Beside it, one worn step had been replaced and the edge beneath it made firm enough for a person to carry a small load. The new work was hardly visible from the river. From the path above, I could see the difference: a foot could reach the higher ground without finding the broken corner.

Raya sat on the step and tested it herself.

"Bring the smaller boat," she told its owner. "Not the cargo boat from across the river. I want a load I can see and stop."

The owner brought his vessel to the upper post with two jars of oil and a basket of cloth. He had left the rest of his goods aboard the other boat. His crewman held the line while Raya checked that it lay against the old post, not the sounding stake.

"One person carrying at a time," she said. "Do not set a jar on the edge and leave it there. If the step moves, put the jar back aboard."

The first jar came ashore in the owner's arms. He stepped on the new wood, paused, and moved onto the higher ground. The timber did not shift. He set the jar on the path and returned for the second.

I wanted him to hurry. The water was rising and I could feel the eyes of the people gathered above us. Raya made him wait until she had looked beneath the edge again.

"Now," she said.

He carried the second jar. The crewman followed with the cloth basket. The step held them separately. No one called it safe for a full cargo.

The owner set down the basket and looked toward his larger rival's landing across the river.

"That is one less jar I will break here," he said.

"At this tide," Raya answered.

"Will it hold tomorrow?"

"If the bank stays as it is, and you do not put a heavier boat against it. I will look again after the next high water."

The keeper marked the test: two jars, one basket, one boat tied to the existing upper post, three separate crossings of the repaired step. She did not mark a second berth open.

The smaller-boat owner offered Raya the food he had brought. She ate with the crewman in the shade, then asked the keeper to record that they had received it. She had been promised something for her own household when the work was done. She had not forgotten.

"What of your sister's roof?" I asked.

"The meal is for today. The covering is still to be agreed."

The smaller-boat owner looked at the cloth he had already given Madan.

"I have another piece, but I have not promised it."

"Then do not promise it because everyone is watching," Raya said. "Come and speak to me when you know what you can spare."

She did not let the day's success turn into an unpaid debt hidden beneath praise.

Dala's sister called from the path. The water had risen enough that she wanted her rack returned before the next fishing tide. I went to help. Pawi had the smaller end again. This time we set it down without catching the crosspiece against my knee.

Dala had not repaired the frame. She had loosened the broken corner and laid the reusable cord in a separate coil. The snapped length lay beside it.

"Can you use the landing now?" I asked.

"I can work where the rack belongs," her sister answered. "That is what we agreed."

Dala held up the broken corner.

"And this is what we have not settled."

The keeper came to us with the strip recording the damage. She had not put it away with the step's account.

"I have the claim," she said.

"Will it buy cord?" Dala asked.

"Not yet."

"Then keep it where people can see it."

The keeper hung it beside the other landing strips, below the account of the new step. Dala watched her do it.

I wanted to tell Dala that I would mend the frame tomorrow. She had already heard me promise to carry what she asked. I had spent this low tide lifting timber for Raya. Amaya still waited for the cloth I owed her. I had no cord of my own to bring.

"I can come after the next low water," I said.

"Come when I send for you," Dala answered. "I will not keep my work waiting for your promises."

I accepted that. It was the first thing I had given her today that she could use.

The owner of the salted fish appeared on the far side of the path as the last jar was being carried away. He looked at the repaired step, the single boat at the upper post, and the empty lower berth.

"Will my next load fit there?" he asked.

"Not at the lower steps," I said.

"Then you have not made me a landing."

"We have made one step sound for smaller boats."

He looked at Raya.

"Will you build the lower post if I bring wood?"

"Not at the place we tested," she said. "I will inspect another place if its users agree. I will not promise your vessel a berth until the ground and the work are ready."

He turned to me.

"Will you ask every household again?"

"Yes."

"Then my cargo stays across the river."

He left. He had not come to threaten us this time. He had come to find out whether the place could take his boat. It could not.

The keeper took the last marks to the hall. Madan's wood had been exchanged for the smaller-boat owner's cloth. The niece's mother had carried it on her own journey and had not lent her boat. Raya and the crewman had eaten the meal promised for their work. Dala's sister had recovered her rack after one tide. The step had held the agreed test, and the lower berth remained closed. The maker's further payment and Dala's damage claim remained open.

Amaya read the strips without moving them.

"Whose cloth paid for the wood?" she asked.

"The smaller-boat owner's."

"And whose food paid for the work?"

"His."

"Then the hall has not given either."

"No."

She nodded, then looked at me.

"You still owe me one."

"I know."

Outside, a narrow boat approached the upper post. Its steersman waited until the last line had been cleared before bringing his bow alongside. The repaired step was visible from the hall only when someone stood on it. There was no wall around the landing, no new post below it, and no man assigned to watch the passage through the night.

I had wanted the bank to hold every boat that came. It held one, for a time, when the people who owned the wood, food, work and space had agreed to let it. The people who had not agreed kept their boats and their claims. Tomorrow the tide would return, and someone would have to see whether the step still stood.
