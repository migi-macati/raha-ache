I had imagined the hearing would take an hour.

The fisherman would ask for a new net. The porter would demand payment for his lost passage. The boat owner would count his broken jars. I would agree to reasonable terms, everyone would thank me, and Sulad and I would spend the afternoon repairing our outrigger.

I had thought about the injured rower too. Mostly I had hoped his hand would be better by morning.

By midday, I was beginning to suspect my mother had arranged the hearing to punish me.

The fisherman brought his brother, who had woven the net. The porter brought a witness who swore that the cargo boat had delayed him before I arrived. The cargo boat's owner brought his daughter, the girl I had helped pull ashore, though he insisted she had come only because there was no one to watch her.

She watched me from behind his knee.

The injured rower's sister came in his place. She carried the paddle he had used to earn his food and set it against the wall. When someone moved it to make room, she put it back where I could see it.

The harbor steward sat cross-legged with his tally board. My mother sat beside him. No one had offered me a seat.

“Three sections of net,” said the fisherman. “And the stakes that held them.”

“Two sections,” said the steward. “The third was old.”

“Old things can still catch fish.”

The porter agreed loudly. I had no idea why he cared about the net.

The steward scratched a mark into his board. “You can mend two sections by tomorrow.”

“With whose hands?” the fisherman asked.

I stepped forward. “Mine, if that settles it.”

Everyone looked at me.

Sulad, standing near the doorway, made a sound that might have been a cough.

The fisherman inspected my hands. “Do you know how to mend a net?”

“I can learn.”

“Before tomorrow's tide?”

I looked at the torn mesh spread across his knees. It was more complicated than I remembered.

“Then I will find people to help.”

The fisherman's expression hardened. “My people were already helping you yesterday.”

He was right. I wished he had been less pleased to discover it.

I tried another solution. “Give him cloth from the household stores. Enough to pay for the loss.”

The steward did not move.

“Did you hear me?”

“Yes, my lord. Which cloth?”

“The cloth we received from the last traders.”

“That belongs to the household,” my mother said.

“I belong to the household.”

“Then you should know what it owes.”

I turned to her. “You said I was to settle the claims.”

“I did.”

“Then why will you not let me?”

“I am waiting to hear how you intend to pay.”

It seemed a distinction designed to annoy me.

The porter raised his hand, as if he were a boy in lessons. “My lord, I cannot eat a promise. My passage is gone. I had buyers waiting.”

“What were you carrying?”

“Salt and dried fish.”

“Then take the next boat.”

“There is no next boat until the tide serves. My buyers may purchase elsewhere.”

I had seen men argue about tides all my life. I had not understood that missing one could cost a household its food.

The girl behind her father's knee whispered something. He hushed her. I asked what she had said.

“She says you are the one who threw the rope.”

“Yes.”

“She asks whether your boat has been repaired.”

“Not yet.”

She considered this and hid behind him again.

I took off the small silver ornament I wore at my waist, a gift given to me by a visiting trader. I laid it on the steward's board.

“Sell that,” I said. “Use what it brings for the broken jars and the porter's loss. If it is not enough, tell me what remains.”

The boat owner reached toward it. My mother stopped him with a glance.

“And the fisherman?” she asked.

“He needs his net before tomorrow. Sulad and I will help mend it. We will bring others who agree to work, and we will replace the stakes.”

Sulad straightened.

“Who agrees to work?” my mother asked.

I looked at him. “Do you?”

“I'll help,” he said. “If someone teaches us what we're doing.”

The fisherman stared at us for so long that I thought he might refuse.

“You'll spoil more knots than you mend,” he said at last. “But I can use hands for carrying the stakes.”

The rower's sister lifted the paddle from the wall.

“And my brother?”

The boat owner looked away. I had been hoping someone else would answer.

“His hand may heal,” I said.

“Yes,” she replied. “It may.”

“I can pay him while he cannot work.”

“For how long?”

I had no idea.

“And when the silver is gone?” she asked.

The boat owner protested that his steering oar had broken before I came alongside. I reminded him that I had stopped his daughter from being thrown into the stakes. Both things were true. Neither made the woman sit down.

“I will bring him food,” I said. “Tomorrow, and the next day. We can hear the claim again when we know how his hand is healing.”

“Then you are not settling it,” she said.

“No.”

That was the first answer I had given her that seemed to satisfy her. She took the paddle and moved toward the door. She had not thanked me.

The steward began dividing the silver's likely value among the claims. The porter objected to his share. The boat owner argued that a painted jar was worth more than an unpainted one. By the time they agreed, the sun had crossed the center of the courtyard.

My mother gave the steward permission to record what had been settled and to leave the rower's claim open. The men left, still talking over one another.

I waited until they were gone.

“You could have told me the household stores were not mine.”

“I did,” she said.

“Yesterday you said I would hear the claims. You knew I would offer what I did not own.”

“Yes.”

I stared at her.

She looked almost tired enough to smile. “You learn quickly when the cost is yours.”

“I learned that three men can argue over a broken jar longer than it takes to sail across the bay.”

“Four,” Sulad said. “You argued too.”

I gave him a look. He gave it back.

My mother rose. “Stay, Ache. There is another matter.”

Sulad started for the door. She stopped him as well.

“Your place is beside him,” she said. “You may as well learn what that costs.”

* * *

Men came to the hall with matters too large for a single fisherman and too small, I had always thought, for a ruler.

The first was a woman whose household had provided food to a visiting crew and had not been paid. The second concerned a quarrel over where a merchant could unload jars without blocking a neighbor's landing. The third was a request for men to escort a boat past a disputed stretch of water.

My mother heard each one. Sometimes she decided. Sometimes she sent the speakers away with instructions to return with witnesses. Once she refused to decide at all until two elders who had been arguing outside came in together.

She did not command the river the way I commanded a sail. She asked who had promised what, who could be trusted to remember, and who would still be angry when the immediate problem was solved.

I began to understand why she was always tired.

Then the visitors from Tondo arrived.

There were four of them. Their leader wore a dark cloth at his waist and carried a small wrapped bundle. He bowed to my mother with the confidence of a man who expected to be welcomed.

He was introduced as a messenger from Lakandula.

I knew the name as well as my own. Lakandula was my kinsman across the river, ruler of Tondo, and a man whose gifts had filled our household more than once. When I was younger, I had thought the visits meant he liked us.

The messenger laid his bundle on the floor. Inside was a carved wooden token.

“My lord sends his greetings,” he said. “He has heard of the trouble at the landing. He is pleased that the child was saved.”

I stiffened. News had crossed the river before the damage was repaired.

“He offers men to help keep the approaches clear,” the messenger continued. “And a clerk to keep a common record of boats passing between our shores. Fewer quarrels, fewer delays. The merchants will welcome it.”

One of our own traders, seated near the wall, nodded.

“Who asked for this?” I said.

The messenger turned toward me. “Those who must pass safely, young raha.”

“I know how to keep our landing safe.”

“Then the offer should please you. A good captain welcomes another paddle when the current is strong.”

He spoke gently. That made it worse.

I looked to my mother, expecting her to dismiss him.

Instead she asked how the records would be kept.

The messenger described a clerk from Tondo sitting beside a clerk from Maynila. Each would witness the other's tallies. Their men would help direct arriving boats when the river was crowded. No vessel, he said, would be made to wait while two sets of officers argued over whose landing it had entered.

The trader by the wall spoke again. “We lose time to these quarrels. Sometimes we pay twice before we unload.”

I turned. “To whom?”

He hesitated.

The messenger answered for him. “To men who claim authority from both shores. A common record would prevent it.”

I could see the trap. If Tondo kept a record of our boats, Tondo could one day claim the right to count them. If its men stood at our landing, people might begin obeying them.

“You want to put your men on our shore,” I said.

The messenger bowed his head. “I offer help.”

“Then send rope and timber. Keep your men.”

A murmur moved through the hall.

My mother said my name quietly. I stopped, though I had more to say.

She questioned the messenger about the length of the arrangement, the number of men, and what would happen when the clerks disagreed. He had answers prepared for most of it. When he did not, she made him repeat that he could not promise on his lord's behalf.

At last she said, “For one season, the clerks may compare records at the river crossing. No officer from Tondo will collect anything on this shore. Any dispute comes before us both. Put that in your message.”

The messenger inclined his head.

The trader by the wall looked relieved.

I felt as though someone had opened our gate and called it hospitality.

After the visitors left, I spoke before the hall had emptied.

“Why give them even a season?”

My mother faced me. “Because our merchants are losing goods while our men argue over the same boats.”

“They will ask for more.”

“Perhaps.”

“You know they will.”

“Then we will answer when they ask.”

“What will they want to count after the boats?” I asked. “The goods? The men who row? The people those men answer to?”

My mother looked toward the trader who had spoken in favor of the plan. He had already left.

“You are right to ask,” she said. “But if I refuse every useful arrangement because it might be abused, someone else pays that price today.”

“And if you agree, someone else pays tomorrow.”

She did not answer at once. It was the first time that day I felt I had said something she needed to consider.

I heard a few men shift behind me. I lowered my voice.

“Father would not have agreed.”

My mother's expression changed. It was slight, but I saw it.

“You never knew your father,” she said.

Neither did she raise her voice nor look away.

I wanted to take the words back. They had already reached everyone in the hall.

“Come to my chamber,” she said. “When you are ready to speak to me without an audience.”

* * *

I found her beside an open chest, folding a piece of cloth whose color had faded at the creases.

On the wall hung a wooden paddle. It was shorter than the ones used by our boatmen, its grip worn smooth. My mother had kept it for as long as I could remember.

“Was that his?” I asked.

She looked at the paddle. “He used it when he was young.”

I had asked about my father many times. The answers had always been pieces: a boat he liked, a quarrel he settled, a feast where he laughed too loudly. There was no memory of him that belonged to me.

“He died when I was an infant,” I said. “I know.”

“You know the words.”

“I know you have ruled since then.”

She folded the cloth once more. “Do you know what that meant?”

“That you kept Maynila for me.”

She set the cloth down.

“When he died, you could not even hold your head upright. Men came to offer condolences, and before the mourning was over they came again to ask who would confirm their rights, who would honor old promises, who would hear their disputes. Some waited for you to grow. Others decided they could not wait.”

“Lakandula?”

“Many people. Some are still our friends.”

I did not like that answer. I preferred an enemy I could name.

“He is my cousin,” I said. “He should have helped.”

“He has his own people to answer to.”

“And that excuses taking from us?”

“It explains why he sees an opening where you see an insult.”

I walked to the doorway. From her chamber I could see the river, the landing, the houses beyond it. The afternoon tide had begun to change. Boats that had seemed still were turning on their lines.

“When I am raha,” I said, “I will make the boundaries clear.”

“Which boundaries?”

“Between us and Tondo.”

“Where will you draw them? Through the water? Across the boats that trade on both shores? Through families with kin on either side?”

I had an answer for land. I had none for a boat.

“Your father left you a claim,” she said. “I have spent your life keeping people willing to recognize it.”

“You let Lakandula stand closer every year.”

“Perhaps I have. And perhaps refusing him yesterday would have brought his men here tomorrow, while our own merchants asked why I preferred a quarrel to their livelihoods.”

I turned back. “So we keep giving way?”

“No. We choose where to stand.”

She opened the chest again and took out a narrow strip of cloth marked with an old household sign. She placed it in my hand.

“This was kept with his things. I cannot tell you what he would do now. I can tell you he had to persuade people too.”

I rubbed the worn thread between my fingers. I had expected some great instruction, a secret my father had left for me. It was only cloth.

“Do you think I am unfit?” I asked.

“I think you are sixteen, and that yesterday you tried to pull a loaded boat with a line too short for it.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

For the first time I saw that she was not waiting for me to become ready. She had been deciding without me all my life, and she would have to keep deciding tomorrow.

“Let me read the landing records,” I said.

She studied me.

“Why?”

“Because the trader said they sometimes pay twice. I want to know when it began.”

She called for the steward.

* * *

The records smelled of smoke and damp wood. The steward spread his tallies across a low table while Sulad held a lamp.

“Some payments were made in goods,” the steward explained. “Some in labor. Not every boat left a mark.”

“Then how do you know which men paid twice?”

“We hear complaints.”

“Show me.”

He sorted the boards into piles. I looked for the same boat appearing twice, for the same cargo unloaded on both banks, for a fee collected by a man whose name I did not know.

The steward grew impatient.

“Not all the men collecting are ours,” he said.

I stopped.

“Whose are they?”

He looked toward the doorway. “Some say they act for Tondo. Some say they act for both shores.”

“Who told them they could?”

“That,” he said, “is the quarrel.”

I had imagined Tondo's encroachment as an armed boat crossing the river, men stepping ashore with spears, a banner raised where ours had stood.

It was harder to fight a man who arrived with a tally board.

Sulad tapped one record. “This boat brought timber. The same captain returned the next week.”

I leaned closer. “And paid again.”

“Different collector.”

The steward nodded. “The captain complained. Then he paid. He needed to unload.”

There were more marks like it. Not enough to prove that Lakandula had ordered every collection. Enough to show that men were already making decisions in his name.

On one board a second set of marks had been scratched beside the cargo.

“Those are not payments,” I said.

“Crew numbers,” the steward replied. “The man from Tondo says they can help identify a boat if its owner denies a debt.”

“Is that what my mother agreed to?”

“She agreed that the two shores would compare their accounts.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” he said. “It is all the answer I have.”

I pushed the boards away.

“We should stop them.”

“With whose men?” the steward asked.

I thought of the men who had helped me at the quay yesterday. They had come when I called. Then I thought of the fisherman and the porter, and how quickly their obedience had become a debt.

“How many can we send?” I asked.

The steward did not answer immediately.

That was answer enough.

Outside, the river moved between the two shores as it always had. I had sailed it a hundred times, certain that knowing its currents meant I understood it.

I had never once asked who was counting the boats.

Sulad put out the lamp. “We still owe the fisherman his stakes.”

“I know.”

“First light?”

“First light.”

He lifted the tallies. “And after that?”

I looked across the dark water toward Tondo.

“After that,” I said, “we find out who has been giving orders in our name.”
