## Canon, chronology and source boundary

**Bonus chapter, after the completed 30-chapter core trilogy.** The Book III Chapter 10 finale resolves a fictional dispute over a damaged landing, rival commercial channels, separately paid work and consent. It does **not** establish Ache as commander of a standing army, founder of a unified kingdom, creator of a universal port levy, father of Sulayman or overlord of Lakandula. Do not restore any of those discarded premises by summary. The story now makes a substantial chronological jump from the early-sixteenth-century core to **1570–1571**. Any changes in Ache's standing during the intervening decades require specific fictional scenes, cautious compression or independent evidence; the novel must not claim to have shown an ascent it never dramatized.

The [1570 *Relation of the Voyage to Luzon*](/raha-ache/sources/relacion-voyage-luzon-1570/) is a partisan expedition account, preserved here through the Blair–Robertson English edition ([Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html)). It describes Goiti's approach, a tide-sensitive river, a palisade, cannon, foreign ships, trade, burning and civilian losses. **Claims by Spanish participants, Chinese crews and other speakers must remain attributed testimony**; numbers are not a census, and motive is not independently proved.

The [1571 Riquel account](/raha-ache/sources/riquel-1571/) names **Raha Ache the Old** and **Raha Solimane the Young** among Manila's principales and **Sibunao Lacandola** as a Tondo principal. These names are firmer than the novel's invented household ties. Separately, the 1570 *Relation* calls the younger Soliman the **nephew** of an older ruler and calls that ruler his **uncle** (English edition pp. 75–76, 82–83). Identifying the unnamed older uncle in that passage with Ache the Old named in 1571 is a plausible cross-document inference, not an explicit name in the 1570 passage. Neither document supports making Ache Sulayman's father or importing a specific earlier political history. The [1572 relation](/raha-ache/sources/relacion-1572/) is important to bonus Chapter 12; its exact original-language citation and succession passage still need collation. Do not silently promote its project paraphrase to a verified quotation.

**1570–71 sequence:** Goiti's approach and violence in 1570 precede Legazpi's establishment in 1571 and the reported Bangkusay fighting. Treat the identity and labeling of the Bangkusay leader, often conflated with Tarik Sulayman/Bambalito, as a disputed historical identification. Do not place the 1572 baptism, inheritance or death in this chapter. The earlier 1521 encounter may be remembered by Ache, but no character knows the later Spanish colonial outcome in advance.

## Chapter purpose and dramatic question

An older Ache faces a foreign expedition whose language of friendship, peace, protection and obedience can carry consequences his earlier bargains did not. The old landing conflict offers an emotional memory of the cost of an unearned promise, not a ready-made state system that can defeat the invasion. The question is how Ache and the people around him respond when a declaration by a stronger party may change the terms even after it has been translated correctly.

The historical outcome is constrained: fighting and burning in 1570, renewed Spanish presence and negotiated submission/accommodation in 1571, with resistance and loss. The fictional suspense belongs to what specific people know, can save, misunderstand or refuse. Do not turn Maynila's Chinese and Japanese residents into background decoration or a single faction.

**Theme:** Words carry unequal weight when one side can enforce its reading. **Subthemes:** translation, property and fire, intergenerational distance without assumed kinship, trade, protection, civilian vulnerability, the limits of retrospective memory.

## Six-scene causal architecture

### Scene 1: The ships at the mouth

Open in 1570 with Ache visibly older. Establish the elapsed decades through a few concrete changes, without an omniscient history lecture or retroactive claim that the core novel built a kingdom. Maynila's defenses, waterfront and trade are present in the 1570 source; their exact development since the 1520s is not documented in this novel. A tide-aware approach and a report of foreign sails force Ache to decide whom he trusts for firsthand news. A memory of 1521 complicates his judgment but does not predict events. **Turn:** the visitors cannot be treated as merely another boat seeking a landing.

### Scene 2: What Mehomete could say

Give [Mehomete](/raha-ache/characters/mehomete-maynila/) a bounded POV transfer if needed. The 1570 relation places him as a Maynila-born provisions supplier and interpreter who had dealt with Spaniards in Cebu, returned with his brother, and had a wife and son. He has local kin, obligations and reasons to protect people; exact private conversations are fictional. Show him trying to carry an offer or warning between speakers with incompatible assumptions. His conversion is attested by the Spanish account, but neither his motive nor his inner conviction is transparent. **Turn:** an apparently shared word proves to have different practical consequences.

### Scene 3: The people in the houses

Anchor the Chinese ships and resident families in attributed 1570 evidence. The source reports four Chinese ships, forty married Chinese and twenty Japanese residents; treat those figures as a source's snapshot. [Pablo](/raha-ache/characters/pablo-japanese-1570/) may appear as a Japanese resident professing Christianity; the report that he fought by the artillery is hearsay, not proven. Chinese sailors' allegation that local rulers seized rudders and goods remains a contested claim. Give individual merchants and residents their own decisions about cargo, shelter and families. **Turn:** the political confrontation threatens people whose aims do not align neatly with either armed side.

### Scene 4: The fire

The 1570 encounter collapses into violence and burning. Follow a limited witness position; no protagonist can see every house or decide every motive. The Spanish account's self-justifications must not become neutral narration. Distinguish a person's direct observation from what others later tell them. Mehomete's translations, Ache's decisions and residents' attempts to protect one another may matter without granting any of them the power to avert the documented destruction. **Turn:** the town's damage becomes an immediate human fact, not a symbolic defeat alone.

### Scene 5: The names they wrote

Move clearly to 1571, when Legazpi's force returns and the record names Ache, Sulayman and Lakandula. Show their political choices as distinct. The 1570 narrative reports an uncle-nephew relation between an older ruler and Soliman; the identification of that older ruler with Ache relies on reading it alongside the 1571 naming. Do not replace this with an invented father-son relation, subordination or hereditary office. If Ache and Lakandula favor accommodation in a particular exchange, attribute it to what each says and can actually authorize. Sulayman's position requires source-specific treatment, not a predetermined youthful-warrior stereotype. **Turn:** the price of an agreement is tested by the foreign side's power to define it.

### Scene 6: The river after the fighting

Stage the 1571 resistance/Bangkusay aftermath with the leader's identity treated cautiously. If a named character did not witness an event, give them news as news, with a source and delay. End with Spanish power established in Manila's political reality, and Ache alive with limited choices that still affect households and property. The chapter must close its own 1570–71 conflict without inventing a restored sovereignty or foretelling Ache's death. **Handoff:** Bonus Chapter 12 follows the documented 1572 baptism, inheritance claim and death, with the original-language succession gap kept visible.

## Character and historical QA gates

**Ache:** older and historically named among Maynila's principales in 1571, but his exact intervening career and relationships are uncertain. Avoid an unearned continuity leap from a repaired landing to a unified state. **Sulayman:** named as the Young in 1571; the 1570 relation describes him as nephew of an unnamed older ruler, plausibly Ache when the documents are compared. Keep the source attribution and do not invent paternal kinship or a predetermined battlefield role. **Lakandula:** Tondo principal, not Ache's subordinate. **Mehomete:** documented interpreter/provisioner with local family stakes; his private voice is fiction. **Pablo:** Japanese Christian resident; artillery service hearsay. **Foreign Chinese households and seamen:** individuals with interests and risks; allegations and numbers remain attributed. **Spanish officers:** constrained by the 1570–71 source chronology, but their explanations are partisan.

Each scene must identify who observed what, who reported it, what was translated, and what the narrator cannot know. Avoid modern nationalist speeches, imagined port bureaucracies, all-knowing diplomatic councils, guaranteed loyalty, unsupported marital or paternal links, and invented quotations presented as primary evidence. Preserve the trilogy's first-person Ache baseline with only necessary, clearly signaled POV transfers. Historical Notes must cite specific witnesses and editions and flag the still-uncollated original-language texts. Review the title *The Foreigners Return* after prose drafting; it should earn its meaning through Ache's memory of 1521 and the new 1570 stakes, not a generic invasion label.
