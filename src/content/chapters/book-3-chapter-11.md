The man at the river gate had tied his warning to a length of cord.

He held it up when I came down from the houses. There were three knots in it, each pulled hard enough to leave a white crease in the fiber. I had seen men use such marks for wages, for journeys, for loads still waiting. This man had made his knots for ships he had not seen.

"Three?" I asked.

"Three that the boatman named. He says there are more small craft with them."

"Did he count them?"

"He saw masts beyond the point. He says the larger one carries guns."

"Then the cord tells me what he said. It does not tell me what he saw."

The gatekeeper lowered his hand. He had known me long enough to let the correction pass. The younger men behind him looked at the cord as if I had made its knots disappear.

I put my palm against the wood of the gate. It was not the same wood that had stood here when I was young. The lower posts had been replaced, then the upper ones, and the earth beneath them had been packed again after a flood. The river kept none of the things men wanted it to keep. My knees had learned that before I had.

Farther down the bank, a boy was calling for a basket to be moved. He had taken it from the path of a woman bringing a net up from the water. The woman shouted that he had put it in the place where she would spread her catch. He moved it again. Neither asked me to decide where it belonged.

I remembered a time when a rotten step had closed the landing for a whole market, and a cord owner had offered another channel. Raya had made us test the new wood before carrying a basket across it. I could still recall the sound the bearing made when the water began to take the small gravel away. The wood was gone now. The place had been mended and changed more times than I could count. The people who used it still asked whether the ground would hold.

A man came up from the lower market with salt drying on his sleeve. He was one of the traders who carried news along the coast. He had not brought salt today.

"Who sent you?" the gatekeeper asked.

"No one. I wanted to tell the people who might send boats out."

"Tell them what?" I asked.

"At the next coast a boat came back without part of its rice. The men aboard said armed strangers took it. They did not know whether those strangers were the same men now in the bay."

"Did you see the boat?"

"I saw its owner. I saw the place where the baskets had been. I heard the story from him."

"Then I will hear it that way."

The trader looked past me at the gate. He had come because the people behind it could choose whether to send boats into the bay. He wanted the warning carried farther than his own voice.

"I am not asking you to believe every word," he said. "I am asking you not to send a child out to look at those ships as if they were coming for fish."

"I will not."

He left before I could ask him more. He had another path to take.

***

The Chinese ships lay where the river widened toward the houses. Their masts had been familiar for days, their crews buying water and food from people who knew the prices. One captain had sent a man ashore that morning to ask whether a cargo of jars could be unloaded before the next high water. The man waited now with his hands on his belt while the boat owners argued about space.

He was not waiting for me.

When I came near, he turned and pointed beyond the river mouth. His words were quick, and the trader beside him translated only part of them. I heard that a vessel had come into the bay with a high side and a shape of sail he did not know. I heard that smaller craft traveled with it. I did not hear that he knew the men aboard.

"Has your captain spoken to them?" I asked.

The trader put the question. The man shook his head.

"He says their boat did not go close enough. They watched from the outer water."

"Does he want to leave?"

The man answered before the trader finished translating. He put both hands flat, then moved one forward and one back. The trader watched him and said, "He wants to keep his ship where it can turn. He does not want another vessel lying across his way."

That was an answer about his ship. I could not turn it into an answer about the other Chinese captains. The river held their goods, their crews and their different debts. I had spent too much of my life hearing one merchant speak and calling it the mind of a market.

The captain's man pointed to the palisade. He asked whether the gates would stay open for traders.

"They are open now," I said.

He waited for more.

"If the men at the gate close them, they must tell the people who are outside. I cannot promise what every man will do when a strange ship comes near."

The trader repeated this. The captain's man looked displeased. He had wanted to know whether he could bring his jars through before the tide changed, and I had told him what I could not promise.

"Ask where he wants to put the jars," I said.

The answer was a dry patch near the market path, not inside the gate. We sent someone to ask the woman who used that ground. The captain's man stayed with his cargo until she came. I left them to bargain. There was nothing in his business that required my hand.

At the upper gate a bronze gun stood on its wooden bed. The men had cleaned the metal that morning. A boy was carrying a bucket of water to the place where the gunner kept his tools. He stumbled, spilled part of it and waited for someone to strike him. The gunner told him to fill it again.

I ran a finger along the worn edge of the wood. I had seen guns before. Nearly fifty years had passed since the foreign ships at Brunei had made their thunder over water. I remembered the smell more sharply than the sound. A gun could be heard from a great distance. The man who fired it might not know what stood where the ball fell.

The boy returned with the bucket. I stepped away so he could pass.

***

Soliman came to the gate after the sun had moved beyond the high roof.

He had been speaking with men who kept watch farther along the river. His hands were stained where he had held a dark cord. He looked first at the water, then at the gun, and only afterward at me.

"How many ships?" he asked.

"One man says three masts. Another saw a large vessel and smaller craft. Neither has counted the men."

"We should put more people at the mouth."

"To do what?"

"Watch. Stop a boat if it tries to come in without speaking."

"Can they stop it?"

"They can warn us before it reaches the gate."

"Then tell them to watch and to bring back what they see. Do not send them to fight a ship they have not measured."

He looked at me with the impatience I remembered from my own younger days. I had been impatient with old men who asked for the length of a rope when I wanted them to approve a crossing. Soliman had reasons of his own for refusing to wait. The palisade, the guns and the houses behind them were matters he had to answer for before people who would never ask me.

"I have men who know the shoals," he said. "They can go in a small boat and return before the tide."

"If they agree to go."

"They will."

"Ask them."

He gave a short breath and turned to one of the watchmen. The man said he would go with another who knew the outer water. He named the time he would return if the wind held. Soliman accepted it and sent them to prepare their boat.

When we were alone he said, "You still speak as if we were arguing over a fish basket."

"I remember what happened when we promised a basket a place before we knew where the water would be."

"This is a ship with guns."

"That is why I want to know where it is."

He looked toward the bay. There was no sail visible from the gate. The river curved and the shore hid the outer anchorage. A messenger could reach us before the ship. A rumor could reach us sooner.

"What if they ask for friendship?" he said.

I thought of the word as I had heard it in other mouths. A merchant could call a buyer friend and still demand payment before unloading. A ruler could call another ruler friend and ask him to send men across a sea. Foreign captains had spoken of friendship while their guns were ready. I did not know what these men would ask.

"Then we will ask what they want done," I said.

Soliman turned away. He had not asked for a lesson in words. He had asked what he should do with men at the gate. He went to see whether the watch boat had left.

***

The boat returned later than its owner had promised.

I was sitting beneath the eave when the watchman came. His companion had a torn patch on his sleeve where a rope had caught it. They had waited outside the river for the tide to turn, then followed the sheltered water toward the foreign anchorage without approaching close enough to be hailed.

"We saw the large vessel," the watchman said. "Its sides stand high. There are smaller craft near it, some with men at their oars. I could not count all of them. The wind moved the boats between us."

"Did you see guns?"

"I saw dark mouths along the side. I have seen guns on ships. I did not go near enough to say how many."

"Did anyone follow you?"

"No."

The companion spoke for the first time. "There is a man with them who speaks like people from here. We heard him call to a boat in our tongue."

"Did you know him?"

"No. He was too far away."

"Did he speak to you?"

"He called to another boat. We did not wait to hear the answer."

I asked where the ships lay and whether the mouth of the river could be sounded from there. The men described the sheltered water and the bar that kept deep vessels outside until the tide was high. Their account agreed on the place. They differed about how many small craft they had seen. I did not make them choose a number.

Soliman came to hear the report. He asked the men to show him the route they had taken. One drew it with his finger on the earth. The other corrected the turn near the outer shoal. They argued for a moment, then found a way to show both lines.

"That is enough for tonight," Soliman said. "Rest. We may need you tomorrow."

The watchman asked whether they would be paid for another journey. Soliman said they would speak of it when he knew what he wanted them to do. The men went away without agreeing to go again.

I stayed beneath the eave until the last light left the water. A man from Maynila was aboard the foreign ships, or at least a man who spoke our language. He might be carrying a message. He might be selling provisions. He might have gone with them because he could not leave.

I knew none of those things. I knew that when the tide rose, the ships could come closer. The people behind our gate would have to decide what they were willing to hear before they knew what the strangers meant.

***

*Mehomete*

At first light I was still thinking about the two men who had come in a small boat the evening before.

They had kept their distance, but one had turned his head toward a voice calling from our deck. He had understood the call. I could tell by the way he looked up, though he had not answered. A sailor beside me had asked whether they meant to come alongside. I told him they had come to see how many vessels lay at anchor and whether the water would bear them closer to the river. He asked how I knew.

"Because I would have done the same."

He wanted a number, not a reason. He asked again how many men had been in the boat. I said two. He took that answer away to the officer who was waiting near the stern.

The boat had turned toward the shore. Its pole rose wet and shining, then dipped out of sight beyond the larger hull. I could no longer see the men's faces. I had left this coast before I knew whether anyone here would want to see mine again. Now I was aboard a ship whose officers expected me to make them welcome.

My brother was sitting beside a basket of rice. He had lifted its lid twice and found the same amount each time.

"Do they want to buy that?" he asked, looking at the officer.

"Not yet."

"They will want to eat before they want to buy."

"I have told them the river is not empty of food."

"Food has owners."

I had brought provisions to the strangers at Cebu. I knew how their hunger changed the sound of a request. When the first baskets came aboard they would ask the price. After a long day without a market they would ask why a price had been named at all. Some paid exactly what they agreed. Some counted the basket differently from the seller. I had learned to stand near enough to hear both numbers.

My brother had kept his house in Maynila. His wife was there, and people who had known us when we were boys. He had agreed to come with me because a familiar face could reach the houses before a stranger's boat. He had not agreed to speak for every house in the town.

"Do you think she has heard?" I asked.

"That foreign ships have entered the bay? Everyone will have heard."

"That you are with them."

"She may have heard that too."

He put the lid back on the rice.

"I will go in when they let me," he said. "I want to hear what the men at the gate are saying before you give them the words of your captain."

"The captain has not asked me to give them anything."

"Then we have a little time."

***

An officer sent for me after the tide began to lift the boats against their ropes.

He stood over a board on which someone had scratched a line for the shore. A smaller mark showed the river mouth. One of the sailors had put a stone on the mark, though I could not tell whether it stood for our vessel or for a gun. The officer moved it aside when I approached.

He spoke slowly. He had learned that a slow sentence gave me time to choose words. He had not learned that the words he wanted were sometimes not there.

He said they had come with peaceful intentions. They wished to speak with the rulers of the town, obtain provisions, and find a place from which their vessels could enter or send boats without being attacked. They did not want a fight. The men at the river should keep their guns silent while the visitors approached.

I asked whether he meant that no one should fire unless fired upon, or that the guns at the river must be put out of use before a boat came near.

He repeated that the visitors came in peace.

I tried another question. If the men at the gate said they would not shoot while a messenger spoke, would that be enough?

He looked toward the gun ports, where the crew were working. He said that he could not let his boats go beneath guns whose keepers had given no assurance.

"Assurance of what?" I asked.

He said a word I had heard before when men on his side wanted another man to promise he would do as they asked. He followed it with a gentler word, one that could be used for friendship. He seemed to think the second made the first easier to accept.

I told him that the people of Maynila could agree to receive a messenger without agreeing that their guns belonged to a visitor's judgment. He asked whether I was refusing to translate his request.

"I am asking which request you want carried."

He stared at me. Then he pointed to the scratched river and said that he wanted his men to be safe.

I could give him that wish in any language. It was the means of keeping them safe that needed naming.

He told me to say that no harm would come to those who dealt with them as friends. When I asked what would happen to those who did not, he said I should not put threats in his mouth.

I had not. I had asked about the space his promise left empty.

I repeated his words as closely as I could, aloud, in the language of the river. My brother listened from the doorway. At the end he shook his head.

"What?" the officer asked.

I told him my brother thought the promise could be heard as an offer to protect traders from violence, or as a demand that the gatekeepers accept the foreign ships' power to decide what violence was.

The officer said I had made a simple message difficult.

My brother spoke before I could answer. "It is difficult before he says it."

I translated that too. The officer's mouth tightened. He said we were to take the message ashore when a boat was ready. He wanted an answer before the next high water.

We left him with his stone and the scratched river.

***

My brother asked to go first.

He wanted to take one small boat and land where his wife's people knew the path. He would tell them he had returned and ask whether someone could come out to hear a message. He would not carry a written demand, and he would not promise that the gates would open.

I told the officer. He asked why I could not go with him.

"Because the first thing my brother must say is that he is still a man of this town. They must decide whether they will hear him before they decide whether to hear you."

The officer considered this. He wanted me to go because I knew the words he had chosen. He wanted my brother to go because he knew the people who would listen. He seemed unwilling to believe that these were different kinds of knowledge.

At last he allowed one boat, with two rowers from our vessel. My brother refused the rowers.

"They will see the foreign clothes before they see me," he said.

The officer thought this was an insult. I told him it was a way to keep the boat from being stopped by men who did not know why it had come.

My brother took a local boatman who had been supplying water to the anchored ships. The boatman agreed after he had been paid for the journey and shown where he could turn back if the landing was closed. He would wait in the boat. He would not go up to the houses or carry the message.

I watched my brother step over the side. He had tied his cloth tightly around his waist. His hands were empty.

"Will you tell them about your wife and son?" he asked.

He meant my wife and my boy, far from this bay. He had asked me the same question on the passage north, when the water was rough and I had spent a night holding down a basket that kept sliding across the deck.

"If they ask," I said.

"They will ask why you went away and came back with these men."

"I can answer why I went away."

"And why you came back?"

I looked toward the officers at the stern. They had offered me work because I could speak. I had accepted because the work paid, because a voyage brought me nearer my birthplace, and because I thought a man who could correct a sentence might keep someone from dying for it. None of those reasons would sound complete to the people at the gate. They were not complete to me.

"I will answer that when I know what I have brought," I said.

He climbed down into the boat. The boatman pushed away from the ship with his pole.

***

When the boat was halfway to the river, a sailor brought me a small cup of water and asked whether the town had a ruler who could answer for all the guns.

I said there were men whose words carried weight at different gates and among different households. He wanted one name. I told him the younger ruler was called Soliman, and that a man could send a message to him without having all the town's answers.

"Someone must decide," he said.

"Someone will decide whether to speak to you. That does not mean he can decide for everyone who owns a gun."

He laughed as if I had described a town without a head. I thought of the men who had supplied him with water, the women who had sold fish to his companions, and the boatman who was carrying my brother for a price. Each had decided something that mattered to the vessel. None had asked permission from the man now laughing.

He went back to his work.

The tide rose. A broad dark line formed along the hull where the current pressed floating weed against it. Beyond the river mouth I could see the small boat stop. It lay there longer than it should have taken to find a landing.

I did not know whether a gatekeeper had turned my brother away or whether the boatman was waiting for a safer line through the shoals. I wanted to call to them, but my voice would not have reached. A man could make a promise on a ship and not know whether it had been heard on shore.

I remembered the first time I had brought rice to the foreigners. I had named a price. One man had smiled and said the price was good. Another had reached for the basket before payment was counted. I had put my hand on the rim and held it there until the coins came. No one had struck me. That small success had persuaded me that I could keep two parties honest by standing between their hands.

Now the hands were on guns.

The small boat moved again. It disappeared beyond the river's turn.

By evening my brother had not returned. The officer asked why his message had not come back with an answer. I told him that my brother had asked to speak with his wife's people before he spoke to any ruler, and that he had not promised to return by sunset. The officer said the messenger must not take longer than the tide required. I could not tell him how long a household needed to decide whether it would receive a stranger's words.

The next morning there was still no boat. The officer sent another man to sound the entrance, but he did not send a second offer. He told me to wait until the first came back.

I watched the shore whenever I was free of work. I could carry the offer. I could not make the two shores mean the same thing by hearing it.

***

The watchman who brought the stranger's name had not heard the stranger speak.

"A man from here came in a boat," he told us. "He gave his name and said another man was waiting with a message. The boat went back before I could fetch anyone."

"Did you tell him where he might land?" Soliman asked.

"I told him the lower steps were full. I said I would ask."

"Did you ask?"

"I am asking now."

Soliman sent him to find a place where a small boat could come ashore without passing beneath the gun at the upper gate. He told the man to take another watchman with him and to report the tide before inviting anyone closer. The watchman went at once. I had no reason to believe the strangers would wait for him.

I wanted to know what name the man in the boat had given. The watchman repeated it. I knew the sound of it, but not the man. He might have been born among these houses or only learned to say that he had. Men had traveled far from Maynila and returned with faces their mothers scarcely recognized. The name alone told us little.

At the market, the Chinese captain's man was still waiting to unload his jars. He had found a patch of dry ground, but the woman who used it had allowed only two baskets there until her fish were taken away. The captain's man had asked for six. Four remained aboard.

He was shouting when I reached him. The trader who had translated for him that morning stood between him and a boat owner, looking tired.

"What has happened?" I asked.

The trader answered before either man could interrupt. "The captain says his steering gear has been taken. The boat owner says the captain owes for a lighter and has not paid."

The captain's man held up both hands, then turned them as if gripping a steering oar. He spoke rapidly. The trader waited until he finished.

"He says men came beside his ship yesterday and carried off the piece they use to turn it. He says they told him it would be returned when the unloading charge was settled. He says the charge was not agreed."

"Did you see the piece taken?" I asked the trader.

"No."

"Did you?" I asked the boat owner.

"I saw it lying in the lighter. It had been brought ashore before I came."

"Who brought it?"

"Ask the men who worked yesterday."

The captain's man stepped toward me. He was not asking for an inquiry into the price of the lighter. He wanted his ship to be able to leave if the foreign vessels moved toward the river.

"Where is the piece now?" I asked.

The boat owner pointed to a shed beyond the drying racks. "The men left it there. I did not take it into my house."

"Then we can look at it."

"No," the captain's man said through the trader. "He wants it returned now. He says the ship cannot wait for men to discuss who saw what."

A boat owner beside us muttered that no captain should be allowed to take a cargo out without paying those who had carried it. The captain's man heard the tone, if not the words. He struck the side of a basket with his open palm.

I remembered how much trouble a strip of marked bamboo had once caused at this landing. Two cuts could mean two loads to one man and two journeys to another. The men who made the marks were long gone. I could still hear the keeper asking which mouth had promised the second trip.

"Tell him," I said to the trader, "that I will walk to the shed and see what is there. I cannot tell him who has the right to hold it until the people who made the agreement have spoken."

The captain's man listened. He said something sharp.

"He says he has already told his story," the trader said.

"I heard it."

"He says a story does not steer his ship."

That was true. I went to the shed.

***

A woman stood in its doorway with a small boy holding the hem of her skirt. She had been hanging reed mats to dry and had moved them aside to make room for a long piece of worked wood. It lay on two blocks, beyond the reach of the water.

The captain's man pointed at it and called out. The woman flinched. The boy hid behind her.

"Whose shed is this?" I asked.

"Mine," she said.

"Who put the wood here?"

"Two men from the lighter. They said they would fetch it when the men from the ship had paid. I told them it could stay until the evening. It is the next day."

"Have they paid you to keep it?"

"No."

"Then you may ask them to move it."

"I have asked. They have not come."

The captain's man was already bending to lift the wood. She caught his sleeve. He stopped. I thought he might push her aside, but he straightened and looked to the trader.

"She says it is not hers to give," the trader told him.

"It is not mine to keep," the woman said. "But they brought it into my shed. If he carries it out and someone says I let him steal it, who will answer for me?"

The captain's man looked at the boy. He took his hands away from the wood.

I asked the woman her name. She gave it as Lian. She said her husband had come from a Chinese ship years earlier, though the boy had been born here. He was away with a cargo that morning. She had no reason to know when he would return.

"Do you want the wood out?" I asked.

"Yes. I want my mats back in the dry. I do not want either set of men coming to my house at night."

I told the boat owner he could send for the lighter's crew. I told the captain's man that Lian would not be made to answer for an argument she had not begun. He asked again when the steering piece would be returned. I had no answer that could make the absent men appear.

A man beside the doorway said the Chinese captains had been complaining about missing gear and goods since the foreign ships came into the bay. He said the strangers would hear those complaints and decide the people of Maynila were thieves.

"Did you hear a captain say he would complain to them?" I asked.

"No. I heard his men talking."

"Then say that."

He looked offended. He had meant to warn us. I had made him sound less certain than he wished.

Lian's boy crept out from behind her. He put one hand on the wood, felt the smooth worn place where many palms had gripped it, and pulled his hand back. His mother drew him away.

"This piece can be moved," she said. "The men who quarrel can quarrel elsewhere."

She had told us what she wanted. We had spent most of the morning asking one another what the ship wanted.

***

Near the upper gate I found a man repairing a basket with a knife whose handle had been bound in pale cord. He spoke our language with the careful rhythm of someone who had learned it in more than one place. I knew him by sight. He was one of the Japanese residents, called Pablo by men who traded with the Chinese vessels.

He looked up when Soliman's watchmen passed with a bundle of rope.

"They have been carrying rope all morning," he said.

"Boats need rope."

"So do guns."

"Have they asked you to help?"

"No."

He cut a thin strip from the reed and fitted it through the basket's rim.

"Some men say you have learned the foreigners' prayers," I said.

"I have heard them. I say some of them."

"Does that tell you what these ships will do?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "Does knowing the prayers of a trader tell you whether he will pay?"

"No."

"Then why ask?"

I had asked because I wanted one man who could tell me what the strangers meant. He had answered as a man whose life was here, among baskets and buyers, whether or not he shared a prayer with men beyond the bay.

"I am asking everyone too much today," I said.

He put down the knife. "The man whose basket this is has paid half. He will pay the rest when I finish. If fighting starts before he comes back, whose basket will it be?"

"Yours until he pays."

"That is what I told him. He says he has paid enough to call it his."

"Then I have no answer you will both like."

"I did not ask for one."

He went back to weaving. I left him with his knife and the unfinished rim.

***

By afternoon the men from the lighter had come to Lian's shed. They said they had kept the steering piece because the captain had not paid for carrying his jars. The captain's man said the ship had paid for a smaller load, and the lighter's crew had taken more than they were owed. The translator struggled to keep their numbers separate. Both sides called the same payment by different names.

They agreed on one thing: Lian had not agreed to store the wood.

The men lifted it from her shed and carried it to the edge of the path. The captain's man put a hand on it. One of the lighter's crew put his hand on the other end.

"Take it to a place both of you can see," I said.

They moved it to an open patch beside the water. No one let go.

The woman who owned that patch came out and asked what they were doing. It was where she laid out her nets. She would not have a quarrel placed there either.

The men stood with the wood between them. The captain's man began to laugh. It was not a pleasant sound. He had come to recover the means of steering his vessel and found that even a place to set it down belonged to someone.

The boat owner offered the floor of his own lighter, provided both sides would name two witnesses and agree that the piece could be taken only after they settled the charge. The captain's man objected to the second condition. He would not give the lighter's owner control over the ship's departure.

At last they agreed to leave the wood aboard the lighter for the rest of the daylight, with a man from each crew watching it. The captain's man said he would return with his captain's answer before dark. The lighter's owner said he would return with the men who had agreed the first price.

It was a delay, not a settlement. But Lian could put her mats back under cover.

Soliman came down from the gate as the men moved the wood. He watched the captain's man go toward the Chinese ships.

"Will they tell the strangers we are keeping their rudders?" he asked.

"They may. The captain says his piece was taken. The lighter's crew says they are owed. Neither has shown me the first agreement."

"The strangers may not care who made it."

"No."

He looked toward the bay, where the sails of the foreign ships were still hidden by the turn of the shore.

"We have sent a watchman to make a place for their messenger," he said. "I want him to come before night."

"And if he comes?"

"We will hear him."

"Will you let him see the guns?"

"He can see them from the river."

He was right. A gun could be concealed from a man at the gate only if he had never looked at the wall. We had built the palisade to be seen by boats that might threaten us.

A child ran past with a basket of fish. He called for his mother, who was waiting farther up the path. I watched her take the weight from his hands. She counted the fish before she paid the boy who had helped him carry it.

There were people who could still settle the price of a day's work. There were others who had spent the day trying to recover the means to leave.

From the lower gate came a shout. A small boat had come downriver from the houses above the bend and was waiting beyond the landing steps. A man stood in it with his hands visible. The boatman sat beside him.

Soliman went down to meet them. I followed more slowly.

The watchman called that the standing man was the one who had arrived from the foreign anchorage the day before. He had spent the night among his wife's people. Now he wanted to know whether a message from the strangers would be heard. He had asked for a place to land, and the men at the gate had agreed to hear him there. No one had yet agreed to what he would say.

I saw the standing man's face as the boat came closer. He looked at the houses before he looked at the gun. The boy who had counted fish that morning could have been his son, if years and voyages had taken him far enough away.

He called out in our language that he had come to ask who would receive the message.

The river carried his voice plainly. It could not tell us whose promise would follow it.

***

The man who had asked to carry the strangers' words stayed among his wife's people while we decided how to answer him.

His name was known at the lower gate by the second day. His brother, the interpreter aboard the foreign ship, was known to the traders who had sold provisions farther south. Knowing their names did not tell us what the men with guns would do if we asked them to wait.

Soliman sent for the first man's older kinsman, who had worked in the houses near the palisade. I saw the kinsman arrive with a cloth tied around his arm where a rope had cut him. He said he would go out to the ships with the younger man. He would tell the foreigners that Soliman was willing to hear an offer, and he would ask what they meant by friendship.

"Do not say the gates will be opened," Soliman told him.

"I will say you will speak."

"Do not say you can answer for every boat."

"I will say I cannot."

The kinsman looked at me. I had listened without speaking. He asked whether I would stand beside Soliman when the visitors came.

"If my knees take me there," I said.

"Then I will tell them there is an older man they should hear as well."

I wanted to object to being carried across the water as a promise before I had agreed to anything. But he had said only that I could be heard. I let him go.

Three days had passed since the foreign ships found their sheltered anchorage when the boat returned to them with our answer. We saw it from the river mouth. The larger vessel lay beyond the bar, its high sides bright in the sun. The small boat went alongside and disappeared under the shadow of its hull.

We expected the strangers to wait until the messenger had come back ahead of them. Instead their smaller craft began to move toward the river while the boat was still between us.

Soliman sent a runner to the lower gate. The runner was told to keep the landing clear of baskets and to tell the men with guns not to fire unless they saw an attack. I heard the order given. I did not hear every man repeat it.

When the first foreign boats passed the bar, the Chinese crews came out in their own skiffs. One captain's man carried a gift wrapped in cloth. Another pointed toward the shed where Lian had kept the steering piece. I could not hear what he told the strangers. Later I was told that he had accused men from Maynila of taking the ship's helm and goods without payment. I knew the complaint. I did not know what answer he had received.

The foreign commander came ashore with armed men.

He was shorter than I had expected from the height of his ship. He carried himself as though the men behind him were a part of his body. A man near his shoulder spoke to the interpreter. The interpreter spoke to Soliman. I watched their faces while they chose which words to let the others hear.

The commander said he wanted peace and friendship.

Soliman asked whether his men would remain on the shore while we spoke.

The interpreter carried the question. The commander said his men would stay where he had put them, so long as they were not attacked.

"Then your friendship begins with your men holding the landing," Soliman said.

I heard the interpreter give him a softer sentence. I could not tell whether the softening came from his own fear or from a word that had no exact place in the other tongue. I asked him to repeat it.

He did. Soliman looked at him sharply.

"That is closer," Soliman said.

The commander embraced me first, mistaking the older man for the one who had been named to him. He smelled of salt, sweat and a sharp oil I did not know. When he learned that Soliman was the younger man beside me, he embraced him too.

I did not know whether an embrace meant the same thing to him as to us. I knew he had kept his armed men within a short walk of the gate.

***

The following day the word tribute came into the market.

No one agreed who had first said it. A boat owner claimed he had heard it from a man selling water to the foreigners. A seller of wax said the word had come from the ships, where it meant a payment owed by those who accepted friendship. Another man said Soliman's enemies had spread it to make him refuse the visitors.

Soliman asked the interpreter to carry a question back: had the commander come to trade, or to demand a payment for leaving us alone?

The answer was that no tribute had yet been asked. The commander wanted to speak again and settle the terms.

He came inside the palisade with interpreters and fewer men than before. He looked at the gun by the gate, then at the houses. The men who had cleaned the gun stood with their hands visible. The commander followed us to a room where a mat had been spread and a cup set out.

There were several ways to make a promise. We chose one the men of Maynila would recognize. Blood was drawn and mixed with drink. The foreign commander did not refuse it. The interpreter said the agreement would make us friends.

I asked what the friendship required.

The commander spoke at length. The interpreter paused before giving us the answer. Those who came to settle with the strangers were to be supported and supplied, he said; there would be no further tribute beyond what had been agreed. Soliman asked what would happen if we had food for our own people and none for visitors. The commander answered that friends did not let friends starve.

I knew that sentence. I had heard men say it beside a boat when they wanted rice without naming a price.

"Tell him," I said, "that a household can share food it has. It cannot promise food it has not grown."

The interpreter gave the words. The commander said we would settle such matters when they arose. He held out his hand as if the answer had closed the question.

We drank. The men around us watched. I knew that something had been agreed. I did not know how far the commander's words would reach when his men were hungry.

When he left, Soliman stayed in the room and looked at the empty cup.

"You made him answer about food," he said.

"He did not answer."

"He answered enough for today."

I wanted to tell him that today was not what troubled me. He had spent two days trying to keep a ship from bringing its guns beneath our wall. I had spent them asking whether the man aboard it would pay for rice. Neither of us had been able to remove the guns.

Outside, the Chinese captain's man was asking again for his steering piece. The lighter's owner had come with witnesses and another account of the charge. Their dispute had survived our compact without changing its shape.

***

The foreign men slept near the water. Some of ours went down to look at their weapons. Some of theirs looked at the guns above the gate. The watchers on both sides grew tired.

A man came to Soliman with news that boats from the other bank might be gathering. He said they meant to strike the foreign vessels. Another man said the boats were only moving cargo before the weather changed. Soliman asked who had seen them. Both men had heard the story from others.

The commander heard the first version. He sent word that he would fight any man who came against him. Soliman sent back that he had not agreed to help anyone attack the strangers. The interpreter carried the answer. Whether the commander believed it was another matter.

By evening there was talk of firing the guns in celebration of the new friendship. I did not know who had first proposed it. Men who had never stood beside a gun were saying it would prove that no one feared the foreigners. Men who had served the guns said it would be a foolish way to prove anything.

I went to the upper gate and found the gunner testing a length of cord.

"Will you fire?" I asked.

"I have been told to be ready."

"Ready for a display?"

"Ready for what comes."

"Who gave the order?"

He named a man who had gone to the lower landing. I went to find him. By the time I reached the steps, he had been sent to the other side of the wall.

The tide was rising. The foreign vessels shifted on their anchors. Their smaller craft moved close enough that I could see the men watching us. I thought of the men who had gone out in a watchboat and asked whether they would be paid for another journey. Their question had been answered with a promise to speak later. The strangers had not yet asked who would pay the men now standing in the rain beside our guns.

That night everyone seemed to be waiting for another man to move first.

In the morning a small foreign boat overturned near the bank. Men shouted and pointed toward the water. I saw one man dragged up by his companions. I could not tell from where I stood whether the others had reached shore.

Later there were sails farther out in the bay. A foreign boat went to see them, then turned back when a gun sounded from the larger vessel. I heard the report and saw a white patch of smoke above the ship. I did not see what signal the gunner had been ordered to give.

A second report came from the river.

Then another.

I was inside the gate when the third sound struck the air. Men ran toward the wall. Someone shouted that the foreigners had fired. Someone else shouted that a shot from our gun had hit their ship. I saw smoke drifting from the upper bank and heard the foreign vessel answer. I could not make a history of the first shot from the echoes.

Soliman passed me with two men carrying powder. He told them to keep the path clear for the gun crew.

"Where are you going?" I asked.

"To the wall."

"Do you know who fired?"

"I know they are firing now."

He went on.

***

The first foreign men reached the palisade while I was still climbing toward the gun.

A man with a burning match fell from the platform. I saw him strike the earth below and try to rise. A foreign soldier crossed the space where he lay. I could not see whether the soldier struck him or stepped over him. Another man hauled a gunner down from the wall by his arm. The gunner's feet beat against the wood before he fell.

I turned toward the houses.

The people nearest the gate had begun to run. A woman came past carrying a pot that spilled grain onto the path. She stopped to gather it. Someone pulled her up by the elbow and made her leave it. A boy ran after them with an empty basket, shouting that he had lost his sister.

The first flame I saw was beneath the eave of a storehouse near the wall. A foreign soldier had gone toward it with a lighted brand. I saw him lift his arm. Then the thatch caught. I could not see whether another fire had begun elsewhere. I knew only what was burning in front of me.

I tried to reach the boy who had lost his sister. He was already gone. The smoke turned the path between the houses into a narrow place where people pushed past one another without seeing who they struck.

Lian was at her shed when I came to it. She had put her mats into a bundle and was trying to lift the boy who had stood beside the steering piece. He would not move. His eyes were fixed on the flames above the wall.

"Take his hand," I said.

"I have his hand."

"Then leave the mats."

She looked at the bundle. It was her work and the dry place she had kept for it. She had spent the previous day making strangers remove a piece of wood from her shed. Now the roof of that shed was beginning to smoke.

She let the bundle fall.

The boy took one step. Then another. We went behind the houses toward a path that led away from the river. A man ran past with a cut on his face, carrying a child whose head lay against his shoulder. I could not tell whether the child was asleep.

At the turn in the path Lian stopped. She asked whether I had seen her husband.

"No."

"Was he on the Chinese ship?"

"I do not know."

"Will they let him come back?"

"I do not know."

She looked at me as if I had withheld an answer she could use. I had none. She took the boy and went toward the trees.

A roof collapsed behind us. I heard the crack of timber, then a rush of air as fire found a new way through the houses.

I wanted to go back for the people who had not reached the path. My knees had begun to shake. A younger man caught me by the shoulder and turned me toward the slope. I pulled away, then stumbled. He held me upright until I could walk.

At the top I looked back.

The town had been built and rebuilt in my lifetime. Its posts had changed, its steps had been replaced, its boats had come and gone. I had believed that if a man knew what belonged to whom, he could keep an argument from becoming a theft. The foreign soldiers were moving through the houses with fire, and no one was asking who owned the roofs.

I had heard men accuse one another of the first shot. I had seen a soldier carry a brand to a storehouse. Those were different kinds of knowledge. I could not make one cancel the other.

The river was full of boats trying to leave. I could see no place for all of them to land.

***

The first man to ask for my name that year held a board against his knee.

He was not the first foreigner to ask it. Men had called from boats after the burning, and men had come ashore to learn which houses might sell rice. They asked whom they should pay and who could keep a promise for the next tide. This one asked how my name should be put into the writing he carried.

"Raha Ache," I told the interpreter.

The man with the board looked up. "The older one?"

"There are men older than I am."

The interpreter waited for me to answer the question as it had been asked. I could hear Soliman speaking with someone beyond the doorway. The foreigner's clerk had heard of him already. I said, "If you need to distinguish us, write that I am old."

He dipped his pen. I could not see the letters from where I sat. He said my name back to the interpreter, who said it to me, and for a moment I was required to recognize myself in three different mouths.

It was the year after the fire. Some houses had been rebuilt on their old posts. Others had new owners, or no owners anyone could find. The palisade had been mended in places and left open in others. A merchant who once brought a load directly to the river gate now sent a boy to ask which path was clear. The boy was paid for asking, whether or not the answer was useful.

The foreigners had returned with more vessels and with a commander whose name people pronounced in several ways. The ships were different enough from the ones that had burned the town that a child might have believed they belonged to another people. No one who had carried a roof beam out of the fire believed that.

A woman stood at the edge of the room holding two thin strips of wood. She had come before the clerk. She wanted to know whether the house she had occupied since the burning would be counted as hers if a foreign soldier took shelter in it.

"Did you own the house before?" the interpreter asked her.

"The house before was burned. I helped build this one."

"On whose ground?"

"On my sister's."

"Is your sister here?"

"She went to the upper river after her child died. I do not know when she will return."

The clerk asked the interpreter to move her away from the table. He said the commander was waiting for names, not disputes about houses.

"Then what will the names decide?" she asked.

He did not answer her. The interpreter looked at me as if the question had been mine.

"Write her name on her strips," I said. "She has brought them to be heard."

The woman put the wood down, one piece above the other. On the upper strip she had cut the marks her sister used for goods left in her care. On the lower she had cut a mark for the timber she herself had bought.

The clerk would not touch either strip. He was copying names onto paper for men who were not in the room. The woman's sister could not be summoned by writing, and neither could a roof be made secure by a clerk who had not seen it. I asked a neighbor to witness what she had told us. The neighbor said she would speak only for the timber she had seen carried. The woman took back both strips. She had not received an answer about the soldiers.

Outside, a man was measuring the doorway of a storehouse with a cord. He had been told to find a place where the foreigners could put provisions. The storehouse belonged to three households. Only one had been asked.

"Tell them all three must hear the request," I said.

He drew the cord through his fingers. "They want the space before the boats unload."

"Then they should have come before the boats."

He looked toward the river. I knew how little use that answer would be to a man carrying orders.

***

Lakandula arrived after the clerk had put away his board.

He had come from Tondo with two companions, neither of whom wore the marks of a messenger sent by me. He did not ask my leave to enter. He had been invited to speak about Tondo's landings, and he came because the question concerned them.

"The foreigners have asked who can answer for the boats at our shore," he said.

"What did you tell them?"

"That the owners can answer for their boats. I can speak for the people who have asked me to speak."

"Will they accept that?"

"They said they understood."

He took a seat on the mat. His companion remained near the door, watching the road.

"Do you believe they understood?" I asked.

"I believe the man who translated it understood. I do not know what the man with the writing will put down."

I told him about the woman's two strips. Lakandula had heard of other claims. A Tondo family wanted to recover a boat held after an argument about cargo. Another had been asked for men to carry provisions to the foreigners' vessels. The men who carried would need wages; the men who requested them spoke as if labor followed automatically from the word peace.

"I can tell my people what I heard," Lakandula said. "I cannot make their hands free."

"Will you refuse to meet the commander?"

"No. A refusal will not keep his men from the landing."

He did not ask whether I would meet him. The town had changed too much for either of us to pretend that one man's answer could hold the other shore.

Soliman entered while we were still talking. He looked first at Lakandula and then at the interpreter's empty place.

"They are asking for words we gave last year," he said. "They say the compact was broken by us. They want us to say we accept their friendship now."

"Do you?" Lakandula asked.

"I want them to stop taking what has not been offered."

"That is a demand," Lakandula said. "Will they hear it as a condition?"

Soliman knelt beside the doorway, leaving space between himself and us. His hand was bandaged where a splinter had worked into the skin. I had seen him help pull a burned post out of a path the week before. He had refused to leave the work to younger men.

"If we say yes," he said, "they will tell their people we are friends. If their people take another house, will we be permitted to say the friendship was broken?"

"Ask them," I said.

"I have asked. They say their commander will hear complaints."

"From whom?"

"From the men he has named to speak."

Lakandula rubbed his thumb along the edge of his mat. "Then we must decide what those men may promise before anyone puts our names beside theirs."

There was no agreement among the three of us. I could say that Maynila's people needed a season without burning. Soliman could say that he would not bless a taking he could not prevent. Lakandula could speak of the Tondo boats and houses whose owners had trusted him to be present. None of those statements made the others disappear.

The interpreter came back to say the commander would receive us before the tide turned. He had been instructed to bring the three names the clerk had written.

"Did he write what we said?" Lakandula asked.

"He wrote that you would come."

"That much is true," Lakandula said.

***

The foreign commander sat beneath a roof that had belonged to a trader before the fire. The trader's surviving nephew had given permission for the meeting. He had not given the building away. I saw him outside beside the water jar, counting the men who entered. One of the foreigners had put his helmet on the trader's bench. The nephew waited until the man lifted it before he took his own seat.

The commander spoke through an interpreter. He offered friendship and protection under his king. He said there would be peace for those who accepted it.

I asked what would happen when a soldier damaged a house.

The commander said the matter could be brought before him.

"By the owner?" I asked.

"By the proper person," the interpreter answered.

"Who chooses that person?"

The interpreter spoke to the commander again. Their answer was longer than the question. The commander had officers, he said, and they would learn who the important men were. Complaints should come through them.

I thought of the woman with the two strips. I could tell her to find an officer. I could not tell her that the officer would know her sister's mark, or care who had carried the timber.

Lakandula asked about the boats that came to Tondo. The commander said trade would be protected. Lakandula asked whether a boat held for provisions would be returned when its owner demanded it. The interpreter asked for the question to be repeated. When the answer came, it was about the needs of the king's service.

Soliman listened without lowering his eyes. At last he asked whether friendship required us to say that the town had burned because we had wished it.

The commander answered that the past had shown the danger of refusing peace. He did not say who had set the fire.

"Then write that we have heard you," Soliman said.

The clerk moved his pen.

"Do not write that I have agreed to what I have not heard explained."

The interpreter spoke quickly. The commander watched him, then us. I could not know whether the commander's patience was wearing thin or whether the interpreter had made Soliman's words harsher. The man with the pen stopped writing.

I said we would bring the terms back to those whose boats, houses, and labor they concerned. The commander said he expected an answer. He did not say the ships would leave while we sought one.

When we came outside, the trader's nephew was still counting. He told me one of the soldiers had taken water without asking. The nephew had asked him to pay. The soldier had laughed, then given him a coin.

"Was it enough?" I asked.

"For the water," he said. "Not for having to ask."

I wanted to tell him the meeting had secured his right to complain. It had secured no such thing. The commander had received our names. The people who lived beneath those names still had to keep their doors, their goods, and their hands.

At the river the tide had begun to fall. Lakandula's boat waited apart from ours. He stepped into it without asking Soliman or me to accompany him. Soliman watched the boat clear the shallows.

"He will make his own answer," he said.

"Yes."

"And ours?"

I looked at the marks a rope had worn into the landing post. A man had tied a boat there that morning. He would come back for it, or someone else would cut the rope.

"We have to know what they will call an answer," I said. "Then we have to decide what we can bear to give."

Soliman went up toward the houses. I remained by the post until the boat from Tondo had passed the bend.

***

The first account of the boats came from a man who had lost his paddle.

He walked up from the lower landing with both hands empty. His clothes were stiff with salt, and the skin over one cheek had been rubbed raw. A boy who knew him brought water. The man drank too quickly, coughed, and put the cup down.

"Where is your boat?" Soliman asked.

"Beyond the channel. I left it when the other men went over."

"Who went over?"

The man named two rowers. He could not say whether they had reached shore. He had seen one climb onto an overturned hull and had heard the other call from behind a boat. Then smoke had hidden them.

He said fighting had broken out where the vessels from the northern shore met the foreigners' boats. Men from communities beyond Maynila had come by water. Their leaders had not asked us to command them. They had come with reasons of their own, and those reasons had been repeated differently by every messenger who passed the river gate.

"Who led them?" I asked.

The man gave a name I did not know well. The boy who had brought water said he had heard another name at the fish market. A third man, standing in the doorway, said the leader was from the Pampanga shore and that no one here knew what he called himself.

"Did you see him?" I asked the first man.

"I saw a boat with a high prow. The men around it shouted for him. I could not hear what they called."

"Then tell us about the boat."

He looked ashamed, as though the boat were too little to bring back from a battle. I asked how its men had moved and where he had last seen it. He drew the channel with his finger in the dust. The line stopped at a patch where the water deepened.

"I saw a man fall there," he said. "I cannot say he was the leader."

Soliman knelt beside the drawing. He asked whether the man had seen Spanish boats close the passage to the shallows. The rower said they had come across the channel with the current behind them. He did not know whether they had planned to close it or had been carried there.

The boy asked if the northern boats had won.

"No," the rower said.

He did not add that everyone had been lost. He had seen too little for that. He wanted someone to find his two companions.

Soliman sent the boy to ask among the boat owners who had returned. He told him to bring names, not a count. A count would change whenever another vessel came in.

***

A woman from the upper landing arrived with a strip of wet cloth tied around her wrist. She had come to ask about her husband, who had taken a canoe toward the fighting without telling her he meant to join it. He had said he would carry water to men waiting near the channel. She had believed him. Now she did not know whether to be angry with him or frightened for him.

I recognized the cloth. It was the sort woven for covering jars. Its edge had been repaired with a thread of another color.

"Did he take a weapon?" I asked.

"He took the pole from our canoe. It has an iron point for pushing free of roots."

"Who was with him?"

"My brother's son. The boy came back without him."

"Where is the boy?"

"At home. He says he did not see the fighting."

"Then I will not ask him to say that he did."

She pulled the wet cloth from her wrist and laid it on the mat. She had tied it there to remember which canoe to ask for: the one with a patch of this cloth around the stern peg. The patch was gone when the boy returned. The canoe might have struck a bank, or another man might have taken it. She had no way to tell.

I promised to ask the people who came back. She looked at me for a long moment.

"Ask," she said. "Do not tell me you will find him."

When she left, Soliman said that more boats were coming down the river. I went with him to the landing.

Three arrived before sunset. One carried a wounded man whose breathing made a small wet sound. His companions asked for a place to put him. A householder opened her shed and told them to lay him on the clean mats. She said she could spare the room for the night, but someone else must bring water and food.

The men looked to Soliman. He turned to the boy who had carried the first cup.

"Can you find his kin?"

"If someone tells me where they live."

"Then ask the men who brought him. Do not leave him here because we are still speaking."

The boy went. I helped lift the wounded man. His weight was lighter than I expected. He had lost one sandal and kept the other tied to his foot with a strip of bark. When we laid him down, the householder cut the bark to remove it. She put both pieces beside his mat in case his kin wanted them.

No one in the shed spoke of victory.

***

Lakandula's messenger came the following morning.

He had been delayed at a crossing where a damaged boat blocked the passage. He had helped its owner move it aside and had been given a place on another vessel. He brought word that Tondo's households were making their own arrangements for the wounded and missing. Lakandula would not promise men or boats for any new fighting without hearing the owners.

"Does he know who died in the channel?" Soliman asked.

"He knows some names. I have brought those I heard from the families themselves."

The messenger unfolded a small cloth with marks cut into thin slips of wood. The names were not all from the same shore. Some had been written by men who could not tell whether a person was dead or merely missing. The messenger had kept those slips separate.

We read what we could. I knew one of the men by the goods he used to bring to Maynila, not by his father's name. The messenger had never met him. I could not improve the account by claiming we had.

"Will Lakandula come?" I asked.

"When the boats can pass and he has finished speaking with his people."

"Tell him we have heard his message."

The messenger nodded. He did not ask me to speak for Tondo in return.

A foreign officer came later with two soldiers and a local interpreter. He asked who had helped the vessels that fought his men. The interpreter used a word that might have meant supplied, carried, or joined. Soliman asked which of those acts the officer wanted named.

"Those who made war against the king," the officer answered.

A householder had given a wounded rower water. A boat owner had taken frightened children from a crowded shore. A man had carried rice to a cousin before he knew the cousin intended to fight. The officer's question could place all three beside the men with weapons.

"I can tell you whom I saw here," Soliman said. "I cannot name every man who used the channel."

The officer asked again. His interpreter said the commander wanted obedience to the peace already offered.

I remembered the clerk's board. I remembered the woman whose timber could not be secured by a name. I asked the officer whether giving water to a wounded man was an act of war.

The officer looked at the interpreter before he answered. He said those who had fought must be identified.

"That was not my question."

He told us to come to the commander if we had complaints. The soldiers went away without receiving a list.

I did not mistake their leaving for agreement. They could return with more men. The commander had ships and weapons that made his questions difficult to refuse even when the words were clear.

Soliman watched them pass the gate. "If they ask again, what will you tell them?"

"Whom I saw," I said. "And what I saw them do."

"They will say that is too little."

"It may be."

He touched the bandage on his hand. The splinter had been removed, but the wound had opened again when he lifted a beam. He had begun to learn how a small injury could last longer than a fight.

***

That evening I went to the lower landing with the woman who had been looking for her husband.

A boy had found a canoe caught among the reeds beyond the bend. Its stern peg was bare. Its pole was missing. The woman walked around it twice, examining the cuts in the wood. At last she touched a shallow notch near the bow.

"My brother made that," she said.

"Is it yours?"

"Yes."

"Do you know who brought it here?"

"No."

The boy said he had found it empty. He had pulled it free with another child. Neither had seen a man nearby.

The woman knelt and felt beneath the seat. Her hand came back with a small bundle of fishing line. She held it against her palm. Her husband had wound the line that way, she said, but other men wound it the same. She did not call the line proof that he had lived or died.

"Can you help me take it home?" she asked.

I called for two carriers who were waiting for work at the path. They came and examined the canoe. One said it could be floated at the next tide. The other wanted to turn it over and check for cracks first. The woman chose to wait while they looked. She asked their price before either man lifted it.

I had once thought that knowing who owned a boat could settle most quarrels around it. The woman knew the canoe was hers. She did not know where its other owner had gone. The two carriers could bring back the wood. They could not bring back an answer.

We agreed on a price for the work. I paid part because I had asked them to come. She paid the rest from the small purse she carried. They did not refuse her money because I stood beside her. She would decide when the canoe went home.

By the time we finished, the water had begun to rise. Across the river, foreign men were setting posts near the place where boats came ashore. I could see them measure the spaces between the posts. I could not hear whose ground they said they stood on.

A child on the path asked whether the men would leave when the ships left.

"No one has told me that they will," I said.

"Will our boats still come here?"

"Some will. Their owners will have to decide where they can land."

The child looked toward the canoe we had recovered. "That one came back."

"Yes."

He waited for me to say that its owner would come back too. I could not.

The town would have to live with the foreigners' posts and the words their officers wrote. We could still ask who had agreed to a day's work, who owned a path, who was missing, and who had been seen returning. Some answers would be refused. Others would cost more than we had to give. They were the questions I could carry to the next morning.

The woman stood beside her canoe as the tide touched its hull. She put a hand on the bow and kept it there until the carriers were ready.

<summary>Historical Notes &amp; Sources</summary>
<p><strong>The 1570 expedition and the fire.</strong> The anonymous Spanish <a href="/raha-ache/sources/relacion-voyage-luzon-1570/"><em>Relation of the Voyage to Luzon</em></a> (in Blair and Robertson, <em>The Philippine Islands</em>, vol. 3, esp. printed pp. 72–83; <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html">accessible English edition</a>) reports a tide-sensitive approach, a three-day wait after Mehomete's brother went ashore, negotiations, fighting and the Spanish burning of Maynila. Its allegations about who initiated violence, Chinese crews' rudders and cargo, and the motives of local leaders are conquest-party testimony, not independent findings. Mehomete's and Ache's private thoughts, the individual families, and the dialogue in this chapter are fictional.</p>
<p><strong>People and political names.</strong> The 1570 account describes an unnamed older ruler as uncle of the younger Soliman; identifying that man with Ache the Old is an inference from the separate <a href="/raha-ache/sources/riquel-1571/">1571 Riquel record</a> naming Raha Ache the Old and Raha Solimane the Young as Manila principales and Sibunao Lacandola as a Tondo principal. No father-son relationship, unified kingdom or command over Tondo is established by these sources. The four Chinese ships, married Chinese households, Japanese residents and the Christian Pablo are reported from the Spanish witness's perspective; the precise numbers and Pablo's alleged fighting are not independently confirmed.</p>
<p><strong>1571 return and fighting.</strong> The arrival of Legazpi's forces and resistance at <a href="/raha-ache/events/bangkusay-1571/">Bangkusay</a> belong to 1571. The reported resistance leader's identification with Bambalito, Tarik Sulayman or the Maynila Sulayman is disputed; this chapter intentionally does not resolve it. Particular missing rowers, household claims, conversations and labor arrangements are fictional constructions. The narrative does not assert that Ache personally witnessed the fighting.</p>

The baptism, succession questions and death associated with Ache in 1572 belong to Bonus Chapter 12 and are not anticipated as settled facts by characters here.</p>
