The first jar knocked against the second before we had lifted the basket.

Amaya heard the sound and put her hand on the rim. I had set the jars too close together. Their shoulders touched where the reed packing thinned, and a dark line of old glaze showed through the gap.

"Take them out," she said.

"We have not moved."

"That is why we can still take them out."

The house above the bend belonged to a woman who had kept the jars under her eaves while Amaya repaired a cover for her. The woman had already brought the cover home. Amaya had come to collect the empty vessels she had lent her for the work. They were not heavy, but the neck of one was narrower than the other, and the basket had been woven for a different load.

I lifted them out. Amaya laid a handful of dry reeds across the bottom, bent them into two rings, and made me set each jar inside one. She pressed another bundle between their shoulders.

"Now move the basket without lifting it."

I drew it a hand's breadth across the mat. The jars stayed apart.

"Again."

I moved it farther. One jar leaned. I stopped before it struck the other and pushed the reeds back into place.

"That is what you will do when the path turns," she said. "Stop. Do not wait for the sound."

The woman of the house watched from the doorway. She had a little boy beside her who kept trying to touch the basket. She took his hand away each time he reached for it.

"Will you send the jars back when you need them?" she asked Amaya.

"If I can spare them. I have been using smaller ones since the cloth went missing."

She did not look at me. The woman knew whose hands had lost the cloth. I bent over the basket and tightened the binding around its handles.

On the way home Amaya walked behind me. The path was dry at first. Where it dipped beneath the trees, water had gathered around a root. I stepped over it and felt the basket pull against my wrist. I stopped, put it down on firm ground, and checked the reeds. Amaya waited without offering to carry the weight.

"You could have crossed there in one stride," she said.

"I could have broken the jars."

"Then you have learned something from carrying cloth."

I wanted to ask whether that was praise. She was already looking at the next turn. I picked up the basket again.

At her house we set the jars beside the wall. She tested both rims and ran a finger along their sides. There was no new crack. She took the reeds out and kept the pieces that had not been crushed.

"Those will serve again," she said.

I stood with the empty basket in my hands. The rice she had taken from my carrying work was in her store jar. It was enough for a meal if she chose to cook it, and nowhere near the price of the cloth I owed.

"Will you ask Tesa for more work tomorrow?" she asked.

"If she has any."

"Ask before you promise Raya your hands."

"Raya may need me to lift the new timber."

"Has Madan given her timber?"

"Not yet."

"Then do not miss work for a piece of wood no one has sold you."

I set the basket down. She took the old cloth measure from a shelf and unfolded it. The square patch was still darker than the rest.

"When I have enough to buy another," I said, "will you come and choose it?"

"Yes. If you tell me what you have earned before you call it enough."

She folded the measure again. I had asked her to trust a future price. She had answered with the work I could do tomorrow.

***

At the next market the river was crowded before the sun cleared the trees.

Two boats had passed our turning without slowing. A third waited at the bend, its owner standing in the stern while a carrier on shore called that the farther landing was full. The owner held his boat against the current and shouted back. I could not hear all of it. I heard the word for waiting more than once.

I went down to the market path, where Marun was carrying an empty pole. He had been paid to bring baskets to the farther landing and had come back without them.

"Did they turn you away?" I asked.

"No. The owner who hired me knew there was room when we left. Another boat came before us and took the dry patch. The man there said he would clear it when the first load was gone."

"How long did you wait?"

"Long enough for the buyer to send a boy asking where his fish had gone."

"Then their promise was no better than ours."

Marun shifted the pole to his other shoulder.

"The man told the owner the place was full. He did not tell him it was safe when it was not. The owner could choose to wait or go farther. He chose to wait. He paid me for the time."

"Would you come here next time?"

"If the boat owner hires me and there is a place to put the load. Yesterday I carried for a woman whose buyer lives above the other path. This bank would have made me walk twice as far."

He went on before I could ask another question. I had heard him say the same thing when the first two boats passed our landing. This time I knew what a loaded basket could do to a wrist. I had carried one uphill for Sela and another home for Amaya. The shorter river still did not make every journey shorter.

The cord owner was standing beneath a palm with a group of buyers. His line hung over his shoulder, three knots pulled close together. He had brought them to look at the other channel, just as he said he would. One man carried a jar covered with cloth. Another held a sample of woven mat. A third had come without goods and was asking about a boat he expected after midday.

The cord owner saw me and did not call me over. He was explaining where the farther landing's dry patch began and how its keeper would warn a boat when the space was taken. I waited until he finished.

"Marun says it filled before one boat arrived," I said.

"It did."

"Then what do you tell these owners?"

"That it fills. They may send a man ahead if the load cannot wait. They may hire the other path. They may stay here and hope a place opens. I am telling them what I saw this morning."

The man with the covered jar looked toward our landing.

"Is the step there open?"

"No," I said. "Raya has not repaired it."

"Then there is no choice between the two."

"There is a choice between waiting here and going elsewhere," the cord owner said. "But I will not bring your jars to a rotten step. Ache has told you the same."

The man with the mat sample asked whether a boat could unload at the old stones when the water fell.

"A person crossed there once with a small sample," I said. "That was under Raya's eye and with Dala's permission for the path. It did not open the stones to a loaded boat."

He turned back to the cord owner.

"Then I will ask the man at the farther landing. I need a dry place, not a story about one journey."

The cord owner led them away. He had not hidden our answer. He had used it to show why his own offer was worth hearing.

I could have followed and told them about the timber Madan might show Raya. I had no price for it, no crew and no day when it would be set. The buyers would have heard another promise without a place to put their goods.

***

When I returned to the hall, the keeper was hearing a complaint from a woman whose boat had waited at the farther landing. The woman had paid a carrier to hold a basket beneath a tree while the first boat unloaded. The basket's binding had loosened, and a small jar had slipped against a root.

"Did it break?" the keeper asked.

"No. But I paid the carrier for the waiting, and I had to buy a new binding. The man who said there was room told us the first boat would leave soon. He did not know its owner had gone to find the buyer."

"Who said there was room?"

"The man at the farther landing."

"Did he promise a time?"

"He said the first boat was unloading."

"Then he told you what he saw. You wanted him to tell you how long the owner would be away."

The woman frowned.

"Do you keep marks for the other landing too?"

"I keep what people bring here. If you want that man to answer, you must ask him. I did not hear what he said."

I stood beside the doorway. The keeper had no reason to defend the farther landing. She was asking the same questions she asked about ours. The woman wanted someone to pay for her waiting, and there might be someone who should. The keeper would not invent that person because the basket had nearly broken.

After the woman left, I told the keeper about the boats passing our turning.

"Will they all go there now?" I asked.

"Who are all?"

"The men the cord owner has brought."

"He brought three that you saw. He did not bring the woman who came here with her complaint. She chose that landing before she spoke to us."

"Could we ask the owners to return when Raya finishes?"

"We could ask. They could refuse."

"Then what is left for me to do?"

She pointed to the strip for the disputed watcher charge.

"Salin came from Naru's household to ask about oil. He did not come to join a war between landings. If you speak to Naru, tell him what you know. Do not make him choose a side because you dislike where another man's boat has gone."

I looked at the strip. It was one of several, and the keeper had left room between them. She had not cut a mark for every trader who passed the bend. The river was full of boats whose owners had never asked her anything.

Outside, Amaya called to me from the path. She had brought an empty basket and was going toward Tesa's awning. I followed her.

"Tesa may have work today," she said. "I will ask with you."

"Raya may need me."

"Then ask Raya when she needs you. You cannot carry cloth and timber at the same time."

We passed the bend where the third boat was still waiting. The owner had decided not to follow the other two. His pole-handler stood in the stern, watching the water. No one had forced him to stay. No one had promised him the waiting would be short.

At the awning Tesa was winding a long length of thread. She looked at my hands, then at Amaya's empty basket.

"I have bundles to carry after the cloth is dry," she said. "If you want the work, come when the shade reaches that post. I will not call for you."

I looked at the post. The shade had a long way to travel. Raya might ask me to help lift a piece of wood before then. Madan might arrive with it. The boat at the bend might leave.

"I will come," I said.

Tesa looked at Amaya.

"Did he bring you the rice?"

"He did."

"Then he knows how to finish one job. We will see whether he can finish another."

Amaya set her empty basket beside the mat. She had not come to speak for me. She had come to see whether the work existed before I promised it. I watched the shade move a finger's breadth along the ground and began to count the time until I had to return.

***

The first man to put his shoulder beneath the roof beam asked Madan where he wanted it laid.

Madan pointed to the dry side of the house above the bend. The woman waiting there shook her head before the men moved.

"Not against that wall. The rain comes through it. Put it under the eave until we can raise it."

One of the men turned toward her. The other held still with his end lifted. I had arrived with the cord Madan's niece had lent me to carry, and stood out of their way while the woman showed them the patch she had cleared. She had not asked for a landing step. She had asked Madan to come and see her roof before he sold the long timber I wanted.

The men lowered the beam onto two short supports. Madan tested the end with his knife and asked the woman to look at it.

"This one is yours if you want it. The piece I brought yesterday was too narrow."

She knelt and pressed the grain where he had pared it. "What about the part that lies over the doorway?"

"We can leave it longer until we set the other end. I will not cut it twice."

She looked at the two men. "Will you be here when it is raised?"

The nearer man answered before Madan could. "If he hires us for that work. We were paid to bring it here."

Madan said, "I will come when the other support is ready. I have not hired them to raise it."

The woman nodded, though she did not look pleased. She gave Madan the agreed part of the price in grain and kept back the rest until the beam was fitted. His niece counted the measure with her, then retied the cord around the container. Neither pretended the work was finished.

Only when we were outside did I ask about the other length.

"Now you can show it to Raya?"

"I can show her what remains unpromised," Madan said. "She can tell me whether it is worth bringing down."

His niece handed the grain to one of the carriers. "And they can decide whether they have another journey in them."

The man rubbed his shoulder. "We will walk to the yard. We will not carry a second tree before we have eaten."

I had brought no food. Madan had brought a small parcel wrapped in leaf. He gave it to the men, who sat beneath the house and opened it without waiting for us. His niece watched me watch them.

"You offered to carry wood when you did not know which piece it was," she said.

"I have carried heavier things."

"Then you know why they eat before they lift another."

***

The long timber lay behind the yard on low supports, its bark stripped in places. I remembered it as a straight length because I had seen only the top face. The underside had a swollen knot near one end. Madan rolled it with his foot and the knot came into view.

His niece uncoiled the measuring cord. She had tied a small twist at the distance I had brought from Raya. The cord reached past the knot, but only if both ends of the timber were sound.

Madan struck the knot with the back of his knife. It answered with a duller sound than the clear wood beside it. He cut a narrow shaving from the swelling. The grain beneath was dark.

"Can you cut it off?" I asked.

"I can cut off anything you point to. What would you set the other end upon?"

I did not answer. I knew where the bank had gone soft, and that Dala's path lay above it. The old step had failed because a length that looked sound from the dry side had rotted where water held it.

"Bring Raya," his niece said. "Do not bring the men to the river to hear her say no."

Raya came after we found her at the landing. She had mud to the ankle and a narrow piece of old wood in her hand. She left it on the bank rather than carry it through the market.

At the yard she made Madan turn the timber again. She scraped the dark place with her knife, then moved her hand along the length, pressing at the pale scars where branches had been removed.

"This end will not take the bearing," she said.

Madan nodded. "I thought as much."

"The other end may. If you cut here, what length remains?"

His niece laid the cord along it. The twist fell just short of the cut Raya had marked.

I could see the answer before she said it. "Too short."

"For the place you measured yesterday," Raya said. "I can move the upper bearing a little lower if the stone beneath it is sound. I have not tested that stone since the water rose."

"Could we move it up?" I asked.

"Up is Dala's path. I told you that."

Madan looked from her to me. "If the lower stone is sound, will you buy it?"

Raya took her hand off the wood. "I am not buying it. I am telling you what it may hold. The people who want the step must find its price and the hands to set it. Then I will inspect it again."

His niece retied the cord at the new mark but left the old twist in place. I asked why.

"Because yesterday's distance was real when you measured it. If the stone fails, we need to know why this shorter piece was never enough."

She set the cord aside. The two carriers had finished eating and returned to the yard. Madan told them what Raya had found.

"One more journey, if the piece is bought," he said. "Down the market path, then to the bank."

The older man shook his head. "Not down that slope with the bark wet. It will turn on us."

"Then where?"

"Past the cooking sheds. The ground is longer but firmer. We need another pair of hands at the bend."

"I can help," I said.

"Are you free when we go?"

I thought of Tesa's shade post. I could still see where the shadow had stood when I left her awning. It would reach the mark she named before the afternoon was gone.

"Not after the shade reaches the post," I said.

"Then you are not the hand I need," he answered.

Madan looked annoyed. His niece did not.

"How much for the carrying?" she asked the men.

They named a measure of grain for each of them, a smaller measure for the third hand, and food before the work. Madan repeated the amounts so no one could later say he had heard a single price for all three. The older carrier added that they would not be paid only if the timber reached the bank. They would be paid for the journey if Raya turned it away after they had carried it.

"That is fair," Raya said. "I may still turn it away."

Madan said he would sell the sound length for a separate price. He named it. I could not pay the timber price, much less the carriers. The men went to put away their ropes. No one held them back.

***

Sela found us beside the yard gate. She had come with two empty fish baskets, one hanging from either end of a pole. She stopped when she saw Raya and asked whether the step was open.

"No," Raya said.

Sela lowered the pole. "I have a buyer who will take one basket on the next market morning if I can tell him where to meet it. The farther channel is crowded by then. Can this wood be ready?"

Raya showed her the dark knot and the two marks on the cord. "I must test the lower stone before anyone cuts. Then the timber must be paid for, carried, cut and set. It may still be wrong."

Sela bent to look at the damaged end. She had lost two sales already and did not want another lesson in what water could spoil.

"What would my share buy?" she asked Madan.

He told her the price of the wood. His niece told her the carriers' terms. Raya said what she would inspect and what she would not promise. It took longer than Sela wanted.

"I can give one measure of grain toward carrying," Sela said. "For a landing I may use for my own fish, if Dala agrees where the baskets stand. I will not pay the whole price and then ask leave from someone whose ground I have not spoken to."

"Then speak to Dala," Raya said.

"I will. But if the step fails, does the grain return to me?"

The older carrier had not gone far. He came back to the gate.

"If I have carried the timber to the bank, my back will not be returned to me. You pay for that work whether the stone holds or not."

Sela stood silent a moment. Then she said, "I will pay for carrying if I hire you. I have not hired you yet."

He nodded and left. I had expected him to be angry. He had heard her correctly.

The clay-weight trader arrived while Sela was lifting her pole. He had been looking for the same answer. When he heard the timber price, he offered a little oil toward the food for the carriers, but wanted a place for his jars on every market morning in return.

"Dala has not agreed to that," Raya said.

"I am offering food to men who will work for all of us."

"You may offer food. It does not buy Dala's path."

The trader considered his oil jar. "Then I will keep it. I can pay a man at the other landing to warn me when there is space."

Sela watched him go. "If he will not pay, we cannot carry it."

"We may carry it later," Madan said. "Or someone else may buy it. The wood has no reason to wait for this step."

I looked at the length on its supports. We had found a piece that might work. We had also found a price and three men who would carry it on terms they could name. Nothing in the yard belonged to us yet.

Raya took the cord from Madan's niece. "I will test the stone before the light goes. I will tell you what I find. That is the work I can do today."

"Will you need me?" I asked.

"To stand and watch me push a stone? No. You told Tesa you would carry for her."

I could hear the market sellers calling from beyond the yard. The shadow would be reaching the post soon.

Sela picked up her baskets. "If Raya says the stone holds, I will come back with Dala's answer. I will not promise my buyer this landing yet."

Madan's niece put the cord away with both twists still in it. I went toward Tesa's awning. Behind me the timber stayed where it had been, and for once I knew exactly why no one was lifting it.

***

When the shade touched Tesa's post, I was there.

She looked up from the cloth spread over her drying frame and pointed to a stack of wrapped bundles. There were four, each tied with its own cord. The first had already been moved once that morning, and the knots had tightened where the cloth beneath had settled.

"Do not carry them by the binding," she said. "It keeps the pieces together. It was not tied to take their weight."

I put my hands beneath the first bundle. It was heavier than the empty jar basket I had brought home for Amaya. The cloth was dry at the top and still held the warmth of the sun. Tesa lifted one corner, checked the folded edge, and told me which buyer would take it.

"To the house behind the pot seller. Ask for the woman who ordered the narrow lengths. She has the price ready. Bring me the cord back."

"Will she pay me?"

"I will pay you for carrying. She pays me for cloth. Do not mix the two."

I had nearly asked for an advance. Tesa had not offered one, and I had already failed to keep one morning she had given me. I lifted the bundle onto my shoulder and set off.

At the first turning a boy came running down the path. He was carrying a small pot with both hands and wanted me to move. I stepped aside onto a dry patch. The bundle slid against my neck, and I had to kneel to set it down before the folds shifted. The boy passed without slowing. I retied nothing. I carried it from below, as Tesa had said, and walked on.

The buyer's house was farther than I remembered. I found the pot seller, then the narrow passage behind her stall. The woman who had ordered the cloth opened the bundle while I waited. She counted the lengths, touched the edges and sent a girl inside for the payment. She counted it again in front of me.

"Tell Tesa the narrow piece has a loose thread," she said.

"Shall I bring it back?"

"No. I am telling her what I found. I will keep it at this price."

She gave me the cord. I went back without carrying the payment; the girl would bring it by the path they had agreed. I had carried cloth from one house to another. I had not become the seller.

The second bundle went to a woman whose doorway was too low for me to enter with the load on my shoulder. I put it down and carried it through in my arms. The third went by the river, where a man wanted to open it on the wet stones. I asked him to let me put it on a dry mat first. He complained that I was wasting his time, but he moved the mat.

On my return Tesa gave me water and a small piece of food. She had not promised a meal. She said she would deduct nothing for it.

"One more," she said. "The last bundle goes above the bend. Can you carry it before the light changes?"

"I can."

"Then finish it. If you cannot, tell me now and I will find another carrier."

I lifted the last bundle. I knew the way above the bend from Amaya's jars. I knew where the root held water. I had time to put the cloth down and check its fold before taking the turn.

***

Raya called to me from the lower path as I came back.

She was standing with a short pole in one hand and mud on her knee. Behind her, two men were looking at the stone below the rotten step. One held a basket of tools. I could see the cord she had borrowed from Madan's niece stretched between two stakes.

"The stone held when I pressed it," she said. "I want to lift the old piece away and look beneath. Can you help us?"

I had no bundle left on my shoulder, but Tesa was waiting for her cords and her answer about the deliveries. She had said she would pay me when I returned.

"How long will it take?" I asked.

"I do not know. We may find more mud beneath it."

"I have to go back to Tesa."

Raya looked at the empty space under my arm. "Then go."

"I could return after she pays me."

"You may. The light may be gone. I am asking whether you are here now."

One of the men with her said he could bring his brother from the cooking sheds. The brother had been paid to move baskets that morning and might be free. Raya asked the man to find out, then set the pole against the bank. She did not ask me again.

I wanted to stay long enough to see whether the stone was sound. Instead I walked back with Tesa's cords. At her awning she counted them and asked what the buyers had said. I told her about the loose thread, the wet stones and the four delivered bundles. She listened more carefully to those answers than to the time I had spent carrying.

"The buyer above the bend has not sent payment yet," she said. "That is between her and me. You brought the cloth where I asked. Here is your grain."

She measured it into my small basket. It was less than the price of a single cloth length. I knew because I had asked her before, and because I had seen what the buyers gave her. She did not try to make it sound larger.

"Will there be work tomorrow?" I asked.

"Perhaps. Some of the cloth I have left is for a buyer who may come here. I will not hire you to carry it until I know where it goes."

"Amaya needs a length to replace what I lost."

"I know what Amaya needs. She told me. I have not kept that length aside for you."

"Could you?"

"With what payment?"

I showed her the grain.

She shook her head. "That buys food for a household. It does not buy the cloth. And I have already sold the batch she preferred."

"Could you make another?"

"I will make cloth for the buyers who bring fiber and pay for my time. I have work before that. You can ask me again when you know what you can pay."

I wanted to say that the landing might be repaired and that men might pay me to help carry the timber. Tesa had heard those promises before. She held out the last cord I had returned.

"This is still wet," she said. "Lay it out before you leave. I cannot tie tomorrow's cloth with it as it is."

I spread it where she pointed. When I left, the cord was drying and my grain was mine.

***

Amaya was washing a small jar when I reached her house. She set it on its side and waited for me to put down the basket.

I poured the grain into a shallow bowl. She looked at it without touching it.

"Did Tesa give you this for all four bundles?"

"Yes."

"Did you finish them?"

"I did."

She took the bowl, then set it beside the store jar. "Did you ask about the cloth?"

"She has sold the batch you wanted. She can make another if I bring enough payment, but she has other work first."

Amaya dried her hands. She went to the shelf, took down the old patched measure and spread it on the mat. She showed me the edge where the missing cloth had been cut.

"This was not the finest length Tesa sold," she said. "I used it because it held its width when wet. I cannot replace it with any cloth you can afford first."

"I know."

"Do you? You asked whether I would choose a replacement when you had enough. You have not asked what I would choose."

I knelt beside her. She made me hold one end of the measure while she stretched it gently, then laid it flat again.

"Ask Tesa what it would cost to weave this width. Ask whether the thread she has now will hold the same way. If she says it will not, come back and tell me. I may choose something else. You do not choose for me because you are the one who owes it."

I had brought her grain before. She had accepted it as payment toward what I owed, without saying the debt was finished. This time she measured a portion into her store jar and pushed the rest back to me.

"Keep enough to eat before you work again," she said.

"I thought you wanted all of it."

"I want the cloth. I also want you able to carry the next bundle without falling."

"How much of the debt does this pay?"

She looked at the old measure. "We will count what I have accepted. We will not pretend it is the same as a length I can use."

She marked the grain in her own way on a small scrap of cord, a knot for the earlier payment and a second for today's. I could see both knots. I could not turn them into cloth.

Outside, voices rose from the direction of the landing. A man was calling for another hand. I stood before I knew I had moved.

Amaya followed my eyes to the door.

"Did you promise Raya anything today?"

"No. She asked. I told her I had to finish for Tesa."

"Then you can ask what happened. You cannot go back and give her the hour you sold."

At the landing I found the old piece lifted away and lying above the wet stones. Raya had borrowed a hand from the cooking sheds and paid him with food and a small measure of grain supplied by the man who wanted his jars moved. The stone beneath had held at first. When they raised the wood, a pocket of mud had opened beside it. Raya had closed the place again and sent the men home.

"You were right to finish your work," she said when she saw me.

"Would another hand have helped?"

"Another hand would have let us lift sooner. It would not have filled the hole."

I looked at the dark mud. We had lost part of the afternoon. We had also found a weakness that would have been hidden beneath a new step.

"Can Madan's wood still reach?"

"Perhaps. I will measure the bearing again when the water falls. If you are free then, you can help me hold the cord. If you are not, I will ask someone else."

The last light lay across the exposed stone. I had missed the lifting, and someone else had earned the food for it. I had brought grain home from a job I finished. Neither fact repaired the step.

When I returned, Amaya had covered the store jar. The two knots lay beside the patched measure, where she could see them without having to ask me what I had done.

***

The mud beside the lower stone took two days to dry enough for Raya to dig it out.

On the first day she went down with a narrow blade and found water still rising through the hollow. She filled it with loose gravel, watched the gravel settle, and took it out again. On the second she found a firmer seat beyond the place where the old wood had rested. Madan's niece brought the cord with its two twists. Raya laid it along the bank and asked her to hold it where the higher ground began.

"That is farther from the water," I said.

"It is farther from the soft earth. A step has to stand somewhere when the boat has gone."

She marked a third distance. It was shorter than the first, longer than the second. Madan's timber could reach it after the bad end was cut away, but Raya would not say it was bought or set.

Sela came after the midday market with grain in a covered bowl. Laman came with the pay he had earned carrying another owner's load through the farther channel. He had not returned to our bank because he was loyal to it. He had come because his next owner wanted fish delivered close to the houses above the bend, and the farther landing would leave the carriers a longer walk.

Madan set his price again, including the part of the wood he would lose when he cut off the knot. His niece counted what Sela offered and what Laman offered. The two amounts together were short.

"Can you take the rest after the next market?" Laman asked.

"Will the next market come if the wood breaks?" Madan said.

"I will owe you whether it breaks."

"You may. I have men to feed before they carry it."

Sela looked at me. I had grain from Tesa's work, and she knew it. I did not move toward my basket.

"That is for Amaya," I said.

"I was going to ask whether you could carry," Sela said. "I know you cannot buy a tree."

Madan's niece set the two payments apart. "There is enough for the wood if Madan accepts the fish Sela has offered for his household. There is not enough for the men."

Madan asked Sela to show him the fish. She had brought two dried portions wrapped in leaf. He inspected them, then agreed to take them at a price both named. His niece moved the grain and fish to the wood side of the mat. No one counted them twice.

Laman offered to pay one carrier from his next hire. The carrier who had warned us about the wet slope refused.

"I will take a promise from the man who hires me," he said. "I will not take one from a boat owner who may be hired by someone else tomorrow."

Laman considered that, then gave him grain from the measure he had kept for his own food. Sela offered a share for the other carrier and the third hand. They agreed on food before lifting, payment for the journey even if Raya rejected the wood, and a route past the cooking sheds. My name was not among the wages. I could help lift when free; I would not be paid unless someone hired me separately.

The clay-weight trader came by while the terms were being named. He heard them, asked whether paying now would reserve a place for his jars at every market, and left when Sela said no. He did not try to stop the work. He kept his oil and his choice of landing.

***

They brought the timber down on the following morning.

I met them near the cooking sheds with an empty shoulder. The older carrier had chosen a third hand, a woman who worked the high path and knew where the turn widened. She took the rear end with him while the other man steadied the front. I carried a rope and kept loose baskets out of the way. When they stopped to rest, I did not touch the wood until they asked me to help lower it.

The timber struck the ground once when a root caught beneath the rear end. The older carrier cursed and made everyone wait. Madan examined the mark before they lifted again. Raya was at the bank when they arrived. She ran her hand over the scar and told them to put the piece down on dry supports.

"That is the carrying done," she said.

The third hand held out her palm for the remaining grain. Sela paid her. The other two were paid as agreed. They ate before going back. None had been promised a share of future cargo, and none stayed to build the step.

Raya and Madan cut the bad end away. The dark wood opened farther under the blade than it had in the yard. Raya measured the sound length again. It would reach the new seat, with little to spare.

"Will it hold?" Laman asked.

"It may hold when it is set. It will not hold while you stand here asking me."

She worked the seat into the bank and packed the bearing with firm stones. Madan helped her raise the wood. I held the rope she told me to hold and released it when she said. A small part of the lower stone shifted under the first pressure. Raya made us take the timber off again, scraped the mud away and reset the stone. It took the better part of the morning.

When the timber finally lay across the bearing, she walked onto it alone, one foot at a time. She stood still where the wood met the bank, then stepped back. She asked Laman to put his hand on the upper end while she pressed the lower one with her pole. The wood moved less than before, but the bank was still damp.

"Can we use it?" Sela asked.

"For a foot, perhaps. I will test a carried weight when the water is lower. Not a loaded boat against the timber. It is a step for people, not a post to tie a hull."

Sela looked toward her baskets. "My fish must go tomorrow."

"Then ask me tomorrow what I have tested."

***

Dala came after Raya had put away her tools. She had been gathering the net from the path above the stones and had heard the cutting from where she worked. She stepped onto the firm part of the bank and looked at the new timber without touching it.

"Where do you want the baskets?" she asked Sela.

"One beside the high root until Laman is ready. Only tomorrow morning."

"Which one?"

Sela pointed to the larger of the two she had brought. Dala walked the path with her, stopping where the net line crossed the ground. The root Sela wanted stood too close to the line.

"If you put it there, I have to move the net," Dala said.

"Could I pay you to move it?"

"I am taking the net out before dawn. If the catch is poor I may have to set it again before your boat comes. Money will not tell me what the fish do."

They found a dry patch a few steps farther up. It was narrow enough that a second basket would block the way. Dala pressed her heel into the earth and looked at the water mark on the trunk.

"One basket. After I lift the net. If the water reaches that mark, the basket goes back up. If I need the path, I will tell you and you will move it."

Sela repeated the conditions. She did not ask for the second basket. Dala said she would come in the morning to say whether the net had been lifted. She did not give leave in advance for any other tide.

Laman stood beside his boat. Hiran was with him, sounding the shallows with her pole. He asked whether she would take the journey if Raya opened the step.

"From where to where?" she asked.

"From the upper bend to the landing. One fish basket out, an empty basket back. I will pay you for the pole work and the waiting if the water turns."

"If the water turns before we start, I may refuse."

"Then we will not start."

They agreed on a measure of grain for the journey and a smaller measure if they waited after arriving. Hiran insisted that the boat would not be pushed against the timber. Laman agreed. She would sound the approach again in the morning.

Sela wanted a warning before she hired the carrier for her fish. Marun had come to collect an empty pole from the market. She asked whether he would walk from Raya's bank to her awning at first light and tell her what Raya had actually tested. He asked for a little grain for the walk, whether the answer was open or closed.

"I will pay for the answer," Sela said. "I will not pay you to make it open."

"Then I will tell you what she says," Marun answered. "If she has not looked yet, I will tell you that."

The keeper came down with a blank strip. She had heard Sela's request to Marun, but had not heard the terms between Laman and Hiran. She asked them to repeat those terms in front of her if they wanted them remembered. They did. Dala told her the place and the one basket she might allow, then added that she had not yet lifted her net. Raya said she had tested only a person's weight and would decide about cargo after the next inspection.

The keeper cut separate marks for the offers and their limits. She asked whether anyone wanted to call the landing open.

No one did.

When the people left, I stayed beside Raya as she gathered the tools. The new timber lay in its seat. We had paid for it, carried it and set it. Sela had a possible place for one basket. Laman had a boat and a pole-handler who could refuse. Marun had agreed to carry an answer that might send the fish elsewhere.

"Are you satisfied?" I asked Raya.

"With the wood? More than yesterday."

"And the step?"

"Ask me when the water is where it will be tomorrow."

She pressed her pole against the lower stone once more. The stone held. She did not mark it open.

***

Marun reached Sela's awning before the fish were packed.

He was breathing hard from the walk and had mud on one foot. Sela set down the binding she was twisting and asked what Raya had said.

"She has not opened the step. She wants the water lower before she puts a basket on it."

Sela looked toward the sun. "Will she look before the buyer leaves?"

"She did not tell me when the buyer leaves. She told me to say the step is closed until she tests it."

Sela gave him the grain she had promised. He counted it in his palm and went back to the market. He had brought an answer worth paying for, though it was not the one she wanted.

I had come to help bind the fish. Sela had asked me the evening before, and I had told her I would come for the first hour. Amaya knew where I was. She had asked whether the work would earn anything toward the cloth. I told her Sela had offered food and a little grain for carrying after the fish were packed, if the landing opened. If it stayed shut, I would still help with the binding I had promised.

Sela had one basket ready and another covered beside it. The first was for the buyer above the bend. The second was for a different buyer who could be reached from the farther landing. She had not asked Dala for ground to hold both.

"Shall I take the first down?" I asked.

"Not until Raya calls it usable. I have paid Marun to tell me the truth. I will not make him walk twice because I do not like it."

She pulled the binding tight. I held the basket steady. The fish had been dried enough to travel, but the air was damp, and Sela did not want them uncovered while we waited.

A boy came from the path to say the buyer had sent him. Sela told him the fish were packed and the step had not opened. She asked whether the buyer would wait until midday. The boy said he would ask and ran back. Sela did not make him carry a promise he had not heard accepted.

***

At the bank, Dala had lifted the net and hung part of it above the high root. The rest lay folded beside her feet. She showed Sela the dry patch and pointed to the mark on the tree.

"The water has not reached it," she said. "You may put your one basket here while the path is clear. If I need the net again, I will tell you."

Raya was below them with a smaller basket filled with stones. She had chosen the stones herself and had not asked Sela to risk her fish in the first test. She put the weight on the new timber and stood beside it, listening as much as looking. The wood made a low sound when she shifted the load.

"Take it back," she said.

I lifted the basket and carried it to the dry bank. She crouched at the lower bearing. Water was moving through the gravel she had packed there. The stone itself had not turned, but a thin stream had begun to carry the smallest pieces away.

"Is it failing?" Laman asked from his boat.

"It is changing," she said. "If I say it is holding now, that does not tell you what it will do after another load."

Hiran had sounded the approach and was keeping Laman's bow away from the stone. She had agreed to a pole journey and a waiting measure. The boat was still empty. Laman asked whether she wanted to move farther out.

"Yes," she said. "The water is running harder beside that root. I will not hold the hull there."

They moved the boat a little downstream. The longer reach would mean an extra step for whoever carried the fish. Raya watched them move and did not call them back.

Sela arrived with the packed basket on her shoulder. She put it on Dala's dry patch, as agreed, and asked whether Raya would open the step.

"Not yet. I need to pack the gravel with larger pieces. I can do it while the water is low enough to reach."

"How long?"

"I do not know."

Sela turned to Laman. "If we wait, will you keep the boat?"

"I will if the owner pays for waiting. I have agreed to carry your basket if the step is safe. I have not agreed to stand here until dark."

Sela said she would pay him the smaller waiting measure they had discussed. Hiran heard the amount and asked whether her waiting was included. Laman said it was not; he would pay her separately, as promised. She agreed to stay until the water reached the dark mark on her pole. After that she would choose again.

The boy returned with the buyer's answer. He would wait until the sun was above the high roof, but not beyond it. He had another seller to visit. Sela sent the boy back with thanks. He went without a fish basket.

We waited while Raya found two stones large enough to hold the gravel. Madan had left an offcut from the timber near the bank. She used it as a lever, not as another step. I helped her lift the larger stone into place. Mud squeezed around my fingers. When the stone settled, the small stream slowed.

Raya washed her hands, then put the stone basket on the timber again. She carried it across herself. The lower bearing stayed in place. She came back and repeated the crossing with the weight held to one side, where a tired carrier might let it pull. The timber moved a little but did not roll.

"One carried load at a time," she said. "No stopping on the step. No boat tied to it. If the water reaches that pale mark, we stop."

Sela looked at the mark. It was lower than the one Dala had named for her basket. The two limits belonged to different work. Both could close the morning.

***

I took the fish basket from Dala's patch. It was heavier than Raya's test load. Sela saw me lift it and shook her head.

"Half first," she said.

She untied the cover and moved part of the fish into a smaller basket she had brought folded beneath the large one. Dala watched the transfer.

"You asked for one basket to wait," she said.

"Only the large one will wait. The small one goes now and comes back empty."

Dala considered that. "Then take it. Do not leave it on my path."

I carried the smaller load down the step. The wood was firm beneath my first foot. At the second step the basket pulled toward the river, and I shifted it closer to my body. I did not stop on the timber. Hiran held the boat where she had sounded safe water, and Laman took the basket from me without making the hull touch the step. I went back with the empty one.

The water had risen while I crossed. Raya looked at the pale mark, then at the gravel below the timber.

"Wait," she said.

I put the empty basket on dry ground. Sela had already moved the remaining fish into it. She did not lift it.

A thin thread of water was running again between the stones. Raya pressed the lower bearing with her pole. The wood held, but she could see the gravel shift beneath it.

"We close now," she said.

"The rest of the fish is here," Sela answered.

"I can see it."

"The buyer will take half a basket for half the price."

"Then that is what you may have sold today."

Sela stood over the fish. I had expected her to argue. She turned to Laman and asked whether he could deliver the smaller load to the buyer while she took the rest by the upper path.

Laman looked at Hiran. "Will you go with what we have?"

Hiran sounded once more. "Yes, if we leave now. I will not wait for another basket."

Laman agreed to carry the smaller load for a reduced price, but said he had still paid Hiran for her full morning. Sela accepted the charge. She had bought waiting and a journey, even though the load was smaller than she had planned.

I carried the remaining fish back up the path with Sela. Dala helped us clear the dry patch because she wanted to set her net again. She did not take responsibility for our loss. She had given the ground she said she would give, for the time she could spare.

At the market Sela found a carrier willing to take the remaining fish overland to the buyer. He named a price higher than she liked. She asked whether he could arrive before the sun reached the roof. He said he could try but would not promise the buyer would still be there. She paid him to try.

By the time I returned to the landing, Laman's boat had gone. Raya had lifted the testing basket away. She was putting larger stones beside the bearing, but she would not set them until the water fell again.

"Did the step fail?" I asked.

"It held the load we put on it. Then the water told me to stop. If I had called it open for the whole morning, I would have been wrong before the second basket."

"Will Sela come back?"

"Ask Sela."

The buyer took the smaller load Laman brought. He did not take the overland basket; the carrier reached him after he had left for another market. Sela paid the carrier for the walk. She sold the remaining fish later at a lower price. When she told me, she did not ask Raya to pay the difference.

We had carried a real load over the new step. We had also lost a sale while the water rose. I could point to both things without changing either of them.

***
Sela laid the stained leaf wrapping beside the keeper's knife. The brown patch had spread through the fold where the fish had rested.

"The boat took half a basket," she said. "The buyer paid for that half. The rest went by the upper path and arrived after he left. I paid the carrier for the walk. I sold the fish later for less."

"Did you see the buyer turn it away?" the keeper asked.

"No. The carrier told me. I saw him come back with it. I sold it afterward."

The keeper set one strip beneath the leaf and another beside it. She would not put the unseen refusal on the same strip as the money Sela had counted.

I had carried the first half across Raya's new timber. The rest had stayed above the rising water.

Laman came in without Hiran. He had delivered oil by the farther channel that morning and returned with an empty boat. She was working for another owner and had said she would speak for herself if anyone asked about her pole work.

"She warned me the water was rising," he said. "I paid her the waiting measure and the journey we agreed. I saw the first basket come aboard. I did not see the other one reach the buyer."

Sela nodded. Laman had paid Hiran for the crossing and the agreed wait even though Sela's load had been cut.

Raya put a wet stone beside the two strips.

"This is what came loose under the lower bearing," she said. "I packed larger stones there. Yesterday, at low water, the step held a carried basket. When the water reached the mark, I closed it. I will inspect it when someone asks to use it. If I am away, no one can say I have opened it."

"Could another maker inspect it?" Laman asked.

"If that person knows the work and wishes to answer for it. I will not answer for a test I did not make."

Dala stood at the doorway with her net weights in one hand. She had come to hear about the ground above the step.

"One basket may wait there after I lift the net," she told Sela. "Ask me on the morning you need it. I may have to set the net again."

Sela touched the stained leaf. "The buyer told me he would pay for a smaller load next market, if it reaches him before the sun passes the high roof. I will ask Marun to bring me word before I hire a boat."

"Has he agreed?" the keeper asked.

"Not yet."

The keeper left space for his answer.

The cord owner arrived with a jar and his line of three loosened knots. He said he had brought the jar from the farther landing for a buyer above the market.

"The owners there pay a man to tell them when the dry patch is full," he said. "They know he may tell them to turn back. They pay for the news."

"Then they know what they have bought," Sela said. "I will pay Marun for a walk if he agrees. I will not pay for news about every boat on the river."

"I could have offered the same service here."

"You offered a measure from every boat," Laman said. "I never hired your watcher."

"I know."

The cord owner asked Sela whether she would bring fish to the farther landing if Raya closed the step again.

"Some, if the buyer is there and the path is clear," she said. "I can still sell by land."

The clay-weight trader had been listening from the doorway. He asked Dala whether two jars could wait on her path at the next market.

"Not on my net ground."

He picked up his jar. "Then I will take them by the other landing. I have already asked for news there."

The cord owner moved aside to let him pass. He had won that trade. He did not ask anyone to call him the winner.

Laman said he might carry Sela's next load if no one had hired him first. He would ask Hiran about the pole work when he knew the journey. Dala went back to her net. Raya took the wet stone away before it marked the keeper's mat.

"Does anyone still ask me to record a charge for every boat?" the keeper said.

No one did.

Sela folded the stained leaf. The keeper left the place for Marun's answer blank. The cord owner took his jar toward the market. No one called him back.
***

I went with Amaya to Tesa's awning that afternoon.

I had carried two more bundles for Tesa since the landing test, and she had paid me for them. Amaya had kept the grain she chose to count toward the cloth. I brought the old measure folded in my hands and set it where Tesa could see its width.

Tesa took it without asking why we had come. She laid it against a fresh length and showed Amaya where the new weave drew inward.

"This one will shrink more when wet," she said. "It will cost less, but it will not keep the width you want."

Amaya ran her fingers along the edge. "Then I do not want it."

Tesa showed her another piece. The fiber was stronger, and the width was closer, but it had already been promised to a buyer.

"When will you have another?" Amaya asked.

"When I finish the work in front of me and have fiber enough. I will tell you the price when I know the work."

I asked what I could bring toward that price. Tesa named grain and a quantity of usable fiber. She would not take a promise of future carrying as payment for cloth she had not yet made.

Amaya unfolded the old measure. She showed Tesa the two knots she had tied for what she had already accepted from me.

"That is what he has paid," she said. "It is part of the debt. I will not call it the whole cloth."

Tesa nodded. She had heard the amount from the person who would receive the replacement. I had no reason to improve it by speaking.

On the way home Amaya carried the measure. I carried the empty basket. At the turning above the market she stopped to watch a boat pass our landing. It did not slow. The owner had chosen another place, and the pole-handler kept the bow in the current.

"Are you going down to call him back?" she asked.

"No."

"Good. You have work with Tesa tomorrow if she asks for you."

"I will ask her after I have brought this basket home."

She looked at the basket. "It is empty."

"Then I should be able to keep my word."

She went on. I followed.

At the lower bend the step stood where Raya had set it, the new wood dark at the waterline. Dala had taken her net down before evening. No basket waited on her path. A small boat came in while I watched, and its owner called up to ask whether he could put a man ashore. Raya was not there. The man on the bank told him the step had been tested at low water and that he would have to look at the present water for himself.

The owner sounded with his pole, chose the old stones a little farther along, and put his passenger down where the footing was firm. The passenger paid him and walked up toward the market. The boat turned away.

I had once wanted every owner to hear that our landing was open. Now I could tell the next one where the wood ended, where the stones began, and who would have to answer before a basket could stand above them. That was enough to send some boats away. It had brought one man ashore.

Behind me someone called for a carrier. I looked at the sun before I answered.
