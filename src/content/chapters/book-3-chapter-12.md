The woman had brought the canoe home, but someone had taken it again.

I found her at the lower landing with one foot in the water. She had her hand on the bow, as she had when the carriers returned it after the fighting. A rope ran from the stern to a new post driven into the mud. The knot was one I did not know. It had been drawn so tight that the wet fibers had bitten into the wood.

Two men stood beside her. One wore the short coat of the foreigners. The other, who had come to Maynila with their boats, spoke our language with a careful tongue. They were waiting for a third man to arrive.

“Who tied it?” I asked.

“The man in the coat,” she said. “He says it belongs among the boats they are counting.”

The man in the coat looked at the interpreter. The interpreter asked what I wanted.

“First I want to know whether the woman can take her canoe home.”

The man answered before the interpreter finished speaking. I heard a word I had heard often since the ships came: the word they used for a thing left without its owner. The interpreter told me the canoe had been found empty after the fighting.

“It was found empty,” the woman said. “I have not denied that.”

The interpreter looked from her to the man in the coat. “He says its master has not come to claim it.”

“I have come,” she said.

“He means the man who used it.”

“My husband used it. I used it before him.”

The interpreter gave her words to the foreigner, but he shortened them. I knew enough of his speech by then to hear when two sentences had become one. The foreigner pointed at the rope and said something about the missing man. He did not look at the woman.

She turned the bow toward me. “See the cut?”

A shallow notch lay beneath the rim, where the grain changed direction. I remembered her showing it to the boy who found the canoe. Her brother had made it when he fitted the bow. I put my fingers against the mark. The wood was rough where salt had dried in the cut.

“Your brother is here?” I asked.

“At my mother's house. He has a fever.”

“Would he come if he could?”

“He would say what he made. He would not know where my husband went.”

The distinction mattered to her. I had been about to ask for the brother as if a maker could tell us everything about a boat.

The foreigner pulled a small board from beneath his coat. A strip of pale material was fastened to it. He showed the interpreter marks made with dark ink. The interpreter read the words aloud in our language: one canoe, taken from the reeds, no owner present.

“That was written when it was found,” I said.

“That is what the men told him.”

“Then they told him what they knew. The woman has come since.”

The interpreter asked the foreigner to amend the words. He moved a finger along the line and shook his head. He said the boat had already been counted for the king's service.

The woman had listened to every word she understood. “If he needs a boat, he can ask its owner to hire it.”

The interpreter's mouth tightened. He did not translate at once. When he did, the foreigner gave a short laugh and gestured toward the river, where larger vessels lay under guard.

I could see the question the interpreter wanted me to answer. Would I tell her to accept the loss? A few years before, someone might have brought such a dispute to my house, and I might have thought the matter settled because I had heard it. The foreigner had soldiers within calling distance. The woman had a wet rope in her hand.

“What will they use it for?” I asked.

The interpreter repeated the question. The foreigner answered that they needed small boats to move loads from the shore to their vessels. He named a place upriver where men had been told to bring rice. I did not know whether those men had agreed to carry it.

“How long?”

The foreigner held up two fingers. The interpreter said two days. The woman asked whether the boat would be returned with its pole and fishing line. The foreigner did not understand the last part until the interpreter showed him the bundle of line beneath the seat.

He said the line could be removed.

“And the boat?” she asked.

The foreigner said it would be returned if it remained sound.

She let go of the bow. “That is a different answer.”

I looked at the canoe. Its stern peg had been bare when the boy found it. The two carriers had checked the hull before bringing it home. They had found a split that needed a short binding, and the woman had paid them to make it. The binding was still there. It had not been made for hauling heavy sacks between vessels.

“Who will pay if it breaks?” I asked.

The interpreter asked the foreigner. The foreigner spoke at length. When the answer came back, it contained no promise about a broken hull.

The woman drew her skirt clear of the water and stood beside me. “I will hire it to them if they pay for it, if they put no more in it than it can bear, and if they return it here. I will not give it away because someone wrote that my husband was missing.”

I knew she was frightened. Her hand had left a damp print on the wood. She did not ask me to speak the last sentence for her.

I repeated her terms to the interpreter and waited while he carried them across. The foreigner looked toward the larger boats. A man there was calling for another load. He answered that he could find a different canoe.

“Then he should,” the woman said.

The interpreter looked at me again.

“She has answered him,” I said.

The foreigner took the board and made a new mark beside the old line. I asked the interpreter what he had written.

“He says the boat is released.”

“Does it say to whom?”

The interpreter hesitated. “It says the claim was refused.”

“Whose claim?”

He did not answer immediately. The foreigner had already turned away. The interpreter called him back and asked a question I could not follow. The foreigner pointed at me, then at the woman. He said my title with an ending that was not ours.

The interpreter came back to us. “He has written that the old chief of Maynila objected.”

The woman stared at the board. “I objected.”

“I know,” I said.

“But his words will stay.”

The interpreter held the board so she could see it. There was a narrow space below the line. He asked the foreigner for the reed pen, but the man refused to hand it over. After a moment he added something himself, two small groups of marks, and showed the board again.

“What now?” I asked.

“The woman says the boat is hers.”

“Did you tell him her name?”

“I told him. I cannot make him write it as I said it.”

The woman gave her name a second time, slowly, as if the marks might listen. The interpreter repeated it. The foreigner said a version that left out part of it. He put the board away.

I wanted to take it from him and hold it until he listened. My right hand had begun to tremble from the effort of gripping the canoe. I closed it around my left wrist, hiding the movement beneath my sleeve. There was a time when men would have noticed my anger before they noticed my hands. Now the foreigner had noticed neither.

The interpreter told us to untie the boat.

The knot would not give. The woman worked at it with her nails until a strand came loose. I tried to help, but I could not get my thumb beneath the wet rope. A young carrier, passing with an empty basket, stopped and offered his knife.

“How much for cutting it?” the woman asked.

“For that? Nothing.”

“You will lose time.”

“I have not been hired yet.”

He cut the knot carefully, leaving enough rope for the woman to use again. She thanked him and asked whether he would help draw the canoe above the next tide. He named a small price. She accepted. I took the loose end and pulled with them, but after three steps I had to stop.

The carrier did not pretend he had not seen. He shifted his hold and asked the woman where she wanted the bow. She chose a place beneath the eave of a house whose owner had given her leave to keep it there. They dragged the canoe up the slope without me.

When it was settled, she counted out the carrier's pay. She checked the binding on the split hull and took the fishing line from beneath the seat. The carrier put his knife away. The foreigner and the interpreter had gone.

“You spoke for me,” she said.

“I tried.”

“You spoke when I had already answered.”

I could not tell whether she was thanking me or correcting me. “Yes.”

“They wrote you.”

“I heard.”

She wound the loose rope around the bow. “If they come again, they will ask you.”

I looked toward the post in the mud. It had been driven deep enough to remain after the rope was gone.

“Then I will tell them where to find you.”

“You can tell them that now.”

“I can.”

She finished the last turn of the rope and set the end beneath it. “My brother may be well enough tomorrow to look at the split. I want to take the canoe out when he says it is sound.”

“And your husband?”

She did not look up. “No one has brought me news.”

The sun had moved behind the houses. I could hear men counting sacks at the new landing, calling numbers in a language I was beginning to recognize but could not yet use. They had counted a canoe whose owner stood beside it. They had counted my objection and written it under my name. The woman had kept her boat for this day. The board would go somewhere else.

I asked her whether she wanted me to return when her brother came.

“No,” she said. “I want you to remember what happened.”

I stayed until she had covered the canoe against the evening rain. When I left, she was checking the cover's edge with the same care she had given the knot. She did not call me back.

At the gate, a boy asked why I was walking so slowly. I told him the slope had grown steeper. He looked at it, then at me, and said nothing. I was grateful for the silence, though I did not deserve to make him carry it.

Behind us the river rose against the new post. There was no rope on it now.

***

The roof began leaking above the sleeping mats before the first rain was heavy.

I heard the drops strike the floor and thought someone was washing a bowl. By the time I reached the back room, the woman who kept our household stores had moved two mats away from the wall. She had put a jar beneath the leak. A child sat on one of the rolled mats, holding a length of cord she had been told not to lose.

The woman's name was Awi. She had come to the house years before the foreigners' ships, when her mother's people were still living beside the upper path. She had never called herself my servant. She worked here because she and the others had agreed on what she would keep, what she would be paid, and where she could sleep. I had been away often enough to forget how much of the house depended on agreements I had not heard renewed.

“Don't step there,” she said. “The floor is wet.”

I stopped with my foot above the dark patch. My knee was already aching from the walk up from the landing.

“Who has looked at the roof?”

“Two makers. One says the ridge needs new leaves. The other says the upper binding has loosened. They cannot both come tomorrow.”

“Which one did you hire?”

“Neither. I need to know whether we can pay.”

She pointed to a low chest beneath the shelf. I had kept trade cloth in it when I was younger. Now it held a folded length of bark cloth, two copper bowls, a small knife and the cord the child had been holding. Awi opened it without asking my leave.

“I will use the bowls to pay for the roof if I must,” she said.

“Those were my mother's.”

“One was hers. The other was given to me when I took over the stores.”

I looked at the two bowls. The older one had a dent in its lip, made when I was young enough to be punished for dropping it. The other had a thin rim that caught the light. I could not remember who had put it in the chest.

“You have kept them together,” I said.

“Because the shelf was dry. That does not make them one person's goods.”

The child laid the cord beside the bowls. She had made three knots in it, one loose, one tight, one so small it was nearly hidden. She said she had been counting drops. Awi took the cord and hung it from the shelf, out of the water.

“Where will you sleep when the roof is fixed?” she asked me.

“In my room, I hope.”

“And when you are no longer here?”

I looked at her. The rain had begun to beat against the leaves. It was easier to hear the water than to answer.

“Have you been asked to leave?”

“Not yet.”

“Then why ask?”

“Because men have begun asking which rooms belong to the chief. One of them said the house would have to be counted again if its head died. I told him the roof was mine to repair while I lived under it. He asked whether I had a husband who could say so.”

“Who was he?”

“A man carrying the foreigners' questions. I did not ask his name. He knew none of ours.”

I thought of the board beside the canoe. A woman's answer had become my objection in a foreign hand. Here Awi had been asked to produce a man for a room she had kept sound through years when I was not home.

“You can stay,” I said.

“For how long?”

“As long as you need.”

She closed the chest. “You have promised the same place to someone else.”

I knew whom she meant before she told me.

After the fighting, a family whose house had burned had come to the gate. A man carried a sleeping child. His sister walked beside him with a bundle of clothes and a pot with its handle broken. I had told them there would be space in the back room until they could rebuild. I had not looked at the mats already there. I had not asked Awi.

“They haven't come,” I said.

“They came this morning. They saw the mats and went away.”

“Where?”

“To the sister's people beyond the upper path. They said they would ask there.”

“I should have told you.”

“You should have asked before telling them.”

She did not raise her voice. That made it harder to pretend she had misunderstood.

I sat on the edge of the chest. The child climbed down from the mat and carried the empty jar to the doorway. Awi called her back before she could step into the rain. She put a dry jar in the child's hands and showed her where to set it beneath the second leak. The child did as she was told, then went to sit by the door.

“Do you want the room only for yourself?” I asked.

“I want to decide whom I sleep beside. I want my niece to know where she will wake. If the family comes, I may offer them space. I may have to say no. That is a choice I should make before you promise it.”

“Would you have said yes?”

“Yesterday? Perhaps. Today I have two jars on the floor and no roof maker.”

I had imagined the house as something I could leave behind by saying who should have it. Awi had spent the morning learning which maker could come and which bowls she could spare. I had given a room twice and secured no roof at all.

“Let me speak to the family,” I said.

“Tell them what you told me.”

“That I promised without asking?”

“Yes.”

She opened the chest again and lifted the newer bowl. “And tell them I will ask about space after the roof is repaired. I cannot have children sleeping under that binding.”

I took the bowl from her and turned it over. There was a shallow mark beneath the base, made with a pointed tool. I had seen it before, on a measure Awi used when buying oil. It was her mark.

“I had forgotten this was yours.”

“You had forgotten who put it there.”

“I did.”

She took it back. “If I sell it, I will be poorer when the roof is finished. If I keep it, the rain will come through. Neither answer can be made by your remembering.”

I wanted to offer money. The purse I carried held less than the maker's price, and some of it had been promised to a boatman who had helped move a wounded man. I had made that promise myself. Awi knew it. She asked me to count what was there before I spoke.

We laid the coins and small pieces of metal on the chest. She separated what I had already committed from what I could spend. The remainder would buy binding fiber, not the new roof leaves. Awi put the older bowl beside the coins.

“Your mother's,” she said. “Will you sell it?”

I thought of the dent. I had carried the bowl through a fire, then through a flood. I had kept it when a good price was offered because it belonged to a woman who had known my name before anyone called me raha. I had imagined giving it to someone who would remember her.

“Ask the maker what it will buy,” I said. “Do not agree to a price until you know whether the leaves can be brought.”

Awi looked at me for a long moment. “Will you come with me?”

“If I can.”

“Then come now, before the rain closes the path.”

I rose too quickly and had to hold the shelf until the room steadied. She waited, but she did not reach for my arm. The child watched us from the doorway.

Outside, the rain had softened to a thin fall. Awi carried the old bowl wrapped in cloth. I carried the coins in a small pouch. The child stayed with a neighbor who had agreed to watch her until we returned. Awi asked the neighbor what she would want in return. The woman asked for help carrying water the next morning. Awi agreed.

We found the roof maker beneath his eave, sorting dry leaves from damp ones. He listened to Awi's account of the ridge and asked whether the binding could be reached without removing the whole upper course. She said she did not know. He offered to inspect it at first light, before naming a price. He would not take the bowl until he had seen the work.

I asked whether he could come sooner.

“I am repairing my sister's roof tonight,” he said. “Her child sleeps beneath it.”

I had no answer that would make our roof more urgent than hers.

On the way back, Awi stopped where the upper path divided. The displaced family had gone toward the houses beyond the trees. She said she would speak to them herself after the maker's inspection.

“I can come,” I said.

“You can tell them you made a promise you had no right to make. Then I will tell them what space I can offer.”

“Will that be enough?”

“I don't know.”

The rain had stopped. Water still ran from the roofs into the path. Awi shifted the bowl beneath her arm and walked ahead. I followed slowly, counting the places where the stones held firm.

At the gate, the child came running from the neighbor's house. She asked whether the roof would be fixed. Awi said a maker would look at it in the morning. The child asked whether the two bowls would still be in the chest. Awi said one would, and perhaps both. She did not promise more.

I entered the back room after them. The jars were filling. Awi showed the child how to empty them without spilling water across the mats. Then she went to put the old bowl away.

I had wanted to leave her a house. For that night I could offer only a portion of a maker's price and an admission I should have made before the rain. She had already found the hands that would keep the room dry.

***

The roof maker found the loosened binding before he found the hole.

He stood on a ladder that belonged to the neighbor and pushed two fingers beneath the ridge leaves. Awi held the ladder at its foot. I stood with the old copper bowl in my hands, though there was nothing for me to do with it.

“See?” he called. “The leaves have slipped because this is slack. I can bind it again. If the old fiber breaks when I pull, you will need another length.”

“How much?” Awi asked.

He named the cost of his morning and the fiber he had brought. She asked whether the price included replacing the leaves already spoiled. He said it included moving them aside, but not new leaves. She had expected as much. She made him repeat the price so the neighbor could hear it.

I put the bowl down beside the door.

“Will that cover it?” I asked.

Awi opened her palm. She had counted the coins before he arrived. “If he finds no more rot. The bowl can stay.”

The maker looked down. “I have not promised the whole roof.”

“I heard you,” she said.

I had once sent men to raise a gate without learning what they would find beneath its posts. I had told them when it must be finished. Here a maker was refusing to name the work before he saw it. I could have learned that much without growing old.

A boy came through the gate while the maker was still on the ladder. He carried no tools and wore the look of someone sent to a house where he did not know the people.

“Is the old raha here?”

Awi pointed toward me. “He is standing in front of you.”

The boy lowered his eyes. “There are two men at the outer path. One is a priest of the foreigners. The other speaks for him. They ask whether they may come.”

“Did they say what they want?” I asked.

“To speak about the water.”

The maker stopped pulling at the fiber. Awi looked up at him.

“Keep working,” she said. “I have hired you for the morning.”

The boy waited. I asked whether the men had brought soldiers. He said they had not. I told him to invite them to the shaded place outside the gate, where a visitor could sit without walking through the rooms. Awi asked the boy to bring a second mat. She did not offer the one beneath the leak.

The priest was younger than I expected. His hair was thinning at the front, and the skin of his neck was red where the collar rubbed it. The man beside him was older, with a salt stain on the hem of his cloth. I had heard him interpret at the landing, though I had never asked where he lived. The priest carried a small wrapped object. The interpreter carried a water gourd of the sort sold in the market.

They greeted me. The interpreter gave the priest's greeting in our language, then added my title. I did not know whether the priest had used it.

“You have come about water,” I said.

The interpreter smiled a little. “He has come about baptism.”

“I have heard the word. Tell me what he asks.”

The priest spoke slowly. The interpreter listened, then said, “He asks whether you will receive the washing and enter the faith of his God.”

I looked at the gourd. “Is that the water?”

The priest answered before the interpreter could ask him. He shook his head, then spoke again.

“He says the water can be taken from here,” the interpreter said. “It is the words spoken over it that matter.”

“What words?”

The priest began to recite. I recognized none of them. The interpreter waited until he finished.

“He names the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.”

“Does he want me to repeat them?”

“He wants you to understand whom you receive.”

The priest said something else. The interpreter translated it as a promise that a person could begin again.

I heard the maker on the roof pull the binding taut. The old leaves made a dry scraping sound. He called for Awi to pass up another length of fiber. She did so without looking toward us.

“When a man begins again,” I asked, “does he cease to owe what he owed yesterday?”

The interpreter hesitated. The priest watched his face.

“That is not what he means,” the interpreter said.

“Then tell me what he means.”

They spoke together for a while. The priest moved his hands as if separating two things laid close together. The interpreter told me that the washing concerned sin and the life to come. He said it did not erase a debt between neighbors.

“Does your priest say that, or do you?”

“I have heard him teach it.”

“I asked what he says now.”

The interpreter turned back to the priest. This time he took longer. When he faced me again, he said, “He agrees. A man must still make right what he has taken.”

I had no way to know whether those were the same words. But I could ask the man who had spoken them, and the interpreter had let me do it.

The priest opened his bundle. It held a small wooden cross, polished where fingers had touched it. He placed it on the mat between us. He did not push it toward my hand.

My mother had taught me prayers that were not his. I remembered the sound of her voice more clearly than every word. She had once stood beside a boat I wanted to take and asked me to count the people whose work would carry it. I had argued that I would return with help. She had asked what would happen if I did not. The argument had lasted longer than the boat's preparation. The boat had gone only when the people who owned its labor had made their own bargains.

I had heard other prayers in Brunei. Some were said by men who kept careful accounts and gave fairly; some by men who cheated a rower and washed their hands afterward. I had never found that the words alone told me which man would pay.

I did not tell the priest all of this. It would have taken another interpreter and a longer morning.

“Will he ask me to say that the prayers of my mother were false?” I asked.

The interpreter's face changed. He asked the priest, who answered at length. I watched the priest's hands. They rested on his knees. He did not reach for the cross.

“He says there is one God,” the interpreter told me, “and that he teaches the way to know Him through his Son.”

“That is what he believes. I asked what I must say.”

The priest listened to the question again. His answer was shorter this time.

“He asks you to renounce what keeps you from that faith.”

“Who decides what keeps me from it?”

The interpreter repeated the question. The priest looked troubled, or perhaps merely tired of speaking through another mouth. He said that I must learn before I made the promise.

“That I can agree to,” I said.

The interpreter seemed relieved. I wondered whether he would report that I had agreed to more.

Awi came from the doorway carrying a jar of drinking water. She set it beside the visitors and asked whether they wanted cups. The priest thanked her. The interpreter said the thanks in our language. She poured for them, then sat near the door. The roof maker was pulling another strip through the ridge.

The priest asked whether she lived in my house.

“I do,” Awi answered.

The interpreter turned to me. “He asks whether your household will receive instruction with you.”

“Ask her.”

The priest looked toward Awi. The interpreter repeated the question to her. She said she had work to do and would listen another day if she wished. She asked whether the priest had come to repair the roof. The interpreter laughed before translating. The priest did not laugh. He asked what was wrong with it.

Awi explained the binding and the price. The priest listened and offered to have someone from the foreign settlement look at the damage.

“The maker is here,” she said. “I have agreed to his price.”

The interpreter told the priest she had already hired a man. The priest nodded and withdrew his offer. He seemed surprised that she had answered him without asking me.

I was glad she had. I was less glad that I had waited for the priest to ask before remembering she could.

A man appeared at the outer path with a message for the interpreter. He waited until the priest finished drinking. The message concerned a boatload of food expected at the foreign landing. The interpreter said he was needed there before the tide turned.

The priest looked at me. He asked whether he might return with someone who could explain his teaching more fully.

“Return,” I said. “I will listen. But do not tell your commander that I have promised to receive the water.”

The interpreter repeated the sentence. The priest answered, and the interpreter said, “He will not call this a baptism.”

“I know it is not. I am asking about the promise.”

The interpreter gave the words again. The priest held my gaze and nodded.

“Does he understand?” I asked.

“He understands what I have told him.”

It was an answer with a space inside it. I had lived long enough to hear the space.

The priest wrapped the cross. As he rose, his knee caught the edge of the mat and he nearly fell. I reached for him. My hand shook, and the interpreter steadied him first. The priest thanked us both. His face had reddened again, this time from embarrassment.

At the path he turned and said something without waiting for translation. I heard the word for tomorrow.

“He will come after the morning prayers,” the interpreter said.

“Tell him to ask at the gate. The house may be busy.”

After they left, Awi took away the cups. She asked what I had agreed to.

“To listen again.”

“And what will they say you agreed to?”

“I asked them to say only that.”

“Will they?”

“I do not know.”

She carried the water jar inside. I watched the maker climb down from the roof. He had used more fiber than he first expected, but less than he had feared. He showed Awi the old length, frayed at the place where it had held the ridge. She asked him to keep it until they had tested the repair in rain. He agreed.

I asked whether I could climb up and look. The maker looked at my knees and said I could inspect the binding from the doorway when the next rain came. Awi paid him the agreed amount and kept the old bowl.

The priest had asked me to begin again. The roof would still leak if the new binding failed. Awi would still own her bowl. The woman at the landing would still have to defend her canoe. I did not know what the water could change in me. I knew what it could not do for them.

Awi took the frayed fiber from the maker and laid it beside the chest. “We will know after rain,” she said.

I went inside before the light failed. The old bowl sat on the shelf, its dent turned toward the room.

***

The first rain after the roof was bound came before dawn.

I woke to the sound of water running from the eaves. For a while I lay still, waiting for the slow strike of drops inside the room. There was none. Beyond the partition, Awi's niece was counting the water in the jar she had left beneath the old leak.

“Empty,” she said.

“Then put it away,” Awi told her.

The child dragged the jar across the floor. I heard its base scrape against the boards, then the soft sound of Awi lifting it so the rim would not chip. I closed my eyes again. The room smelled of wet leaves and the smoke of a fire that had not quite caught.

I had promised to go to the upper landing that morning. A boatman who had helped move a wounded man after the fighting was waiting for the last of his pay. I had separated his money from the roof coins with Awi two days before. I meant to hand it to him myself. Afterward I would go with her to the family I had offered a room without asking her.

I sat up. My head moved after the rest of me. The mat tilted, and the wall seemed farther away than it had been. I waited for the room to settle.

Awi heard me reach for my sandals.

“Where are you going?”

“To the landing.”

“You have not eaten.”

“I will eat when I return.”

“You said that yesterday.”

I had eaten yesterday. I could remember the bowl. I could not remember finishing it.

She came through the partition carrying a little rice in a shallow dish. The child followed with a cup of water. I took the cup and found that my hand shook enough to make the surface tremble. I held it in both hands.

Awi touched my forehead. Her fingers were cool.

“You are hot.”

“It is the room.”

“The rain has cooled the room.”

I drank. The water had the taste of the clay jar. I had known that taste since I was a child. It did not steady me.

“Give me the boatman's coins,” I said.

“They are where you put them.”

“Then I will take them.”

“You will fall before the gate.”

I tried to stand to prove her wrong. The room moved again. I sat down hard enough to make the dish jump.

Awi did not say she had warned me. She set the rice beside my knee and went to the chest. The coins were in the small pouch I had tied myself. She brought it to me, opened it, and laid out the pieces one by one. I counted them. There was enough for the boatman's promised amount, and no more.

“I can send someone,” she said.

“I told him I would come.”

“You told him you would pay. He needs the money more than he needs to watch you carry it.”

I looked at the pouch. She was right about the promise, though I had made the visit part of it in my mind.

“Who will take it?”

“I will ask the neighbor's son. He goes to that landing to sell oil. I will give him something for carrying it.”

“From what?”

“From my own money.”

“You should not have to pay for my promise.”

“Then tell me which of your coins will pay him.”

I counted again. The answer had not changed.

“Ask the boatman to take his money here,” I said.

“He has work. You would make him leave it to collect what you owe.”

She tied the pouch and put it beside the dish. “Eat. I will decide how to send it.”

I wanted to argue that it was still my debt. It was. That did not give me the right to make every person around it lose a morning.

The rain ended. Awi opened the shutters and let the damp air in. The child went outside to see whether water had collected beneath the ridge. She returned with a small leaf in her hand, unbroken and wet.

“Nothing fell inside,” she said.

“Then the maker's work held,” Awi told her. “For this rain.”

The child put the leaf beside my dish. She said it was proof. I told her it was a leaf, and she laughed. I had not meant to make a joke.

***

By midday I was cold.

Awi covered my legs with a cloth. She had already sent the neighbor's son to the landing with the money and a message: the boatman should count it before the carrier left. She had told the boy not to ask the boatman to thank me.

The boy returned before the sun had crossed the roof. He said the boatman had counted the coins, accepted them, and given him a little oil for his mother. Awi asked whether the boatman had named any amount still owed. The boy said no. She asked whether he had heard the answer himself. He said yes.

I was relieved, then ashamed of being relieved. Awi had spent her morning securing a payment I had been too weak to deliver. The boatman had been paid because she had acted, not because I had risen from my mat.

I tried to thank her. She was washing the dish I had barely touched.

“Eat more first,” she said.

“Did you pay the boy?”

“I did.”

“I will return it.”

“When you have it.”

She set the dish down and came to look at me. I could feel sweat cooling beneath my shirt. She asked the child to bring the neighbor who knew which herbs were used for fever. The child ran out. Awi sent a second message to a man who could bring a healer from the next cluster of houses if the neighbor thought it necessary.

I said the healer would cost money.

“Yes,” she said.

“We have little.”

“I have counted it.”

“You have the bowl.”

“I also have work I can do. I will choose what to spend.”

She turned toward the door. I caught her sleeve.

“The family,” I said. “I promised to speak to them.”

“I am going.”

“You should wait for me.”

“No. Their child has been sleeping in a crowded room. They need an answer today.”

“I have to tell them I was wrong.”

“I can tell them you know it.”

“They should hear it from me.”

“Then you can say it when you can walk there. I am going to ask what they need now.”

I let go of her sleeve. She smoothed the cloth where my fingers had creased it. She did not ask permission again.

When the neighbor came, she brought a clean cloth and a small bundle of leaves. She examined me with the practiced impatience of someone who had seen many old men insist they were merely tired. She asked when the fever began, whether I had passed water, whether I could swallow, and whether the pain in my chest was new. I answered as well as I could.

She did not name an illness. She told Awi to keep me drinking, to change the damp cloth, and to send for the healer if my breathing worsened. She did not promise I would recover.

The child sat near the door, turning the leaf she had brought me between her fingers. Awi told her to take it outside before it dried into the floor. She obeyed.

I slept for a while. When I woke, voices were coming from the front of the house.

***

Soliman had arrived without his men.

He stood beside the doorway speaking to Awi. I could see his shadow on the mat. The lines of his face had deepened since the fighting, but I still recognized the way he held his shoulders when he had brought a question he did not want to ask.

“There is a message from the foreign settlement,” he said. “They want the old raha to come and hear a new account of the houses near the river.”

“He is ill,” Awi answered.

“Can he speak?”

“He can. He is not going there.”

I called him in.

He came to the edge of my mat and sat. He did not reach for my hand. He looked at the untouched rice and the cup of water beside it.

“They have asked for you by name,” he said.

“What do they want counted?”

“The houses near the landing, and the people who say they have rights to the ground. They want someone who knows the old claims.”

“Who has brought the claims?”

“Some owners. Some men who say they speak for owners.”

“Ask the owners.”

He gave me a tired look. “I have asked. Some are afraid to go. Some have already spoken and say the interpreter wrote another thing.”

I thought of the woman and her canoe. The written objection had worn my title when it belonged to her.

“Then do not bring them as a single answer,” I said.

“They will ask for one.”

“They may.”

He waited for me to tell him how to refuse. I had no words that would make the soldiers less numerous or the questions less dangerous. He knew that as well as I did.

“Will you come when you are well?” he asked.

“I will hear them here if they come without men to compel the household.”

“That may not satisfy them.”

“No.”

Awi appeared with the cup. She put it in my hand and watched until I drank.

Soliman looked toward her. “Who will speak for the house while he is ill?”

“I will speak for the rooms I keep,” she said. “The others will speak for theirs.”

He seemed ready to say that the foreigners would not accept so many voices. Instead he asked whether she had been troubled by their men. She told him about the visitor who wanted a husband to answer for her sleeping place.

“Did he return?” Soliman asked.

“Not yet. If he does, I will ask his name.”

Soliman nodded. He stayed until I had finished the cup, then stood. At the door he turned back.

“I came because I thought you would tell me what to do.”

“I would have told you to come tomorrow,” I said.

He almost smiled. “Tomorrow they will still be there.”

“Yes.”

He left with no command from me. I heard him speak to Awi again outside. He asked where he could find the owners who had complained. She named the houses she knew and told him to ask each person whether they wanted to be found. He thanked her. I had never heard him thank me for that kind of answer.

***

Awi returned near sunset.

The displaced family had been staying with the sister's people. The roof there was sound, but the room was too crowded for another night. Awi had offered them a corner of our back room for three nights, beginning after the sleeping mats dried. She had asked the woman who already slept there whether the arrangement suited her. She had said yes, provided the newcomers helped carry water.

“You offered the room?” I asked.

“Part of it. For three nights. Then we will speak again.”

“Will that be enough?”

“It will be enough for tonight. They may choose another place before the third night.”

She told me that the man who carried the child had asked whether the old raha still stood behind his earlier promise. She had answered that I had made it without asking the people who lived here. The man had been angry. He had also been tired. When Awi described the space and the work expected, he said he would bring his family at dawn.

“I should tell him myself,” I said.

“When you can.”

She had arranged the room, secured the boatman's pay, tested the roof and found someone to tend my fever. None of those things had waited for me. She was tired enough to sit on the floor rather than the chest.

“Did you spend the bowl?” I asked.

“No.”

“The healer?”

“He will come after dark. The neighbor sent for him when your fever rose.”

“How will you pay?”

“I have made an arrangement for a morning of work in his household. He agreed to hear what I can offer.”

I looked at her hands. The skin near one thumb had split from wet rope. I had wanted to leave her a house. She had used her labor to keep me in it for another night.

“You must not give him work for me,” I said.

“I already have.”

“I did not ask you.”

“I know.”

I wanted to say I would repay her. She had heard too many promises from my mat.

Awi rose and checked the roof above the partition. The binding held. She told her niece to put the jars away so the new family would have room to set down their things.

I watched the child carry them, one at a time. She stopped to ask where the last jar belonged. Awi pointed to a shelf that was dry. The child put it there without looking toward me.

Outside, the tide was turning. I had meant to cross the upper path that morning. The boatman had his money. A family had a place to sleep. Soliman had gone to hear the owners of the houses. I had not left the mat.

The healer arrived when the first lamp was lit. He knelt beside me and asked for my hand. I gave it to him. Beyond the partition, Awi was telling the child how many mats to unroll before morning.

***

The healer held my wrist for a long time.

He had come in the dark, when the house was quiet except for the newcomers settling their mats in the back room. Awi had told them I was ill. The family had come anyway, carrying the pot with the broken handle and a bundle of clothes. They had put the pot where the child could reach it without stepping over anyone asleep.

The healer counted my pulse without moving his lips. Then he listened to my breathing and asked Awi how much I had drunk. She told him. He asked whether I had been coughing before the fever. I said I had, but only at night. He wanted to know whether I had told anyone.

“I thought it would pass.”

“Many things do,” he said.

He gave Awi instructions about water and rest. He said there were signs she should watch for and that he would return if she sent for him. He named no certain cure. I was glad he had not asked me to promise I would recover.

When he left, the house became quiet again. I could hear the little movements of people who had learned to sleep close to strangers: a mat drawn aside, a child whispering, someone reaching for a cup in the dark.

Awi sat beside me.

“The priest came this morning,” she said.

I opened my eyes. “While I slept?”

“Yes. I told him you were ill. He asked whether he should return with his interpreter.”

“What did you say?”

“That I would ask you.”

I remembered the small cross on the mat and the priest's embarrassed stumble when he rose. I had asked him to come again. I had not expected to be unable to sit before him.

“Ask him to return,” I said.

Awi studied my face. “Do you want the washing?”

“I want to hear him again.”

“You heard him once.”

“I heard another man's words for him.”

“That will happen again.”

“I know.”

She took the empty cup and went to fill it. I watched the doorway until she returned. She did not tell me whether she thought I should receive the water. I did not ask her to decide.

***

The priest returned when the light had moved across the floor.

He brought the same interpreter. The interpreter had a band of cloth around one wrist where a rope had rubbed it raw. The priest saw my mat and knelt so he would not stand over me. He placed the cross on the floor. He had not brought soldiers or a clerk.

The interpreter asked whether I was strong enough to speak. I said I could speak if he would let me stop to breathe.

The priest began with the teaching he had offered at the gate. He spoke of a God who made the world, of the Son who had lived among men, and of a life that did not end with the body. The interpreter gave me the words slowly. Sometimes he asked the priest to repeat a phrase. Sometimes he chose a word I knew from trading with men whose prayers differed from ours.

“Does he mean that the dead return to this house?” I asked.

The priest shook his head before the interpreter had finished. He answered that the life he promised was with God. I did not ask him to describe a place he had not seen.

He asked what I believed.

I thought of the prayers I had heard as a boy, of my mother's hands raised when she was frightened for someone she could not help, of the men in Brunei who washed before praying and then went back to bargaining over cargo. I thought of those who had carried wounded men from the channel and those who had refused them water. I had seen both acts done by people who named God.

“I believe there is more to answer for than the words a commander writes,” I said.

The interpreter spoke to the priest. The priest listened and asked whether I believed in the God he had described.

“I believe there is one God,” I said. “I have said that for many years. I do not know all the names by which men tell me to approach Him.”

The priest did not look satisfied. He asked me about his Son. The interpreter tried one rendering, stopped, and tried another. I asked him to tell me which part had changed.

“The first word might mean a son born to a household,” he said. “The priest does not mean that in the way we use it.”

“Then do not use that word alone.”

He explained again. I listened until I could tell him what I had heard. The priest corrected one part. We went through it once more.

I could not have done this a year earlier without growing angry. I had once thought that if a man asked a question he should accept the answer given in the first language available. I had spent too much of my life watching people lose a boat or a room because a speaker had shortened their words.

“Do you wish to receive baptism?” the interpreter asked at last.

I looked at Awi. She was seated by the partition with her hands folded over her knees. She did not turn away. The child was outside helping the new family carry water.

“Will he ask the people of this house to do it because I have?” I asked.

The priest listened, then said that each person must be taught and must answer. The interpreter gave me that much. I asked whether the priest could promise that the foreigners' officers would leave the household in peace.

“No,” the interpreter said before he turned.

He caught himself and asked the priest. The priest answered at length, his hands still on his knees. The interpreter said the priest could speak for his teaching and for his own conduct, not for every soldier or officer.

“Then I will not ask him to promise what he cannot give.”

The priest waited.

I had wanted to know whether I could enter his faith without lying about the life I had already lived. He had told me I must renounce what opposed it. I did not know whether I understood every part of that renunciation. I understood that he was asking me to make a promise about myself.

“I wish to receive the water,” I said. “I will answer the questions as I understand them.”

The interpreter repeated this. The priest asked me the confession he had taught. He stopped after each question. I answered in our language. Twice I asked for the words again. The priest waited while the interpreter found them.

I will not say that every doubt left me. The house had not changed around us. Awi's neighbor still had work to do before evening. The family in the back room still needed a place after three nights. I knew that water could be poured over a man whose hands had done harm. I did not know how a man could undo all that harm before he died.

The priest asked for water. Awi brought it in a clean cup. He asked whether I could sit higher. I tried. The interpreter helped me, then moved away when Awi came to support my back.

The priest said the words. I heard the names I had practiced. Water touched my forehead and ran along my temple into my hair. It was cool. I had expected the sound of it to be louder.

He made a sign above me and spoke a blessing. I closed my eyes because the light hurt them. When I opened them, he was still kneeling there. He had not become a man who could answer for the commander. I had not become a man who could pay every debt.

He asked whether I wanted him to return. I said he might, if Awi would admit him and I could still speak. He thanked her for the water. She took the cup and set it with the others to be washed.

After the priest left, the interpreter remained a moment.

“What will they write?” I asked him.

“That you received baptism.”

“Will they write what I asked?”

“I do not know.”

“Then tell anyone who asks that the people of this house answered for themselves.”

He looked at Awi. “I heard her answer.”

“Remember it.”

He left by the outer path. I could hear the sound of his sandals on the dry ground until he passed the gate.

***

I slept through part of the afternoon.

When I woke, the older copper bowl stood beside the mat. Awi had taken it from the chest. Its dent caught the light. I put my hand against the rim.

“Did you bring this for water?” I asked.

“No. You asked me yesterday what would be left in the house.”

“I asked too many things yesterday.”

“You asked me whether I would spend the bowl. I told you I had not.”

She waited. I remembered my mother carrying that bowl to a boat when I was small. She had brought food for men who were leaving before dawn. I had dropped it on the stones and made the dent. She had been angry until she saw that I had not broken my foot. Later she had kept it where I could see it.

“I want to offer it to you,” I said.

Awi did not take it.

“Why?”

“You have worked for this house longer than I have been willing to count. The healer will be paid with your work. I have no money left to return that time to you.”

“If I take the bowl, will you say I owe you care?”

“No.”

“Will you ask me to keep it because it was your mother's?”

I looked at the dent. I wanted her to keep it. I wanted someone to remember why it was marked. That was a wish I could not make into a debt.

“No,” I said. “You may sell it. You may use it. You may give it to the child when she is older. You may refuse it.”

She lifted the bowl and turned it over. The underside was worn smooth.

“If I take it,” she said, “someone may come after you die and say it belongs with your other goods.”

“I can tell them it is yours.”

“They may write your words differently.”

I thought of the canoe and the woman who had insisted on her own name. I asked Awi whom she wanted to hear the offer. She named the neighbor who had watched the child and the maker who had repaired the roof. The maker had gone to another job, but the neighbor was home. Awi sent her niece to ask the woman to come.

The neighbor arrived with wet hands. She had been washing cloth. Awi explained what I offered and why she was uncertain. The neighbor asked me whether I was giving the bowl as payment for work already done or as a gift.

“Both,” I said, then heard the confusion in it.

Awi shook her head. “Choose.”

I thought about the healer's fee, the roof, and all the days she had kept the house while I was away. I could not reckon them fairly from my mat.

“It is hers if she accepts it,” I said. “I am not buying her next day.”

The neighbor repeated the words. Awi asked her to remember them. Then she took the bowl from my hands.

“I accept it,” she said. “I will decide what to do with it.”

She did not thank me. She had asked for clarity and received as much as I could give. The neighbor went back to her washing. Awi carried the bowl out of my room and put it among her own things.

The shelf beside the mat looked bare without it.

***

Near dusk I asked Awi to open the shutters.

The river was beyond the houses, hidden by roofs and trees, but I could hear a boat pole strike wood at the landing. Someone shouted for a basket to be moved. Another voice answered that the path was already in use.

I could not tell who had spoken. I remembered a landing we had repaired years before, when every merchant wanted the same dry place. We had argued about a charge, a watcher, a path, and whose hands would do the work. The wood had held for a while. Other people had repaired it after us. I had gone away and returned, and the people at the landing had kept asking their own questions.

Awi came back with a fresh cup. She held it while I drank. I was too tired to take it in both hands.

“The priest may come tomorrow,” I said.

“If you want him.”

“And the family?”

“They are here. Their child is asleep.”

“Soliman?”

“He sent word that he is hearing the owners of the houses. He will come when he can.”

I had wanted to leave instructions for all of them. I could no longer remember which instruction belonged to whom. Awi did not ask me to recite them.

The room grew darker. She lit a lamp and placed it where the child could see the path to the back room without stumbling. I watched her move it once, then again, until the light reached the doorway.

I wanted to tell her that my mother had once done the same thing with a lamp when the tide was high. I could not find the words. Awi saw me looking and asked whether the flame troubled my eyes.

“No,” I said. “Leave it there.”

She left it.

***

*After Ache: Awi*

I knew he was dying when he stopped asking what had been done.

For three days he had wanted to know whether the roof held, whether the boatman had received his pay, and whether the family in the back room had enough mats. Even when he could scarcely lift the cup, he asked. On the last morning he looked at the shutters and did not ask me to open them.

The child had woken early. She was kneeling beside the jars, checking whether rain had come through the roof during the night. I told her to leave them. She wanted to know why we kept jars beneath a roof that had been mended. I said we kept them until we trusted the binding through more than one rain.

She asked whether the old raha would be able to see the next rain.

“I don't know,” I said.

She went to fetch water without asking again.

The healer came before the market opened. He sat beside the mat, felt the old man's wrist, and asked me how long it had been since he drank. I told him. He tried to wake Ache enough to swallow. A little water touched his lips, but he turned away.

“Should I call the priest?” I asked.

“Ask him when he wakes,” the healer said.

“He may not wake.”

“Then call someone he asked to see.”

I sent the neighbor's son to the foreign settlement for the priest and another boy to find Soliman. I paid the first boy from my own purse. The second would not take money until he had returned. I told him to come back to me, whether he found Soliman or not.

The healer stayed. He asked the family in the back room to keep their child away from the doorway so he could move freely. The mother carried the child outside and sat with her beneath the eave. They had been here only two nights. She had not asked whether they must leave when Ache died. I had been waiting for her to ask.

I changed the cloth beneath his head. He opened his eyes once and looked at my hands. I told him the roof had held through the night. I do not know whether he heard me. His fingers moved against the mat, then rested.

I wanted him to say something I could carry to the people who would come. He had spoken too many promises in his life, and I had spent years asking him to be more exact. Now I found myself wanting one more. I did not ask.

***

Soliman arrived before the priest.

He came alone and stopped at the doorway when he saw the healer. He had brought a bundle wrapped in cloth. Inside were a few pieces of rice cake and a small length of cord, which he said had been left for Ache by a man who used to work at the river gate.

“Did he say what the cord means?” I asked.

“He said the old man would know.”

I put it on the shelf. It had two knots, one tight and one loose. I had no reason to believe Ache would know what they marked. Men had been sending him things for years, certain that his memory would finish the message.

Soliman sat beside the mat. He spoke Ache's name, then his title. Ache did not answer.

“Has he received the water?” Soliman asked me.

“Yes.”

“Was he able to speak?”

“He asked questions. He answered what the priest asked him.”

Soliman looked at the healer. “Will he live?”

The healer said he could not promise another day. Soliman turned back to the mat. I could see that he wanted to argue with someone, but the healer had offered no bargain.

He stayed for a while. He told Ache that the owners of the houses near the landing had been heard separately. Some had refused to come. A few had sent kin to speak, and the interpreter had asked which kin could answer for which ground. The discussion had not ended.

“He asked me to hear them,” Soliman said.

Ache's eyes remained closed.

I wondered whether Soliman was speaking for himself now. I had heard him quarrel with the old man more than once. I had also heard him return the next morning with another question. I knew little about what the two men had promised each other when I was not there.

The priest arrived while Soliman was still seated. His interpreter had come with him. They stood outside until I invited them in. The priest looked at Ache, then at the healer. He asked whether he could pray beside him. I said yes. The priest did not ask me to move the household's things or send the other people away.

He prayed. The interpreter did not repeat every word. I heard the name of the God the priest had taught and the name of the Son. Ache had asked those names to be explained before he received the water. I had heard his questions. I could not tell the priest what he had believed when he closed his eyes.

The healer put two fingers against Ache's neck. He waited, moved them to the wrist, and waited again.

I watched his face. I did not watch the old man's chest. I had watched it too long already.

The healer looked up at me. He said that Ache had died.

The priest continued praying for a short time. Soliman lowered his head. I went to the doorway and told the family waiting outside. The mother drew her child close. The child asked whether she should stop carrying water. I said no. We would need it.

I had expected a sound from the river when the old raha died, though I knew there would be none. A boat pole struck the landing post. Someone called for a basket. The morning market had begun.

***

The first argument over his goods came before we had finished preparing the room.

A man from Ache's wider kin arrived with two others. He had been told of the death at the gate. He said the household must keep its goods together until the family decided who could claim them.

“Which goods?” I asked.

“The things belonging to the old raha.”

“Name them.”

He looked toward the chest.

I opened it. There were folded cloths, a knife, two small measures, and a pouch containing nothing but a length of broken cord. One of the cloths belonged to the family staying with us. The knife had been left by a maker who was coming back for it. The smaller measure was mine.

“Where is the copper bowl?” the man asked.

“Mine,” I said.

“I was told it belonged to his mother.”

“It did. He gave it to me while he lived.”

The man looked at Soliman. “Did you hear him?”

“No,” Soliman said.

The man seemed to take this as an answer in his favor. I called for the neighbor who had witnessed the gift. She came from her washing with her sleeves still wet. She told them what she had heard: Ache had offered the bowl, I had asked whether it was payment or a gift, and he had said it was mine if I accepted it. I had accepted it.

The man said a nephew might have a claim to the old raha's goods. He did not say which nephew. I did not ask Soliman to answer for him.

“If there is such a claim,” I said, “bring the person who makes it. We can speak about the things that were Ache's when he died.”

The man asked where I had put the bowl.

“Among my things.”

“Bring it here so we can see it.”

“No.”

He looked angry. I was frightened enough to feel my hands go cold, but I had watched the old man lose his place in another person's account of a woman's canoe. I would not bring the bowl out merely because someone had named a man who was not there.

Soliman stood. He did not declare the bowl mine or settle the inheritance. He asked the visitor whether he had heard the neighbor's account. The man said he had. Soliman asked whether he had another witness. He said he would return with one.

“Then we can hear that person,” Soliman said.

The man left the chest open. I closed it after him.

There would be other questions. The foreigners had their own ways of counting property and people. The kin who came to the house had theirs. I had lived under this roof long enough to know that a bowl could be held by one person, used by another, and remembered by a third. I would not settle every claim by saying the old raha had once owned it.

***

We prepared him for burial with the help of people who had known him in different years.

I did not choose every part of the rite. The priest spoke with Soliman and with members of the household. Some wanted the prayers they had known since childhood. Others wanted the Christian prayers Ache had accepted before his death. The discussions were not quiet. I asked them to take their arguments outside when the child began to cry. They did.

The priest told us what he believed should be done for a Christian. The others told him what they would and would not do. I carried water, found clean cloth, and made room for the people who came to mourn. The rites were arranged among those present; I cannot say that everyone agreed.

The woman whose canoe had been taken and returned came late in the day. She stood outside the gate until I went to her.

“I heard he died,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Did he ever ask about my husband?”

“He asked whether you had news. I told him I had heard none.”

She looked toward the river. Her canoe was now sound enough to take out on the next tide. Her brother had repaired the binding. She had come because she remembered that Ache had promised to tell anyone who asked where to find her.

“They may ask him again,” she said.

“They cannot.”

“Then they may ask you.”

“What shall I say?”

She gave me her name. I had heard it before, but she spoke it slowly. I repeated it until she was satisfied.

“And the canoe?”

“Yours,” I said.

“If they need it, they can hire it.”

“I will tell them.”

She stayed long enough to put a small coil of rope near the doorway. It was not payment. She said it was for the work of tying a mat or carrying a bundle, if we needed it. I asked whether she wanted it returned. She said I could use it and keep what remained.

I put the rope beside the chest. I did not add it to the old raha's goods.

***

The family in the back room stayed for their third night.

The mother asked whether I wanted them to leave the next morning. She had heard the argument over the chest. She had heard the men say the house must be counted again.

“I offered three nights,” I said. “You have had two.”

“And after tomorrow?”

“We will speak again. I can offer space while the roof holds and the people sleeping here agree. I cannot promise what another claimant may demand.”

She said she would ask her sister's people whether they could make a longer place for her child. She would help carry water in the morning, as she had agreed. We left it there.

The neighbor came to help wash the cups. She asked where I had put the copper bowl. I showed her the shelf among my own things. She touched the dent and asked whether I meant to keep it.

“For now,” I said.

She nodded. She had heard Ache say I could sell it, use it, or give it away. She did not ask me to choose that night.

After the lamps were put out, I went to the front room. The mat where he had died was rolled against the wall. The shelf beside it was empty except for the cord Soliman had brought. I took the cord down. Its two knots were different sizes, and one was so loose it might have been made by a child.

I could not know what the gate worker had meant by them. I could ask him tomorrow, if he came. I laid the cord beside the rope the canoe owner had left. Neither belonged to the old raha merely because it had been brought to his house.

The child came in rubbing her eyes.

“Where will the bowls go?” she asked.

“Which bowls?”

“The two that were in the chest.”

“Both are mine now. One was given to me long ago. The other was his mother's, and he gave it to me before he died.”

She considered this. “Can we use the one with the dent for the rice?”

“Yes. Wash it first.”

She went to the shelf and lifted the copper bowl with both hands. The dent showed where her thumb rested. I watched her carry it toward the water jar, careful not to strike the rim against the door.

I followed with the lamp. The roof above us held.

<summary>Historical Notes &amp; Sources</summary>
<p><strong>The 1572 account.</strong> The anonymous <a href="/raha-ache/sources/relacion-1572/"><em>Relation of the Conquest of the Island of Luzon</em></a>, dated 20 April 1572, describes a deceased Christian Manila chief named <strong>Laya</strong> (Blair and Robertson, <em>The Philippine Islands</em>, vol. 3, printed p. 128) and says his property passed to an <strong>unnamed legitimate nephew</strong> (p. 145; <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html">English edition</a>). The writer belonged to the conquest party. His account of kinship and legitimacy reflects Spanish categories, not a complete record of local households.</p>
<p><strong>Names and identification.</strong> The relation calls the deceased chief <strong>Laya</strong>, not Ache. Connecting him to Ache the Old, named separately in the <a href="/raha-ache/sources/riquel-1571/">1571 Riquel record</a>, is a historical inference. The nephew is not identified in the inspected passage, and this novel does not assert that Sulayman inherited all Ache's property. The original Spanish text printed by Retana in 1898 remains uncollated; no exact baptism or death date is established by the cited English passages.</p>
<p><strong>Baptism and belief.</strong> A Christian death is reported for Laya. Ache's baptismal conversation, the priest, interpreter, water, prayers and private reflections in this chapter are <strong>fictional reconstructions</strong>. Neither a particular request for baptism during illness nor Ache's inner conviction is documented by the passages inspected. The novel does not claim that a priest's assurances controlled the conduct of Spanish officers.</p>
<p><strong>Household testimony.</strong> Awi, her niece, the canoe-owning woman, the disputed record, the copper bowls, roof repair, wages, illness care and inheritance argument are invented. The transfer to Awi's first-person account after Ache's death is a narrative device, not a surviving witness statement. It preserves the distinction between what the 1572 writer reported and what later readers might wish a family had said.</p>
