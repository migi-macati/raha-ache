The path ended at a house with a roof that came low over its steps. Fish bones had been swept into a heap beside the water jar. There were three sleeping mats rolled against the wall, and a fourth laid out where the shade reached it.

A woman sat on that mat with a child across her knees. She was working a thin strip of leaf through the torn edge of a basket. The child watched her hands. When she saw us, she finished the turn before looking up.

“The buyer sent us,” I said.

“He sends people who have eaten his fish.”

“We worked for him.”

“Then he gave you fish.”

She drew the leaf tight. It was an answer, but not to the question I wanted to ask.

Sulad put down our jar. He had carried it from the landing without changing hands. A red mark crossed the base of his thumb.

“We were told you might have room,” he said.

“I might.”

The child turned to look at us. The woman pulled the loose end of the leaf through the basket and bit it off.

“How long?” she asked.

“One night,” I said.

Sulad looked at me. We had not decided what would happen after one night.

“Perhaps two,” I added.

“Then you do not know.”

“We can pay.”

“With what?”

I reached toward the cloth where I kept our last piece of silver. Sulad laid his hand on the jar between us. He did not touch my arm or stop me. I left the silver where it was.

The woman watched my hand.

“Do you know how many people have asked to sleep here since the last boat came in?”

“No.”

“Neither do I. I remember the ones who left owing me.”

She nodded toward the rolled mats. One belonged to her sister, who was away with a sick relative. Another belonged to a man who had promised to come back for his things. She had no word from him, and she would not let us use what he had left.

“The floor under the eaves is dry if the wind stays as it is,” she said. “You may sit there until dark. That costs nothing.”

“Could we sleep there?”

“People who sleep here use my water. Sometimes they use my fire. Sometimes they say they have no money until morning, and in the morning they are gone.”

“We would not leave without paying.”

“You say that before I let you stay.”

I could feel myself reaching for the name of my mother's house. I had used it so often in Maynila that it had become a way of speaking before I knew what I wanted. Here it would tell this woman only that someone far away had once been able to command other people.

Sulad crouched beside the jar.

“Is there work you need done before dark?” he asked.

She looked at his hands, then at mine.

“The water jars need filling. I have a roof edge to tie down before the rain comes. The child is not to climb up there.”

“I can carry water,” Sulad said.

“So can I,” I said.

“Then you can begin by asking where it comes from.”

I had already looked toward the jar near the steps, assuming the water was close. The woman pointed past the house, toward a narrow path that turned behind a stand of trees.

“Not from the landing,” she said. “That water tastes of boats.”

She named a place beyond the trees. I did not catch the word. Sulad asked her to repeat it, and she did, slower this time. He repeated the sound to himself without pretending he understood its meaning.

“How many jars?” he asked.

“Two full ones. The small one too, if the child has emptied it.”

“And for that?” I asked.

“For that I will have water.”

Sulad almost smiled. He looked down before I could be sure.

The woman set aside her basket. “If you bring the water and help tie the roof, I will give you space under the eaves tonight. Not a mat. Not food. You have eaten already, and I have a child to feed.”

“Tomorrow?” I asked.

“Tomorrow you can ask again.”

I had been ready to give her silver for a promise she had not made. Now she offered one night, bounded on every side. I did not like the smallness of it. I liked even less that it was enough to keep us out of the open.

“We agree,” I said.

Sulad stood but did not pick up the jar.

“I will carry the first,” he told the woman. “He will carry the second. If the roof needs both of us, we will work together. But I want to see where we are to sleep before I take the jar.”

She rose with the child on her hip and showed us the strip of hard earth beneath the eaves. There was room for two people lying close, provided the rain did not come sideways. A woven screen hung at one end, frayed where it met the post. At the other end, the roof dipped low enough that I would have to turn on my side.

“No fire there,” she said. “And if my sister returns, you move.”

Sulad bent to test the ground with his palm. He looked at the roof, the open side, and the place where the water would run.

“We can sleep here,” he said.

The woman looked at me for my answer. I had made the first promise. Sulad had made her show us what it bought.

“Yes,” I said.

She gave him the empty jar. He shifted it into the crook of his arm and waited while I took the other.

We had come looking for shelter. We left to earn the right to ask for it after dark.

* * *

The path back from the water was longer with a full jar.

I had noticed a root crossing it on the way out. On the return I caught my foot beneath the same root and nearly let the jar strike a stone. Sulad stopped ahead of me. He did not offer to carry it.

“Move the weight closer,” he said.

“I have it.”

“You had it before you stumbled.”

I shifted the jar against my ribs. Water came over the lip and ran down my side. Sulad waited until I had found my footing, then went on.

At the house, the woman took one look at my wet cloth and asked whether I had filled the jar or bathed in it.

“Both,” Sulad said.

She set the child down and inspected what we had brought. His jar was full. Mine had lost enough water for her to see the difference.

“There are two more jars behind the screen,” she said. “You may fill them tomorrow if you stay.”

I thought she had forgotten her bargain. “We agreed to two today.”

“And you have brought two. I did not say they had to be equally full.”

The child reached for my wet hem. His mother pulled him away before he could put it in his mouth.

She showed us the place where the roof had begun to lift. A strip of binding had parted near the end of a beam. Sulad tested the remaining length with his fingers and asked for something sound to replace it. The woman brought a coil from inside, measured off a piece, and held the rest beyond his reach.

“That is for the other side,” she said. “Do not use it all because this end is easier to see.”

Sulad climbed onto the low platform beside the wall. I held the beam where she directed me. When I pushed too hard it moved against the post.

“Not that way,” she said.

“Which way?”

“Hold it where he can work. You are not trying to lift my house.”

I moved my hands. Sulad drew the new binding through, tested it, then asked her to pull while he secured the end. She knew exactly how much pressure it would bear. I had expected him to instruct her. Instead I watched him listen.

From the landing came three sharp calls, then a longer one.

The woman kept pulling.

“Is someone in trouble?” I asked.

“Someone wants a place,” she said. “They all sound like that when they want a place.”

Another call came, closer. The child ran to the edge of the platform to look down the path.

“Stay where I can see you,” she told him.

We finished the binding. Sulad tested it once more, and the woman stepped back to examine the roof herself. She said we could sleep beneath it when the work was done. She did not invite us inside.

I was wiping my hands on my cloth when a man came up the path carrying an empty basket. He asked her for the use of a rope.

“Which rope?” she asked.

“The one you lent the buyer last month.”

“I did not lend it. He borrowed it without asking and sent back half.”

The man looked past her toward the coil Sulad had just used.

“That one is not for boats,” she said.

He opened his mouth, then shut it. He went back down the path without the rope.

The woman watched him leave. “If you are going to the landing,” she said to us, “do not offer my things to anyone.”

“We wouldn't,” Sulad said.

I had already been wondering whether there was a way to help.

We took our own jar and went to see what the calls had meant.

The trader's boat was still there. His crew had shifted the rejected baskets away from the edge, but the space where we had sat during the crossing was full of goods again. Beyond it, another boat waited with its bow turned toward the landing. It had not been tied to a post. Two men held it off with poles, each taking a step when the hull drifted closer.

A third man stood near the front. He was young enough that I might have taken him for a rower if the others had not kept looking to him before they answered the men ashore.

“Only until morning,” he called.

The buyer stood beside the post. “That is what the last man said.”

“The last man is not here.”

“His rope is. I cut it off myself.”

The buyer repeated the request for the men ashore, pointing at the post. I understood his words; the young man's answer had reached me in pieces. I had been taking the pauses for anger when some of them were spent making sure each side had heard.

The young man looked down at the rope trailing from the post, then at the man holding the boat with his pole.

“Ask him whether he wants it,” he said.

The man at the pole shook his head. “I want ground under my feet.”

“Then we agree on one thing.”

The buyer did not laugh.

A narrow bundle lay across the waiting boat's middle. Its covering had been pulled back to show folded cloth, with another bundle beneath it. A crewman was pressing the wet edge of the covering against his knee, trying to wring out the water without letting it reach the cloth.

The young man noticed me looking. When he spoke to me, he chose shorter words than he had used across the water.

“Have you bought something?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then I have nothing to sell you.”

“I was looking at the covering.”

“It was dry when we left.”

The crewman with the bundle raised his head. “It was torn when we left. I told you.”

“You told me after we had pushed off.”

“I told Nima before we loaded it.”

A fourth man, standing farther back in the boat, turned at his name. He had one arm wrapped around a pole and was using his free hand to keep a jar from rolling against the bundles.

“I told him,” Nima said. “He said we would cover it again before the weather changed.”

The young man looked from one to the other. For a moment I thought he would order them silent.

Instead he said, “Then I was wrong twice. I still need somewhere to put it.”

Nima shifted the jar away from the cloth. “You can put your own goods on the wet ground. That one is my sister's.”

The buyer pointed to the baskets on the landing. “And these belong to people who will come for them. If you put wet cloth beside them, who will answer when something is spoiled?”

“We will keep it apart,” the young man said.

“You say that while you are in the boat.”

A fisherman carrying a net came between them. He glanced at the waiting vessel and stopped.

“Is that my place?” he asked the buyer.

“It is still your place.”

The fisherman looked at the young man. “I go out again before dawn.”

“I will leave before then.”

“On whose word?”

“Mine.”

The fisherman held up the net. “This needs drying. It has no use for your word.”

The young man rubbed his face with both hands. He seemed to be trying to keep count of several things at once: the wet cloth, the men holding the poles, the buyer's baskets, the fisherman and the light leaving the shore.

I knew that look. I had seen it on my mother when a messenger arrived before the first had gone.

“Could they bring the bundles ashore without tying the boat?” I asked Sulad.

“Where would they put them?”

“There is room beneath the roof where we're staying.”

He looked at me until I remembered whose roof it was.

“We haven't even slept there,” he said.

“I could ask.”

“You could. You should ask her before you tell them.”

The man Nima heard us. He had been listening while the others argued.

“Whose roof?” he called.

“No one's who has agreed,” Sulad answered.

The young man turned toward us. “Then do not make us a promise we cannot keep.”

“I was trying to help,” I said.

“Everyone here is trying to help someone.” He pointed toward Nima. “He is trying to keep his sister's cloth dry. The fisherman is trying to keep his net ready. I am trying to keep my boat out of their way.”

“And the buyer?” I asked.

“He is trying not to be the man everyone blames tomorrow.”

The buyer's expression changed. I thought for a moment he might let them tie.

“Then you understand me,” he said. “Keep your boat off the post until we have agreed what you will put ashore.”

The young man nodded once. Nima did not.

“We cannot stand out here while they count every fold,” Nima said.

“We may have to,” the young man replied.

He said it quietly enough that the men ashore almost missed it. I did not.

A small wave moved beneath the boat. The man at the forward pole lost his footing for an instant and recovered it. He looked toward the young man, waiting for a decision. No order came.

Sulad picked up our jar.

“We should go back,” he said.

I looked at the cloth, then at the strip of shade beneath the woman's roof. There was room there for two people. I had measured it with my eyes. I had not measured it for anyone else's goods.

The young man called to the buyer again. This time he asked what would count as an agreement.

I stayed long enough to hear the buyer begin to answer. Then I followed Sulad up the path.

* * *

The woman had set the smaller jar inside when we returned. The child was asleep with one hand caught in the edge of her cloth. She had not yet unrolled a mat.

“Is the roof holding?” Sulad asked.

“For now.” She looked from him to me. “What did you find at the water?”

“A boat with cloth getting wet,” I said.

“Most boats are wet.”

“The cloth is meant to stay dry.”

“Then someone should have covered it.”

“They did. The covering tore.”

She laid the child down, eased her cloth free, and waited. I could hear people calling below us. I had come back to ask a question, but the answer seemed to be waiting in the house already.

“There is room beneath your eaves,” I said.

“For two people who carried my water.”

“Only until the cloth can be covered again.”

“Whose cloth?”

“A man's sister's.”

“Where is his sister?”

“I don't know.”

“Does he want it here?”

“I haven't asked him.”

“Then you have asked the wrong person first.”

I looked at the ground we had measured for sleeping. There was no mat on it, and no fire. I had thought of the space as ours because she had said we could use it. Now I saw the basket she had been repairing, set beside the post to keep it out of the path, and the marks where the rain had washed soil down from the roof.

“I could pay you,” I said.

“You could. You would still need to ask the people whose things you want to put here.”

Sulad was beside the water jars. He had taken the lid off ours to see how much remained.

“We should leave it,” he said.

“It's getting darker.”

“Yes.”

He replaced the lid. I waited for him to say more. He did not.

Below us another call rose from the landing, shorter this time. The woman picked up her basket and sat where she could see the path.

“If you go back,” she said, “do not tell anyone I am keeping their goods.”

“I won't.”

“And if someone comes asking, I will tell them the same.”

We went down again. Sulad carried nothing. I had brought our small piece of silver out of my cloth and kept it closed in my fist.

At the landing the visiting boat was still being held away from the post. One man had changed places with another at the forward pole. The fisherman had spread part of his net over the high ground beside the baskets. Its weighted edge lay where I had imagined the cloth could be set.

The young man was speaking with the buyer. Nima stood behind him, one hand pressed to the wet covering.

“The net could be moved,” I said.

The fisherman heard me. “It could. Where would you put it?”

I had no answer ready. I pointed to a man waiting near the baskets with a carrying pole.

“Would you help lift the cloth clear of the water?” I asked him.

“For what?”

I opened my hand.

He looked at the silver, then at the boat. “I can hold the bow while they shift it. I cannot tell you where it may lie.”

“That would give them time.”

“It would give them one more pole.”

I gave him the silver. He took it and stepped into the shallows, calling to the man at the bow to make room. The two of them steadied the boat against a small wash. The crewman holding the cloth lifted one corner of the covering, and Nima caught it before it dragged through the water.

“Bring the bundle up,” I called.

Nima turned. “Who said we were bringing it up?”

“There's dry ground.”

“Where?”

I pointed beside the net.

The fisherman planted his foot there. “That is where I put my catch when I come in.”

“Only for a little while,” I said.

“You do not know when I come in.”

The buyer looked at the man I had paid, now braced against the bow. “Did you hire him to take my place?”

“No,” I said. “To help.”

“Then let him help with what he agreed to.”

Nima had pulled the covering back over the bundle. He kept his hand on it as if I might reach across the water and take it.

“My sister told me to bring this to the buyer she named,” he said. “If I set it on another man's ground and it is spoiled, will you tell her that you meant well?”

I had heard most of his words. I asked him to repeat the last part. He pointed first at the bundle, then at me, and said it again more slowly. The young man said nothing while I tried to answer.

“I wasn't taking it,” I said.

“You were telling us where to put it.”

The young man turned to the fisherman. “Is there ground you will allow us to use?”

“Not that ground.”

“Then we are where we were,” he said.

The man I had hired held the bow until the crew shifted their weight and the boat stopped striking against the wash. He came ashore dripping to the knees. I looked at the silver in his hand.

“You asked me to hold her steady,” he said. “I did.”

I had. He had done the work, and I had no claim on the payment.

Sulad stood near the path, away from the people still bargaining. He had watched without offering a word on my behalf. When I reached him, he looked toward the boat.

“You paid for another pair of hands,” he said.

“I thought they could unload.”

“You thought the ground was empty.”

“It looked empty.”

“It wasn't.”

He turned uphill. I followed him. Behind us the calls began again, and I could no longer tell which side was asking the other to wait.

* * *

Sulad stopped halfway up the path.

I nearly walked into him. The silver was gone, the boat was still in the water, and I wanted to be under the roof before someone below thought of another thing to ask of me.

“What?” I said.

“You called to the man with the pole before you knew where the cloth could go.”

“I paid him for holding the boat. He held it.”

“He did.”

Sulad looked downhill. Through the trees I could see the visiting boat shift away from the bank, then back again. A man at the stern braced his pole in the mud.

“You keep finding work for other people,” Sulad said.

“I was the one who paid.”

“This time.”

I tried to pass. He stepped aside, but did not follow. When I turned, he was looking back toward the house as though he might leave me to finish the walk alone.

“We still have a place to sleep,” I said.

“For tonight.”

“I know.”

“You say that as though it means tomorrow too.”

The woman's roof appeared through the leaves. I could see the basket she had been mending. She had warned me not to send anyone to her house with the cloth, and I had not. It seemed an achievement until I remembered I had brought nothing back to show for the silver.

“Are you going home?” I asked him.

“Not on a boat I haven't found.”

“Then come up.”

“I will when I decide to.”

I went the last few steps alone. The woman was crouched beside her jar, scraping grit from its lip with a sliver of wood.

“Did they bring the cloth?” she asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

“The boat is still there.”

“It was there before you went down.”

I could hear the child moving inside. She lifted the jar, saw that the bottom was clean, and put it back.

“You can sleep where I told you,” she said. “The ground will be drier if you leave it alone until night.”

I thanked her. She went inside without asking whether I had made myself useful.

Sulad came up the path and stood at the edge of the roof's shade. He did not look at me. After a while the child began to fuss and the woman called for us to move out of the way of the door. We moved.

Below, the calls had changed. They were shorter and farther apart. The boat had not left.

“The buyer knows where people can stand,” I said. “He could ask the fisherman again.”

“He already asked.”

“Then I could help move the buyer's baskets.”

“You could.”

“There are two of us.”

Sulad turned toward me.

I heard it after I said it. I had meant that two men could carry more than one. He had heard that I had counted his hands before he had offered them.

“I didn't mean—”

“You did.”

I wanted to say I had only been thinking aloud. The fisherman would have heard the same words if I had spoken them at the landing. He would have made plans around them, as I had made plans around the woman's roof.

“Come down with me,” I said. “You don't have to carry anything.”

“I know.”

He picked up our jar. He came because he wanted to hear what I would say, not because I had persuaded him.

At the landing, the man I had paid was gone. The buyer was counting the baskets nearest the path, touching each one as he passed. The fisherman had lifted a corner of his net to shake out the grit. No one had moved the bundle of cloth. Nima was still beside it.

I waited until the buyer looked up.

“Could the baskets be shifted to that hard place above the water?” I asked.

“Why?”

“So they could use the post long enough to mend the covering.”

The buyer looked at the fisherman. “You have already heard his answer.”

“I can move them,” I said. “We can put them back when they're done.”

The fisherman caught the last word.

“We?”

I looked at Sulad. He held the jar against his hip.

“I haven't said I will,” he told the fisherman.

The buyer's fingers stopped on the rim of a basket.

“I spoke too soon,” I said. “I meant I could move them.”

“And carry them back?” the fisherman asked.

“If he leaves before the boat is free, will you still do it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you say we?”

Because I had thought Sulad would stay. Because I had seen him carry water, and assumed he would carry whatever came next. I did not give the fisherman those answers. He was waiting for one he could use.

“I shouldn't have spoken for him,” I said. “You have my word for my hands. Not his.”

Sulad's expression did not change. He had not asked for an apology. He had asked me to stop making bargains he would have to keep.

The buyer moved a basket with his foot, no farther than the width of his sandal.

“Even if you carry these,” he said, “what will the boat do with the space?”

I started to point toward the bundle. Nima saw the movement and leaned across it. I let my hand fall.

“The cloth stays with its people,” I said.

The young man called from the bow. The buyer made him repeat himself, then turned to the fisherman.

“They need the post while they mend the covering. The cloth stays aboard.”

Nima interrupted. The buyer listened again.

“And no one touches the bundle unless Nima asks,” he added.

The fisherman looked from his net to the baskets. “And my ground?”

The buyer asked whether the post could be used while the covering was secured. The fisherman looked at the boat and did not answer.

Sulad held the jar beside me. For once I let the silence belong to someone else.

* * *

The fisherman lifted the wet end of his net from the ground and found a stone caught in it. He shook the mesh until the stone fell out. Then he put the net down again, in the same place.

I had expected him to move it when the buyer asked. He had listened to the request without saying no. I was learning that silence could last longer than an answer.

The visiting boat still lay beyond the post. Its men had changed hands on the poles so often that the wood shone where they gripped it. Nima sat beside the covered bundle, keeping one foot against it when the boat rolled. I could see a dark line creeping along the covering toward the fold beneath.

The young man who led them came ashore in the shallows. He did not come farther than the water allowed.

“How long?” the buyer asked him.

The young man held up two fingers, then one. The buyer frowned. I had taken the gesture to mean the rest of the night. When the buyer repeated the request to the fisherman, I heard that it meant only enough time to mend the covering and shift the bundle higher inside the boat.

“Not until morning?” the fisherman asked.

“No,” said the buyer.

He turned to the young man and said it again, slower, pointing first at the low sun and then at the post. The young man repeated the limit and showed where he would take his rope afterward. I understood his words this time. I was less certain he understood the fisherman's answer.

The fisherman set his net across his knees.

“If they use the post, I cannot spread this here.”

The buyer pointed toward his rejected baskets. “I have dry ground on that side. The baskets must be moved before they can use it.”

“By whom?” the fisherman asked.

The buyer looked at me.

I shook my head before he spoke. I had no silver left to buy another man's help.

“I can move them,” I said. “My own hands.”

Sulad stood beside me. I had not turned to him.

Nima called from the boat. The buyer repeated his condition: the cloth would stay aboard, untouched. The fisherman pointed to the baskets.

“Who moves them?”

“I do,” I said.

“And who takes them back before I come in?”

“I will,” I said, too quickly.

“How do you know when I come in?”

I looked at the buyer. He did not help me.

Sulad set our jar beside the path and stepped forward.

“I can help carry the baskets back,” he said. “If I choose to stay until the boat is free. I am not agreeing to anything after that.”

The fisherman looked from him to me. “And if he leaves?”

“Then I carry them,” I said.

Sulad glanced at me. He was not smiling. It was the first answer I had given that did not borrow his hands.

The buyer made us show where the baskets would go. I carried one to the strip of hard ground above the water, where the fisherman could still spread his net without laying it over them. The buyer moved it half a pace farther from the path. Only then did he nod.

The young man came close enough to hear the buyer's repeated terms. He said them back: his boat might use the post until the covering was secured; the cloth would remain aboard; no one was lending the ground for storage. The fisherman made him point to the place where his rope would be removed. Nima leaned forward and repeated the part about the bundle. He did not look satisfied, but he stopped objecting.

“Now,” the fisherman said.

The visiting crew brought their own rope. No one went to the house for the woman's coil. The fisherman stood where he could see the post while the line was made fast. The boat eased toward the bank, and the men with poles finally let their shoulders rest.

I took up another basket.

There were fewer than I had feared. They were heavier than they looked. By the time I had moved the last one, my palms stung where the wicker had rubbed them. Sulad had carried three. He had said he would help if he chose; he had chosen three, and stopped.

Nima lifted the edge of the covering while another man slid a board beneath the bundle. They raised it clear of the wet floor of the boat. The young man held the covering against the wind as Nima drew its torn edges together. Their fingers worked quickly, but the repair was ugly. I could see light through one gap when they finished.

“It will need doing again,” I said.

The young man looked at the gap, then at me.

“Yes,” he said.

He turned to Nima and said something I did not catch. Nima answered sharply. The buyer repeated only part of it for the fisherman: they would not leave the cloth on this shore, even if the buyer offered to take it.

“Did he offer?” I asked.

“No,” the buyer said. “That is why they can agree.”

The fisherman was already spreading his net. He did not wait for thanks. The visiting men eased the rope from the post and pushed off into water deep enough to hold them away from the bank. Nima sat beside the bundle again. The covering stayed above the dark line of wetness.

The young man remained at the bow until the boat was clear.

“Will you go toward Brunei?” I asked.

He looked toward the buyer, as if weighing whether my words needed repeating. Then he answered with the same short speech he had used when we first met.

“Not for you.”

“I wasn't asking you to go for me.”

“Good.”

He watched his men settle the poles.

Sulad had gone to fetch our jar. I went back to the baskets and carried them from the hard ground to the place the buyer had chosen for them. He moved the last one half a pace and nodded. By then the fisherman had spread his net again. I waited until Sulad returned before we started uphill. Behind us the rope was coiled inside the visiting boat. The post stood bare.

* * *

The woman had brought the basket inside by the time we returned. The child slept with his face against her knee. She was drawing a strip through the same broken place in the basket, and I wondered how many times she had already repaired it.

Sulad set down our jar.

“Did they take your rope?” she asked.

“No,” he said.

She looked at me.

“They had their own.”

“And the cloth?”

“Still in the boat.”

“Good. You can sleep where I showed you.”

I had expected another question. I stood with my hands open, unable to find anything useful to do with them. The wicker had left a pale cross of scratches across each palm.

The woman saw them. She put her basket aside and reached for the jar.

“Wash before you lie down. I don't want blood on the screen.”

“There isn't any blood.”

“Then it will be easy to wash off.”

She poured a little water into a shallow bowl and left it at the edge of the platform. She did not offer us the bowl itself; it belonged where she had set it. Sulad rinsed his hands and waited for me. When I lowered mine into the water, the scratches burned.

“The roof held,” he told her.

“I know,” she said. “I live under it.”

A wind moved through the trees behind the house. The newly bound edge made no sound. Somewhere below us a man shouted from the landing and another answered. I had spent most of the evening listening for the visiting crew's voices. Now I could no longer distinguish them from the others.

The woman carried her child inside. We lay under the eaves with our bundles between us and the path. Sulad turned on his side to keep clear of the screen. I had to bend one knee to fit beneath the lowest part of the roof.

“You told the fisherman we would move the buyer's baskets,” Sulad said.

“I took it back.”

“After I asked what happened if I left.”

“Yes.”

He waited. I could hear the little bowl being emptied inside the house.

“You had already answered for me once,” he said.

I had thought he would be angry about the silver. It would have been easier to say I had wasted it and be done.

“I know.”

“No. You know now. At the landing you didn't ask.”

I rubbed a thumb across the sore place in my other palm.

“I thought you would help.”

“I did help.”

“You carried three.”

“I chose three.”

I turned to face him. His eyes were open. There was barely enough light to see the line of his cheek.

“Would you have left?” I asked.

“I was deciding.”

“And now?”

“Now I'm trying to sleep.”

He drew the edge of his cloth over his shoulder. I listened to the house settle around us. I wanted him to say he would still come with me in the morning. He had already told me once that he might want to go home. Asking again would not make him less free to do it.

Before daylight the child began to cry. The woman got up and walked him from one end of the house to the other. We had been given a place to sleep, not a night without noise. Sulad lay still until the crying stopped.

When the sky began to lighten, he sat up and examined our bundles.

“Do we have anything to eat?” I asked.

“Not unless you saved something.”

I had not. He tied his cloth and picked up the jar. I went to thank the woman. She was sitting on the step with the child asleep again against her shoulder.

“May we come back tonight?” I asked.

“You may come back to ask.”

“Will you need more water?”

“I will always need water.”

She looked at Sulad, who was waiting at the path.

“If you go to the landing, tell the buyer his baskets are still in the place he chose. He sent a boy to look before sunrise.”

“Did he move them?” I asked.

“No. That is why he sent the boy.”

We went down. The fisherman was lifting his net from the ground, drawing one edge through his fingers to find the places that needed mending. The post stood free. The buyer had already begun arranging his baskets for the day's work.

Beyond the shallows the visiting boat moved slowly on its poles. I could not tell whether it had returned during the night or waited beyond the landing after its rope was taken down. Its covering had been folded over the bundle and weighted at the corners. Nima sat beside it, watching the shore. The young man who had argued with the fisherman stood near the bow.

I raised my hand. He looked toward the buyer, then brought the boat close enough for us to speak without shouting.

“You are still here,” he said.

“So are you.”

“Not for long.”

“I wanted to ask your name.”

He looked at Sulad before answering. Sulad was asking the buyer whether we might fill our jar from the smaller vessel beside the baskets. The buyer nodded, and Sulad bent to pour. He had not come to hear another bargain.

“Mulaw,” the young man said.

I repeated it. He corrected the way I had said the first part. I tried again.

“And yours?”

“Ache.”

“Is that what they call you here?”

“It is what I am called.”

He looked at my hands. The marks from the baskets were still visible.

“You worked yesterday,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Then they know something about you here.”

I thought he was making fun of me. He did not smile.

“Nima's sister is waiting for her cloth,” he said. “He has waited long enough. We have another place to reach.”

“Will you come back?”

“If the work brings us back.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then I won't.”

Nima called to him from the middle of the boat. Mulaw answered without taking his eyes from the shore.

“Ask the buyer when the boats come,” he told me. “He hears more than I do. If he tells you one is coming, ask the people aboard before you spend anything.”

I looked toward the buyer. He was busy with a man who wanted to inspect the baskets and had no reason to interrupt his work for me.

“I will,” I said.

Mulaw moved to the pole. I had wanted him to offer something that would survive his departure: a name to speak elsewhere, a friend who would carry us, a debt that could be collected. He had given me his name and advice that cost him nothing.

The boat moved away from the post. Nima kept one hand on the bundle until they were beyond the shallows.

Sulad came back with our jar. He had filled it without asking me to hold it steady.

“Who was that?” he asked.

“Mulaw.”

“Does he have a boat for us?”

“He has his own.”

Sulad shifted the jar against his side.

“Then we should find breakfast.”

The buyer was still counting baskets. I waited until he finished one stack before asking whether he needed hands.
