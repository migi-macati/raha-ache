The first time I brought Jamil a count without leaving a space for something I had forgotten, he did not look pleased.

He turned the piece of wood in his hands, called the owner of the third boat back from the steps, and asked him whether he had agreed to carry six baskets or seven.

"Six," the man said.

I pointed to the mark I had made. "Six."

"And the seventh?"

"The seventh belongs to his brother's boat. I put it on the other edge."

The owner took the wood and inspected it as though the marks might be a kind of trick. He had agreed to the count, but he had not agreed that I should keep it.

"Can your brother read these?" Jamil asked.

"No," the owner said. "But I can tell him."

Jamil returned the wood. "Then tell him now."

That had been nine mornings before the boats went out to the outer landing.

In those mornings I learned how many ways a man could say yes without promising anything. He could agree to carry a load but not to unload it. He could accept a return fare while refusing to wait for a passenger. He could offer his boat if the water rose before midday, and be gone with another man's cargo when it did not. Once I marked a jar as delivered because I had seen it lifted from a boat. Hasan took me back to the landing and showed me the same jar standing beneath a roof, where its owner had refused it for a cracked lip.

I scratched out the mark. The cut remained visible beneath the new one.

On the fourth morning I asked a boy from the timber bank whether Sulad had returned. The boy had been sent upriver for a replacement wedge. He said Sulad had stayed with the men clearing a fallen trunk from the channel beyond the second bend. They would be paid when the owner inspected the passage. A man who left before the work was counted might lose his share.

"Did he send anything for the witnesses?" I asked.

"He said he would speak when his work was finished. He did not give me words to carry."

I nearly told the boy to fetch him. I wanted Sulad's account entered beside mine before Jamil sent me farther from the landing. But the boy had brought news of a job, not permission to take a man from it. I left the space for Sulad on the witness board empty. He had not refused to speak. He had not agreed to speak that day, either.

After that I stopped making the marks while men were speaking. I listened first. I asked them to say what they meant again. When they had gone, I made the cuts and went back to see whether I had understood.

The morning of the convoy, I had three pieces of wood laid beside one another on a mat in Jamil's room. There were no gaps on the first two. The third held a space beside the name of the man who had agreed to carry the last load, because his crew had not yet come.

Jamil sat with a trader named Hamzah, whose boat had been drawn up beside the landing for two days. Hamzah owned the cargo but not every basket in it. He had borrowed space from two other merchants and was already tired of answering questions about which bundles belonged to whom.

"Rice in the lower baskets," he told me, touching the marks with a thick nail. "Cloth above them, where it will not take water. The resin goes beneath the cover at the stern. I will not have it pressed against the cloth."

"Will you unload at the outer landing?"

"My men will carry the baskets to the bank. The people who bought them must bring their own hands beyond that."

"And the return?"

"If the buyers have paid. I will not bring an unpaid load back because you have made a mark for the hour."

I turned the wood. "I have marked the return as uncertain."

"Good. Leave it uncertain."

A young man came through the doorway carrying a length of rope across his shoulder. He set it down without making a sound. The rope was still damp; it left a dark curve on the floor.

"This is Nasir," Hamzah said. "He will count the baskets as they come off. Ask him what the men have agreed."

Nasir looked younger than I had expected from the way Hamzah said his name. His forearms were scratched and one of his fingers had a pale scar running across the nail. He took the wood from me and read the marks by having me name them.

"The man at the stern gets a share for steering," he said. "The two carrying men are paid for the outward load. If the buyers make us wait, they pay for the waiting. Hamzah said so yesterday."

"Did the buyers agree?"

Nasir looked at Hamzah.

"They agreed to pay when the baskets arrive," Hamzah answered.

"That is not the same," I said.

"No," Nasir said, before Hamzah could answer. "I told him."

Hamzah rubbed the side of his jaw. "You told me twice."

"Then he has heard it twice."

I liked him for that. I had been told to speak plainly to owners and had found that speaking plainly was easier when someone else began.

"Will you come back with the boat?" I asked.

"If Hamzah brings it back."

"You have another place to be?"

"My sister is waiting for her share of the rice we sold last week. She gave me her basket to carry because she could not leave her stall. If I return after dark she will have closed it, and tomorrow she will say I made her wait for her own grain."

"She would be right," Hamzah said.

Nasir gave him a short look. "You told her I would be back before dark."

"I said I expected you."

"She heard you say it."

I held the wood between my fingers. I had no mark for a sister's expectation, and no place to put one without making the return look more certain than it was.

"Write what I have agreed," Hamzah said. "Not what my sister or his sister hopes."

Nasir took the rope from the floor and carried it out. I heard him speaking to someone on the steps, asking whether the stern cleat had been checked.

Jamil waited until the sound of his voice had moved away.

"What did you leave out?" he asked.

"That Nasir needs to be back before dark."

"Did Hamzah agree to bring him back before dark?"

"No."

"Then you may tell Nasir what is written. You cannot make Hamzah promise it."

I looked at the third piece of wood, at the empty place for the crew that had not arrived. Nine mornings of counting had made the work less confusing. I had begun to believe that anything I could understand clearly enough could be settled before a boat left the bank.

Outside, Safi's voice rose from the lower landing. He was arguing with a man about the weight of a water jar. I could hear the man laugh, then stop laughing.

Safi came in without waiting to be called. His hair was wet and there was a red line across one palm where he had been pulling a rope.

"The small boat is ready," he said. "The steersman wants to know whether he is taking the inner water or the long way round."

"The long way," Jamil said. "He is to keep the cargo boats in sight."

"He says he can bring us through the inner cut before the sun reaches the roof."

"He is not carrying the cargo."

Safi glanced at my pieces of wood. "Ache has put the hours down."

"I have put the agreed hours down," I said.

"Then he can tell you which hour the boats will reach the point."

I opened my mouth. The passage had been described to me by three different men. One said the inner cut was shallow only when the water was low. Another said a loaded hull could pass if its steersman knew where the sand ended. The third had never taken a cargo boat through it and thought we should leave it alone.

"I can tell you what the owners expect," I said. "Not when the water will let them through."

Safi looked at me a moment longer than the answer required. Then he nodded.

"I will tell the steersman."

It was the first time he had accepted one of my answers without making me defend it.

A man appeared in the doorway, holding his cap in both hands. Hasan was behind him. The man's trousers were wet to the knee and one side of his face had been darkened by the sun.

"He came from the outer reach," Hasan said. "He says he saw two boats beyond the wooded point before dawn."

The man stepped inside. "I saw them from the bank where we keep our traps. They were not fishing. No nets, no baskets. One had a dark cover over the middle."

"How many men?" Jamil asked.

"I could not count. They were too far off."

"Armed?"

"I saw long things standing above the side. They might have been poles. They might have been spears. I did not go nearer."

"Did they move?"

"One turned toward the bay. The other stayed where it was. I went back for my traps. When I looked again, I could not see either."

Hasan asked him where he had stood. The man described a fallen tree above the mud, then the mouth of a narrow creek. Hasan repeated the places until the man said he had understood.

Jamil sent for water. The man drank and went out to wait beneath the roof.

"He did not say he saw an attack," Safi said.

"He did not," Hasan answered.

"Nor that the men were armed."

"No. He saw something long. He could not tell what it was."

"Two boats waiting without nets," I said.

Hasan turned to me. "He saw no nets. He could not see what was under the cover."

Before I could answer, another man came up the steps. He was older, with salt dried white along the edge of his sleeve. He had brought his own boat in from the outer landing that morning. He said he had passed a trader's hull on the seaward side of the point after sunrise, one he recognized by its patched covering and a pale strip along the bow.

"Did you see the other boat?" Jamil asked.

"I saw only the trader. I came in by the wider water. There is no reason to take the inside of the point when the current is against you."

"Was he armed?"

"I saw two men at the stern and one working near the cover. They did not call to me."

"Did you see where he had come from?"

"No. He was already moving when I saw him."

The two reports lay before us like pieces of wood cut from different trees. The fisherman had seen two boats before dawn, but could not name them. The returning trader had seen one after sunrise, and thought he knew it.

Jamil asked the second man how far from the point he had passed. Then he asked him to describe the pale strip again. He listened until the man had finished.

"I have seen that trader here," Jamil said. "He has carried resin and dried fish to the outer landing."

"Then one of the boats may have been his," Hasan said.

"The other may have been a fishing boat."

"Or it may not."

Jamil rubbed his thumb along the edge of my third counting stick.

"We cannot hold every cargo for a report that cannot name the boats," he said. "Hamzah's buyers have been waiting two days. The supplies at the outer landing are already short. We will send the three vessels together."

Safi looked toward the steps. "And if the fisherman was right?"

"The small boat will go with them. You will watch the approaches. If you see a craft you cannot account for, bring the cargo boats back before they reach it."

"What if it comes between them?"

"They are to stay close enough to hear one another."

Hasan had not spoken. Jamil looked at him.

"You think I am taking the wrong report?"

"I think the later witness recognized one boat. He did not see the water behind the point. His account does not tell us what became of the second."

Jamil listened without interrupting.

"I know," he said. "We have a man who has seen something and a man who has recognized something. I will act on what can be named. I will answer for it."

He gave me the third piece of wood. I could feel the old cuts on its edge.

"Go to each owner and repeat the agreement. Three boats. The small boat watches the seaward side. The two cargo boats stay within call of it and of each other. No one takes the inner cut. If anyone sees a vessel he does not know, all three turn back together. You will not change the order of the boats or the passage. Is that clear?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

I did. He made me say it a second time, without looking at the marks.

"And if a steersman asks whether he may save time by going inside the point?"

"I tell him the order is to stay together by the outer passage."

"You do not decide for him. He owns his hands and knows his water. But you will not carry a different order in my name."

Safi stood beside the doorway. He had heard every word.

I went down to the boats. Hamzah's men were loading the last baskets. Nasir was kneeling at the stern, feeling under a coil of rope for a loose end. He had taken his sandals off and put them beneath the seat, where they would stay dry.

"Has Jamil settled the passage?" he asked.

"Yes. All three together, outside the point."

"And the return?"

"Your owner has not promised an hour."

Nasir made a face. "I know what he has promised. I asked what Jamil said."

"He said the boats must come back together if anything strange approaches."

Nasir stood and looked past me toward the water. Safi's lighter boat was moving away from the landing, then coming back in a short turn while its steersman tried the feel of the loaded current.

"That will take longer," Nasir said.

"It may."

"My sister will ask why."

"Tell her I was the one who carried the instruction."

"She does not know you."

"She can ask who I am."

He smiled, though I could not tell whether he thought I had made a joke. He climbed over the baskets and began tying the cover down.

I repeated the instruction to the other cargo owner. I repeated it to Safi's steersman, who said he had heard it already from Safi. I made the last mark on the wood and returned it to Jamil before anyone cast off.

He inspected the cuts and asked whether all three owners had agreed. I told him what each had said. One had asked to stop at a fishing landing on the return. Jamil told me that was his business after the cargo was delivered, provided the other boats did not have to wait.

I watched him add a small mark beside that owner's name. He handed the wood back to me.

The men began pushing the boats clear. The water had risen against the lower steps and the rope that held Hamzah's stern floated free of the post. Nasir caught it before it slid beneath the hull.

I stepped into the last cargo boat while its bow was still against the landing. Safi stood at the bow of the lighter vessel, speaking with its steersman. He looked across at me and raised his hand. I raised mine.

The three boats moved out from the landing in the order Jamil had chosen. I had repeated every word correctly. The owners had heard me. There was nothing missing from the count.

When our boat passed the far steps, I looked down at the wood in my hand. I could see where I had marked the expected hour of arrival. The wider passage was meant to keep the boats together. I also knew that if one of them took the shorter water, the others might reach the outer landing before the buyers lost patience.

I put the wood beneath my seat.

---

The last cargo boat belonged to a man called Bakar. He had a short beard, two missing teeth, and a habit of asking a question after everyone else had agreed to something. He had asked whether the outer landing would have enough men to lift his jars. He had asked whether Jamil would pay if the buyers refused a cracked one. When I stepped aboard, he asked where I meant to sit.

"Where I can see the other boats."

"Then not there."

I had put my hand on the raised board beside the steersman. Bakar lifted it away.

"Sit behind the jars. My man needs that board to move his feet."

I sat behind the jars.

We passed the last houses on piles and entered a stretch where the bank fell lower and the trees stood close to the water. I could still hear a woman calling from a landing behind us. The words became indistinct, but the sound carried farther than I expected.

Safi's lighter boat took the seaward side. It had room for men to turn and look without stepping over cargo. Hamzah's heavier boat followed a little nearer the bank. We kept behind Hamzah, close enough to hear Nasir call when a loose basket shifted.

For a while the three boats moved as though they had been tied to one another by an invisible rope. When Safi's steersman eased his stroke, Hamzah eased his. When Hamzah slowed to clear a floating branch, Bakar's man let our bow fall away from his stern. No one had to tell them to do it.

I kept the count-stick beneath my knee. I had promised Jamil I would remember what the marks meant, and I was afraid the water would take it if I held it in my hand.

The man steering Bakar's boat was called Tuma. He had a broad shoulder that pressed against the stern post whenever he leaned to see past the jars. He had been quiet at the landing. Now he spoke to the water more often than to us.

"Not that side," he said once, and shifted his weight.

I thought he meant me.

"Not you," he said. "The current."

Bakar laughed. Tuma did not.

We came to a place where the river widened and the low bank broke into several green tongues. One was no more than a strip of mud with young trees growing on it. Beyond it I could see water shining between the branches, almost straight toward the outer landing.

"The inner cut," I said.

Bakar looked. "One of them."

"Is it deep enough?"

"For this boat?"

"For Hamzah's."

"Ask Hamzah."

"Do you know?"

"I know what it looked like when I came through last month. The water has moved since then."

Tuma shifted the steering oar. "I took a boat through yesterday. Not Hamzah's boat. A smaller one."

"Could his boat pass now?"

"Probably."

"Why probably?"

"Because I have not stood on his bottom and felt where it lies."

He said this without anger. I had begun to dislike the word probably. It made a man sound as if he knew enough to be useful while leaving himself room to be wrong.

Ahead, Hamzah's boat rode low beneath its covered load. Nasir stood near the stern, holding a rope against his leg. The current pushed the bow toward the opening, and the steersman corrected with a short movement.

"He is already going that way," I said.

"He is holding his bow clear of the bank," Tuma answered. "That is not the same as taking the cut."

Safi's lighter vessel was farther out now, where the wider water curled around the point. Its steersman had chosen the long course Jamil ordered. Safi stood near the middle, looking back at us. He raised a hand, then turned toward something the steersman had said.

I could see the time we would lose. The lighter boat would round the point, then Hamzah would follow, then Bakar. By the time the last of us reached the outer landing, the buyers might have found another boat, or gone back to their houses. Nasir's sister would close her stall and tell him that other men's agreements had cost her the day.

If Hamzah took the inner cut, he could reach the landing first and begin unloading. Bakar could follow the outer passage with Safi. We would all meet beyond the point. The two courses seemed no farther apart than the two banks of a wide creek.

"How long would the inner cut save?" I asked.

Tuma looked at the water between the trees. "If he gets through, perhaps enough to finish half his unloading before we come around."

"And if he does not?"

"Then he waits for the water or puts men overboard to push. I have not measured his load."

I looked again at the covered baskets. Hamzah had been waiting two days for this sale. He had agreed to the outer passage because Jamil had asked it. I had heard him say that the agreement would cost him time.

"We could tell him to take the cut," I said.

Bakar turned toward me. "Who is we?"

"I could carry the change."

"Jamil said no one takes it."

"I know."

"Then what change are you carrying?"

I took the wood from beneath my knee. The marks were dark where my thumb had rubbed them. Jamil had let me repeat his instruction because I had brought him accurate counts for nine mornings. I knew the difference between what I had been told and what I wished he had told me.

I could still have put the wood away.

"Bring us alongside Hamzah," I said.

Bakar shook his head. "We are keeping our distance."

"Only long enough to speak."

"Speak from here."

The current had pulled us a little behind. I raised my voice. Nasir turned first. Hamzah came to the side after him.

"Can you hear me?"

"I can hear you," Hamzah called. "What is it?"

"The inner water is open."

"I see it."

"Your boat could go through and meet us at the outer landing."

Hamzah leaned over the gunwale. "Did Jamil say so?"

I had expected the question. I had even prepared an answer that did not contain a lie.

"He sent me to carry the passage instructions," I called. "We have the same destination. If you take the inner cut, you will reach it first."

"That is not what he asked," Bakar said beside me.

Hamzah did not hear him. He looked toward Safi's boat, which had already begun the wider turn.

"Will Safi come inside with me?"

"No. He will take the outer water. You can meet him beyond the point."

Nasir moved to Hamzah's side. "The small boat is meant to watch us."

"It can still see the entrance," I said.

"Not what happens beyond it."

I looked through the trees. The opening was bright. The water farther on was hidden by leaves and a bend of the bank. I had been looking at the part I could see.

"We are only taking a shorter passage," I said. "The other boats will be near."

Bakar put his hand on my arm. His grip was not hard, but I could not pretend he had not touched me.

"Tell him whose order this is."

Hamzah was waiting. Nasir had turned away from the rope and was watching my face. Behind them the steersman held the bow from drifting into the mud.

I could have told Hamzah that I was suggesting a different route, and that he must ask Jamil before treating it as an instruction. But Jamil was behind us at the landing. By the time Hamzah sent someone back, the water might have fallen and the buyers would have waited another day.

"I am carrying Jamil's instruction for the three boats," I said. "The destination and the return remain the same. I am telling you that you may take the shorter water."

Bakar let go of my arm.

Hamzah looked from me to Nasir. "Can we pass?"

Nasir asked the steersman. The man studied the opening and said the water was high enough if they kept toward the bank with the taller trees. He did not promise that they could turn easily once inside.

"Then we will go," Hamzah said. "I will not lose another day to the long passage."

Nasir looked at me once more.

"Tell Safi we are taking it," he called.

"I will."

I had made two promises with one sentence. I had given Hamzah leave to go where Jamil had forbidden him to go, and I had promised to tell Safi after the choice was already made.

Hamzah's steersman moved the bow toward the opening. Nasir went forward to lift a rope clear of the baskets. The hull passed between two roots that stood out of the mud like bent knees. Its stern swung close enough to the bank for a branch to drag across the cover.

Bakar watched it go.

"You should have told him the truth," he said.

"I told him the water was open."

"You told him he could take it."

"He asked whether Jamil said so. I told him I was carrying Jamil's instruction."

"That is why he believed you."

Tuma did not look around. He kept our bow in the outer current, where the pull was stronger. The boat rolled gently as it crossed the wake of Safi's lighter vessel.

"We can still call him back," Bakar said.

Hamzah's stern was visible between the trees. Nasir stood beside the cover, one hand on a branch to keep it from striking his face.

"If he turns now, he may ground the boat," Tuma said. "He will have to go on until there is room."

Bakar looked at me. "You see?"

I had seen the opening, the bright water, and the shorter line to the landing. I had not thought about the space a loaded boat would need to turn around. It seemed a small oversight beside the larger fact that the passage was open.

"Then we tell Safi," I said.

The lighter boat had rounded the outer edge of the point. Its stern showed between us and the open bay, farther away than it had seemed from the landing. Safi was looking seaward, not toward the trees.

I stood behind the jars and called his name. The wind carried the sound away. Tuma told me to sit before I upset the load.

"Wave your cloth," Bakar said.

I pulled the cloth from my shoulder and raised it. Safi turned after a moment. He lifted his hand in answer.

"Does he understand?" I asked.

"He sees you," Bakar said. "That is all I know."

I pushed the count-stick back beneath my knee and waved toward the inner cut. Safi followed the direction of my arm, then spoke to his steersman. The lighter boat changed its angle slightly, as though they were trying to look behind the point without entering the shallower water.

Nasir called from the trees. I caught only the end of his words.

"What did he say?" I asked.

Tuma listened. "Something about the bank."

"Can you tell him we are coming?"

"We are not coming. We are taking the outer passage."

The bow of Hamzah's boat disappeared behind the green bank. For a moment I could still see the top of its cover. Then the trees hid that too.

I tried to picture the three boats from above, as I had pictured them on the mat before Jamil. The lighter one was outside the point. Ours was following it. Hamzah's had taken the inside and would come out ahead of us. If I could have scratched the routes onto the wood, the lines would have met at the outer landing.

The water did not show me the place where they would meet.

Bakar sat down beside the jars. He took a cord from his waist and began fastening the cover more tightly, though the knot had not come loose.

"He may reach the landing first," Bakar said.

"That was what I meant."

"I know what you meant."

"Then why are you angry?"

"Because I cannot see his boat. If he calls for help, what do I tell my steersman to do?"

He pulled the knot tight and returned to the stern.

Ahead, Safi's lighter boat held to the wider water. Its crew had begun to look toward the point more often than toward the open bay. I could not see Hamzah. I told myself that the trees were thick, that the inner passage curved, and that we would hear him before we saw him.

I kept listening for the sound of his men working the boat through the cut. The water moved against our hull, and beyond the point a bird called from the trees. I heard no men.

---

The first strange boat came into sight so quietly that I mistook it for a dark strip of bank.

It lay beyond the wooded point, where the inner water returned to the wider passage. A narrow hull crossed the bright opening and turned toward the shore. I saw two men standing, then a third rise from behind a low cover. One of them held something longer than his arm.

"Is that the trader from the morning?" I asked.

Bakar stood to look. "I cannot see the bow."

"Jamil said the trader had a pale strip on it."

"The man who came in said so. Jamil did not see him."

The hull turned again. Its bow came clear of the shadow. I saw no pale strip.

Tuma eased our boat toward the outside of the passage. He did it without asking me, and Bakar moved one of the jars so the weight sat more evenly. The water had begun to roughen where the river met the bay. A small wave struck the side and left a line of bubbles against the wood.

Safi's lighter vessel was ahead and to seaward. Its steersman had also seen the stranger. I knew because the bow changed direction before Safi stood up. They did not go toward the unknown boat at once. They held where the water was wide enough to turn.

"Is it waiting for us?" I asked.

"Perhaps it is waiting for the tide," Bakar said.

"With no cargo?"

"You cannot see its bottom."

The strange boat moved across the mouth of the inner cut. A man at its stern bent down, and when he rose the long object was in his hands. It might have been a pole. From where I sat I could not tell whether the end was pointed.

I searched the opening behind it for Hamzah's cover. There was only water and the far bank, then a bend that hid the inner passage.

Nasir should have been near the stern, counting the baskets as they came off. Hamzah should have been asking his buyers why they had not brought enough hands. I had sent them ahead to earn time. I had not yet seen them earn it.

Safi's boat turned toward us. It came close enough for his voice to reach.

"Where is Hamzah?"

I had prepared to tell him that Hamzah was taking the inner cut. Now that he was asking, the words felt like a confession.

"Inside the point," I said.

Safi looked at the trees, then back at me. "Why?"

"He thought he could reach the landing sooner."

"Did you tell him to go?"

I could have said that Hamzah had chosen the route. That would have been true. I could have said his steersman believed the water deep enough. That would also have been true.

"I told him he could take it," I said.

Safi's face changed only a little. His hand remained on the side of the boat.

"Did Jamil change the passage?"

"No."

For a moment the only sound between us was the water striking the two hulls.

"Then you have put him where I cannot watch him," Safi said.

I wanted to tell him that I had already waved, that he had raised his hand, that I thought he understood. I could see the cloth still hanging from my shoulder. He had seen me wave. He had not seen the boat go inside.

"I tried to tell you."

"You told me to look at the bank. I looked. I could not see Hamzah."

The stranger moved again. It had not approached us. It had turned toward the inner mouth, where the trees made a narrow gate between the channels.

Safi spoke to his steersman. The man pointed at the shallow water between them and the cut. They could cross it, he said, but not without slowing. If they took the longer curve they might arrive at the mouth after the stranger.

"Can you call Hamzah?" Safi asked me.

"I tried. The trees carry the sound away."

"Try again."

I stood behind the jars. Bakar caught my elbow before I could put a foot on the raised board. He pointed to the place where the hull was lifting under the wave.

"Stand there. Keep both feet down."

I called Hamzah's name until my throat hurt. The trees gave back no answer. I called Nasir, then stopped because I could not imagine how he would know where I stood.

Safi's steersman was watching the unknown boat. He said something I could not hear. Safi bent to listen, then looked toward the outer bay.

A second craft had appeared behind the first. It was lower and shorter, and at first I saw only the heads of men above its side. It came along the far bank, not toward us. Its men were looking into the inner passage.

"Those are the two boats the fisherman saw," I said.

"We do not know that," Safi answered.

"One had a dark cover."

"So does that one."

"The fisherman said there were no nets."

"He said he could see no nets."

I had heard Hasan make the same correction that morning. It was harder to accept now, when the boats were moving and Hamzah was out of sight.

The first craft reached the inner mouth and slowed. A man at its bow lifted an arm. He shouted something. I heard the sound but could not make out a word.

"Was that for us?" I asked.

Safi did not answer. He waited until the man called again. The second call was shorter.

"Can you understand him?"

"No. He may be calling to the other boat."

The shorter craft answered with a shout of its own. A man near its stern raised a long shaft. The object caught the light at one end. I could not tell whether it was a blade, a point, or wet wood.

Bakar moved to the bow of our boat and called to Safi.

"I will not take these jars into that mouth," he said.

"I have not asked you to," Safi answered.

"If you go after Hamzah, who keeps the outer water clear?"

Safi looked toward the wider passage. Beyond us lay open water and the route home. The two strange boats had not blocked it.

"Keep your boat where you can turn back," Safi said. "If anyone comes toward you, do not wait for me."

"And Hamzah?"

"I am going to see whether I can reach him."

Bakar shook his head. "You cannot watch both ends."

"No," Safi said. "I cannot."

He turned to his steersman. They spoke quietly. I could see the steersman pointing at a line of darker water along the edge of the trees. Safi listened, then asked a question. The man answered with a shake of his head and a movement of his hand that showed how the bow would have to turn.

I remembered Safi's return above the bend: his steersman had given the small boat room, and the woman at its bow had known the water. I had remembered only that they came back first.

"Take me with you," I said.

Safi looked at the jars behind me. "You are aboard Bakar's boat."

"I can cross when you come alongside."

"Why?"

"Because I sent Hamzah inside."

"That is why you must stay where I can find you."

The answer stung. I wanted to be the one who went into the trees, who found Hamzah and brought him back, who showed Jamil that the altered passage had not been foolish after all.

"At least let me tell you what his steersman said."

"Tell me now."

"He said the water was high enough if he kept toward the taller trees. He could not turn easily once inside."

Safi repeated this to his steersman. The man looked at the trees, then at the strange craft holding near the mouth.

"If we go in from here," he said, "we must come out the same way if they block the far end."

"Can we turn?"

"Not quickly."

Safi asked whether they could reach the mouth without entering. The steersman said they could, but the current would set them toward the strangers if they came too close. Safi listened and chose to approach from the outside, where the boat still had room to turn.

I watched him make the choice. He had fewer facts than I had wanted when I changed Hamzah's course. He did not fill the spaces by saying what he hoped was true.

A sound came from behind the trees. It was a shout, then another, so faint that I could not tell whether it came from Hamzah's boat or the men at the inner mouth. Bakar heard it too. He looked at me, but did not speak.

Safi's steersman began working the boat toward the cut. The stranger's bow turned to face them. One of the men at its side stood up and lifted his long shaft.

"Stay back," Bakar called to Safi.

Safi did not answer. He had moved beyond easy speaking distance.

I looked down at the count-stick beneath my knee. I had marked the expected hour of arrival and the names of the owners. I had made a cut for the boat watching the seaward side. There was no mark for the moment when I had told Hamzah to leave it.

I put my thumb over the empty place and tried to listen past the water.

Another shout came from the trees. This time I heard a man's voice break in the middle of it. Then there was a sound like wood striking wood, twice in quick succession.

Bakar called to Tuma to hold our boat clear. The steersman brought the bow around. I rose before he had finished and caught myself against the jars.

Beyond the point, the shorter strange craft had begun to move. It was heading toward the mouth of the cut. Safi's boat was moving there too.

Hamzah's boat remained hidden.

---

Safi's boat reached the outer mouth before the shorter stranger did. I could tell because I saw the small bow cross a patch of pale water and stop there, while the other craft was still coming along the bank. Neither went farther inside. For several breaths they lay facing one another, each close enough to the trees that a man could have reached out and caught a branch.

I heard Safi call. The words were lost before they reached me.

The man in the other boat lifted his long shaft. He did not throw it. He pointed it toward the opening and called something back. His companions moved along the side, making the narrow hull lean.

"Why is Safi waiting?" I asked.

Tuma had turned Bakar's bow toward the wider water. He was looking behind us as often as ahead.

"Because he has not yet found a way in," he said.

I wanted to tell him that the way was there, between the two banks. I could see it myself. But I could not see what lay behind the first turn, and I had already made one decision from the part of the water that showed.

A cry came from inside the trees. I heard it plainly enough to know that it was not a call about a basket.

Bakar stood up. "Hamzah!"

There was no answer. The shorter stranger swung its bow across the opening. Safi's steersman backed his boat away from it, not far, only enough to keep the two hulls apart. The other stranger was moving on the far side of the point. I could see men bent over its gunwale, pulling at something below them.

Then Hamzah's cover appeared between the trees.

It came out sideways, not following the bow of his boat. The cloth had been pulled from its fastenings and hung low over the water. One corner was torn. I saw a man's arm rise beside it and catch the edge of the hull. He pulled himself up, then dropped out of sight.

For a moment I thought the boat had grounded and its men were trying to free it.

The hull moved again. Its bow turned toward the bank while the stern was pulled the other way. A rope stretched from its forward post toward the unfamiliar craft. Men aboard that craft were hauling. The cargo boat came after them a little at a time.

"Someone has taken her line," Bakar said.

I could see the rope now. I could not see who had fastened it.

A man came out of the inner passage in the water, swimming badly. He raised one arm and sank to his mouth. When he surfaced he tried to reach the low bank. His hand closed on a branch and slipped.

Safi's steersman pointed toward him. Safi looked once at Hamzah's boat, which was being drawn away, then toward the swimmer.

He shouted to his men. Their bow turned away from the strangers.

"No," I said.

Bakar heard me. "No what?"

"If he goes for the man, they will take the boat."

"They have the boat."

"Not yet."

Tuma pushed his steering oar against the current. "And if the man goes under while we argue?"

Safi's boat passed between the stranger and the swimmer. One of the men at its bow lay flat and reached down. The swimmer struck at his hand, missed, and caught the side instead. They dragged him close enough for a second man to grip his cloth. His legs remained in the water while they worked him aboard.

The nearer stranger did not follow. It moved toward the rope that held Hamzah's vessel. I saw a covered bundle pass from one hull to the other. Then another. From this distance I could not tell whether they were the rice baskets or the cloth Hamzah had put above them. I knew only that they had come from his boat.

The swimmer was lying across Safi's gunwale. He tried to rise, then folded over his leg. A dark mark spread down the cloth around his calf. I did not see what had hurt him.

"That is Hamzah's steersman," Bakar said.

He had known the man before this morning. I had counted his share and could not remember his name.

Another figure appeared on the muddy bank inside the cut. He was moving along it with one hand against the roots. When he reached an opening in the trees he waved both arms.

"One on shore," Tuma said.

Safi had seen him. His boat could not reach that bank directly; the stranger lay between it and the narrow water. Safi sent one man to the stern and called toward us.

Bakar cupped his hands around his mouth. "What?"

"Keep clear! There is a man on the bank!"

"Can he come out?"

The answer was carried away. Safi's steersman pointed toward a strip of mud nearer the outer mouth. The man on shore began working toward it, slipping twice where the roots were wet.

The second stranger, farther inside, turned its bow toward the hidden passage. Hamzah's cargo boat followed on the rope. I saw the torn cover catch on a branch and tear farther. The men hauling did not stop.

I stood before I knew I had moved. "We can cut them off."

Bakar caught my wrist.

"With what?"

"Your boat is closer to the outer water. We can go round the point."

"With my jars aboard? With Tuma steering us into two boats whose men have already taken another?"

"We might get Hamzah's boat back."

"That is not a promise you can make with my hull."

I looked at the strangers. They had begun to pass behind the trees. Their rope was still tight. It would take only a few moments for them to be out of sight.

I could not bear the thought that they would leave with Hamzah's cargo because I had watched.

"Let me take a small boat," I said.

"Whose?"

I had no answer. There was no boat waiting beside us for the thing I wished to do.

A second man came into sight near Safi's bow, not in the water but clinging to a root at the bank. He was heavier than the first. He held one arm against his chest. I recognized Hamzah when he turned his face toward the open water.

Safi's steersman worked the boat toward the mud, holding it out from the roots. The man who had hauled the wounded steersman aboard lay down again and reached for Hamzah. They pulled him over the side. He landed on his knees and stayed there.

The first man on shore was still moving toward the outer mouth. He had reached the strip of mud Tuma had pointed out. From there he could walk along the exposed bank until it came opposite our boat.

"Take him," Bakar told Tuma.

Tuma did not turn immediately. He watched the current along the mud, then worked our bow across it. Bakar had the rope ready. The man on shore came knee-deep into the water and caught it. He was one of Hamzah's carriers, a narrow-faced man whose basket I had marked that morning. He came aboard shaking, without his sandals.

"Where is Nasir?" I asked.

He was bent over, breathing hard. I asked again before Bakar could stop me.

"I don't know," he said.

"Did you see him?"

"I saw him beside the cover. Then the other boat struck us."

"Which other boat?"

He pointed toward the trees. Both strange craft had gone behind them. "The one that came up the passage. Men came over. I went to the bank."

"Was Nasir in the water?"

"I don't know."

I wanted him to tell me where Nasir had stood when the rope was taken, whether he had held to the side, whether he had gone with the cargo. The man was looking at his own hands. He had scraped the skin from his palms climbing the roots.

Bakar put a jar of water beside him. "Drink."

Safi's boat came toward us, lower than it had been before. The wounded steersman lay along the middle boards with his leg raised on a folded cloth. Hamzah sat beside him, holding his arm. One of Safi's men pressed a strip of cloth to the wounded calf.

"How many?" Safi called.

"One carrier here," Bakar answered.

"Hamzah and his steersman with me. Where is the other?"

The man beside our jars raised his head. "He went into the trees before I did. I heard him calling from the bank."

"Which bank?"

He pointed behind us, toward the stretch we had passed. The man might still have been there, hidden by the branches.

Safi looked at the water, then at the two boats. "We will go back as far as we can see him. You keep the outer passage open."

I had not understood that there was another man missing from the count. My eyes had been on Nasir.

"Nasir?" I called.

Hamzah heard me. He put his good hand against the side of Safi's boat and leaned forward.

"I saw him at the stern when they came alongside," he said. "He was trying to keep the cover from catching. After they climbed aboard, I lost sight of him."

"Did they take him?"

"I did not see."

"Could he have reached the bank?"

"He could have. I did not see."

His words gave me nothing to hold. I had asked him two questions that would have allowed me to imagine Nasir alive in one place or another. Hamzah would not choose a place for me.

Safi's boat turned back toward the trees. He did not go after the cargo boat. He went along the open edge, calling for the other carrier. Twice the men stopped to listen. At the second place a voice answered from the bank. The carrier came out through the roots with mud to his waist and a cut across his forehead. Safi's men brought him aboard.

We had Hamzah, his steersman, and both carriers. I counted them as they crossed the open water. I counted them again because I was afraid I had missed a man.

Nasir did not come out of the trees.

The strange boats were gone. I could not tell whether they had taken the inner passage farther inland or turned beyond the point into another channel. Hamzah's cargo boat was gone with them. We had no vessel left that could safely follow, and Safi would not leave the injured man to search.

When Safi reached us, I asked whether we could wait until the water fell and look along the bank.

"We can look from here," he said. "We can send word to the people who work this reach. We cannot take these men back inside while the others may still be there."

"I sent him in."

"I know."

"I could go ashore."

"From which boat?"

I looked at Bakar. He was holding his jars in place as Tuma turned us toward home.

Safi followed my look. "Bakar has not agreed to risk his boat. Nor have I."

I had thought that admitting what I had done would give me the right to put myself in danger afterward. Safi was carrying men whose lives did not belong to me.

He told his steersman to take the wounded man home by the wider passage. I watched the man on the boards lift his head when the bow turned. He asked for water. Hamzah held the cup while he drank.

The place behind the point grew smaller. I kept looking for a hand in the branches, a head above the mud, anything that might turn the missing man into one more man to be counted.

Nothing moved there that I could distinguish from the trees.

We brought two boats home where three had gone out.

---

The landing was crowded before our bows touched it. Someone had seen Safi's boat coming back with a man lying in the middle, and word had gone up the steps faster than we could row.

A boy ran down with a rope. Tuma told him to stand aside until the bow had settled against the post. The boy held the rope out anyway. Bakar took it from him and made it fast himself.

The wounded steersman had begun to shiver. He had spoken twice on the return, once to ask whether his boat was behind us and once to ask whether the bleeding had stopped. Hamzah had answered the second question. No one had answered the first.

Safi would not let the men on the landing pull the steersman upright by his arms. He sent them for a board wide enough to carry him, then asked where someone who could tend the wound might be found. Two women came down from the houses with cloth and a covered bowl. They spoke to the steersman before they touched his leg. He nodded. One cut away the bloodied wrapping while the other pressed clean cloth against the place where it had soaked through.

I could see the wound only when the cloth was lifted. It was longer than I had imagined. The skin below the knee had opened in a line that was not straight, and there was grit caught in it. I turned my face away.

The steersman saw me.

"Was it you who said the inner water was open?" he asked.

I looked back at him. "Yes."

He closed his eyes. He did not ask another question.

Hamzah stepped from Safi's boat with help. His arm hung close to his body. When someone offered to take him inside, he refused.

"Count my men first."

"They are here," I said.

"Then name them."

I had been counting them since the outer passage. I opened my mouth and found that I knew the name of only one of the five.

"Nasir," I said.

Hamzah stared at me. "Nasir is not here."

I looked toward the two carriers. The one with the cut forehead was sitting on the lower steps. The other stood beside Bakar's jars, holding the water cup in both hands.

"I know the others by their work," I said. "Not their names."

Hamzah called them to him. The first was Iman, the man who had come along the bank and taken Bakar's rope. The second was Dali, the one Safi had found farther among the roots. The steersman's name was Latif. Hamzah said each name once, as if he were putting the men in their places aboard the boat that was no longer there.

"Four," he said. "And Nasir."

I made no mark.

A man from the landing came with a fresh strip of cloth for Latif's leg. One of the women told him to bring water that had been heated, not the cup everyone had been passing around. The man went back up the steps.

Jamil arrived with Hasan behind him. He took in the board, the women, the two boats, and the space where Hamzah's vessel should have been.

"Where is the third?" he asked.

Hamzah answered before anyone else could.

"Taken."

"By whom?"

"I do not know their names."

"Where?"

"In the inner cut."

Jamil looked at Safi. "Why was he inside?"

Safi did not look at me. "Ache told him he could go. He said it carried your instruction."

The people standing closest to us turned. I could feel them looking, though no one spoke.

Jamil asked me, "Did you?"

"Yes."

"Did I give you leave to change the passage?"

"No."

He held my gaze for a moment. "Then we will hear the whole account. Not here, while a man needs tending."

Hamzah would not go inside. "My boat is gone. My goods are gone. Nasir is gone. You may hear it here."

Jamil glanced toward Latif. The women were moving him onto the board. He waited until they had lifted it and carried him up the steps. Then he turned back.

"Tell me what you saw," he said to Hamzah.

Hamzah spoke of a narrow craft that had come alongside them in the inner passage. He had first thought it was waiting to pass. Men aboard it shouted. His own steersman had tried to keep their bow from striking the bank. Another craft had appeared farther ahead, and Hamzah's boat could neither turn nor pass between them.

"One of them climbed over our side," he said. "Then more. We had the baskets under the cover. They cut the fastenings. I tried to stop them."

He raised his arm a little and winced.

"What did they use?" Hasan asked.

"Hands, first. One struck Latif when he tried to move the stern away. I saw something in the man's hand, but I cannot tell you what it was. We were against the trees."

"Did you see Nasir leave the boat?"

"No. He was at the stern. He shouted to me. Then the cover came down and I could not see him."

"Did you see anyone take him?"

"No."

Hasan repeated the last two answers quietly, so that the people standing farther back would hear what Hamzah had said and what he had not.

Dali said he had jumped toward the bank when the strangers came aboard. He had heard a splash behind him. He did not know who had made it. Iman had climbed into the roots before he could see what happened at the stern. Neither had seen Nasir reach land.

Safi told Jamil where his own boat had been when he saw the strangers. He described the man in the water, Latif's leg, Hamzah clinging to the bank, and the choice to retrieve them while the other craft hauled the cargo boat away.

"Could you have followed?" Jamil asked.

"Not without leaving the men in the water. The strangers still had two boats in the cut. I did not know how many more they had."

"Did you see them carry goods away?"

"I saw bundles pass between the hulls. I could not name the goods."

Hamzah spoke again. "I can name them. They were mine, and the two merchants' whose goods I carried."

"Then we must tell those merchants what you know," Jamil said. "Not pretend we have counted what came back."

Bakar came forward with his own account. He said that he had refused to bring his loaded boat into the cut. He told Jamil how I had called to Hamzah, how Hamzah had asked whose order it was, and how I had answered.

I had expected Bakar to sound angry. He sounded as he had when he asked whether someone would pay for a cracked jar.

"He told Hamzah he was carrying your instruction," Bakar said. "He did not say that you had changed it. Hamzah took it for leave to go inside. I told Ache to say whose order it was."

Jamil asked whether Bakar had seen the signal to Safi.

"I saw Ache wave his cloth. Safi raised a hand. I could not tell what he understood."

Tuma agreed. He added that he had warned the inner cut might not give Hamzah room to turn.

"He asked whether it was deep enough," Tuma said. "I told him I had taken a smaller boat through yesterday. I did not tell him Hamzah could turn a loaded one inside."

Jamil looked at me. "What did you think Safi understood?"

"That Hamzah had gone inside."

"What did he actually say?"

"He did not say anything. He raised his hand."

"And you did not go after him to make sure?"

"No."

I had answers for all the questions. Each answer left less room for the story I had been preparing during the return: that I had acted quickly, that I had meant to save the sale, that the strangers had come at a place no one could have foreseen. I had been imagining how I would tell Jamil about the first report he had set aside. The words were still ready in my mouth.

Jamil turned to Hasan.

"I accepted the later man's recognition of a trader as enough to send the boats. I knew the first man had seen two hulls. I did not know what they were, and I sent the convoy anyway."

Hasan did not soften the statement. "The later man did not see behind the point."

"No," Jamil said. "He told me that."

"Your order kept the boats together," I said.

"Yes."

"I changed it."

"You did."

"If the boats had stayed together, they might all have met those men on the outer passage."

"They might have," Safi said. "We did not meet them there. We found them in the cut, where Hamzah had no room to turn."

I looked toward the empty water beyond the posts. I could still hear Nasir asking whether Safi would come inside with him.

Someone was coming down the steps, calling his name.

The woman was carrying a shallow basket beneath one arm. She had tied a strip of cloth around its rim, and the knot had come loose as she hurried. She stopped when she saw Hamzah, then looked past him toward the two boats.

"Where is my brother?"

Hamzah went toward her. She passed him without waiting for an answer and looked into Bakar's boat, where Iman and Dali had been sitting. Then she looked into Safi's.

"Nasir?" she called.

No one answered.

Hamzah told her that he had not come back. She asked whether he was hurt. Hamzah said he did not know. She asked whether he had been taken. He said he did not know that either.

"Then where did you leave him?"

Hamzah looked toward the water. "At the inner passage. We were driven from the boat. I could not find him."

The woman put her basket on the lowest step. She had to hold its rim with one hand to keep it from tipping.

"He has rice for me," she said. "From last week. He was meant to bring it when he came home."

"I remember," Hamzah answered.

"Did you give it to him?"

"He was to take it from my store when we returned."

She looked at him. "Then it is still there."

"Yes."

"I will take it now. And I want someone to show me where you last saw him."

Jamil stepped forward. "We will send men to ask along the banks as soon as it can be done without putting another crew into that passage blind."

She looked at him, then at the boats.

"Who will go?"

He could not name a crew yet. She waited while he told her that.

I had thought she would ask me why I had sent her brother inside. Perhaps she had not heard the account. Perhaps she had heard enough and had more immediate questions. She picked up her basket.

"Bring me his sandals," she said to Hamzah. "He left them under the seat. If you find the boat, do not leave them there."

Hamzah looked down. The boat was gone, and with it the seat.

"I will remember," he said.

She went back up the steps with the basket still empty.

I had watched her leave without telling her that I was the one who changed the route. I could tell Jamil and Hamzah the truth while they stood among men who already knew my name. I had not yet found the courage to tell the person who had come looking for Nasir.

Jamil sent Hasan to gather the witnesses' accounts separately. He told Safi to remain until the two cargo owners had spoken, and Bakar to give the names of everyone aboard his boat. Then he turned to me.

"Bring the count-stick," he said.

It was still beneath my knee in Bakar's boat. I went to fetch it. The expected hour of arrival was marked near the end, dark from the pressure of my thumb.

I had no mark for a return with one boat missing.

---

I carried the count-stick back to Jamil's room. I had cleaned the mud from my hands at the landing, but it remained in the cuts of my nails. When I put the wood on the mat, a little grit fell beside it.

Hasan was already there. He had laid out three short pieces of wood for the three boats, though only two had returned. He had put the names of the men on separate strips, with space beside each for what that man had seen.

Jamil took my stick and read the marks. He stopped at the place where I had written the expected hour of arrival.

"Who agreed to that hour?"

"Hamzah expected it. Bakar said he would try."

"Did Safi promise it?"

"No."

He put the stick down. "Then you knew before you left that an expected hour was not a passage order."

I had no wish to hear another question about the passage. I wanted to ask whether we were sending boats back, whether the strangers might still be within reach, whether Nasir could be waiting on the bank with an injured leg. But Jamil had called me to account for what I had done, and I knew that speaking first about Nasir would let me avoid it.

"I told Hamzah he could take the inner cut," I said.

"I heard you at the landing."

"I told him I carried your instruction. I knew you had forbidden the cut."

"Why did you do it?"

"His buyers were waiting. Nasir wanted to return before his sister closed her stall."

"Was that what Nasir asked you to do?"

"No."

"Did Hamzah ask you to find another passage?"

"No. I asked him to take it."

Jamil was quiet. Outside the room someone was splitting wood. The blows came at uneven intervals, and each one made me want to speak before the next.

"I thought I could get them there first," I said. "Then Safi and Bakar would come round the point, and we would be together again."

"You told Hamzah where the boats would meet. Where was Safi supposed to find him before then?"

"I did ask Tuma about the water."

"You asked whether Hamzah could pass. You did not ask whether Safi could reach him."

I looked at Hasan. He had said nothing since I entered.

"Did you know the boats were dangerous?" I asked him.

"I knew the first man had seen something he could not identify."

"And the second?"

"I knew what he recognized. He did not tell us what he could not see."

"Jamil chose to go."

Jamil answered before Hasan could. "I did. That is my error. It will be answered for by me."

He drew one of the short pieces of wood toward him. It was the strip for Hamzah's boat.

"I will tell the owners what I judged. You will tell them what you said."

I could feel my face warming. I had wanted him to say we were both wrong. He had said it, but not in the way I wanted.

"What happens to Hamzah?" I asked.

"He has lost a boat and goods belonging partly to other men. He must tell them. They will ask him why he entered the cut, and he will tell them that he believed he had leave. I will not ask him to hide that."

"Will you pay him?"

"Some of what is owed may be settled. Some must be argued. His cargo was not all his. His men have their own claims. A payment made before we know those claims can put money in the wrong hands."

"I could give him what I have."

"How much do you have?"

I thought of the small amount I had kept from my own allowance. It would not replace one of Hamzah's baskets.

"Not enough."

"Then do not offer him a boat with words."

I looked down at the mat. Hasan moved one of the witness strips nearer to me.

"These are the men who returned," he said. "Latif is being tended. Iman and Dali can speak. Hamzah has given his account. Nasir's place remains open. I need you to write down which of them you heard yourself, and which words came to you through someone else."

"Will I go with the search?"

"Not as the man who gives its instructions," Jamil said.

I looked up.

"You will no longer carry passage orders in my name. You will not tell an owner to change his route, even if you believe the change will save a life. When a crew asks what I have ordered, you will bring the question to Hasan or to me. You will not answer for us."

"For how long?"

"Until I decide you can be trusted to carry what was said without making it what you wanted said."

I had expected to be sent away. The thought of returning to the houses with nothing to do had frightened me on the way back. Now that he was giving me work, I resented its smallness.

"Am I to count baskets again?"

"You are to learn whose baskets have been lost. You are to sit with Hasan when he hears the owners. You are to take the names of the people waiting for the men who went out. You will carry their questions back without improving the answers."

He paused.

"Tomorrow Bakar will tell us what his boat can do. If he agrees to carry men to the safer bank, you may go with them to listen. You will not command his crew."

I thought of Bakar's hand on my wrist. I had asked him to turn his boat toward the strangers, and he had asked me whose boat I meant to risk. I had not thanked him for refusing.

"Will Safi lead the search?" I asked.

"Safi has spoken with the men who work the outer traps. He is asking them what they can see from their own banks. He will not take a boat into the cut merely to show that he is brave."

"He could have pursued the strangers."

"He could have tried. Then I might be hearing how many more men did not return."

I remembered Safi turning toward Latif in the water while Hamzah's boat was still being hauled away. I had called out no, as if the rescue were the mistake.

"Does he get another command?"

"That is between Safi and the men who agree to go with him."

The answer did not satisfy me. I wanted to know whether he had risen while I had fallen. I could see the difference without anyone saying it.

Hasan gave me a clean piece of wood. I made a mark for Hamzah, then for Latif, Iman and Dali. I left a space for Nasir. Hasan did not tell me to fill it.

We worked until the light in the doorway changed. One of the merchants whose cloth had been on Hamzah's boat came to ask how much had been taken. Hasan told him which bundles had been seen leaving the hull and which had only been listed at departure. The merchant was angry that we could not give him an exact count. He was angrier when he learned that the boat itself had been taken.

"You sent men to protect my goods," he said to Jamil. "How many goods did those men protect?"

Jamil did not send him away. He let the merchant ask again. When the man had finished, Jamil gave him the names of the witnesses and said he would hear his claim after he had spoken to Hamzah.

The merchant left without thanking him.

Near dusk I went down to the lower landing. Bakar was there with Tuma, moving the jars from his boat. They had finished their delivery nowhere. The buyers at the outer landing would have to wait, and Bakar was trying to find out whether they would still take the jars tomorrow.

He saw me standing beside the post.

"Has Jamil given you another passage?" he asked.

"No."

"Good."

I wanted to argue, but Tuma was working a jar over the gunwale and needed the space. I took its weight when he asked. The clay was slick with water. I set it where Bakar pointed, beside the others, and stepped back.

Bakar looked at my hands.

"That one belongs to a man who will complain if the lip is chipped," he said.

"I put it down carefully."

"Then perhaps he will not complain."

He went back to the boat. I could not tell whether he meant to forgive me anything. He had not said he did.

Nasir's sister came down the steps after the other men had gone. Her basket was no longer empty. Someone had given her the rice she was owed. She carried it close against her hip, and when she stopped she set it down with care.

"Did they find him?" she asked.

"No."

"Have they gone to look?"

"Safi has sent word to the people on the banks. Jamil is asking for boats and men who know the passage."

"Are you going?"

"Only if an owner agrees to take me."

She studied my face. "You were with them."

"I was in Bakar's boat."

"Did you see Nasir?"

"I saw him when Hamzah went inside the trees. After that I did not."

"Why did he go inside?"

I could have told her that Hamzah chose to turn. He had. I could have said that Jamil sent the boats and that strangers had attacked. Both were true. Neither answered her question.

"I told Hamzah he could take the shorter water. Jamil had ordered us to stay together. I spoke as if he had changed his mind."

She looked past me to the place where Hamzah's boat had been tied before the voyage.

"Did Nasir know?"

"He asked whether Safi would come with them. I told him Safi would take the outer passage and meet them."

"So he knew they would be alone."

"Yes."

She stood a long time without speaking. I wanted her to ask whether I was sorry. I wanted to tell her that I had not meant for this to happen. She did not give me either opening.

"My brother counted what other men owed him," she said. "He would come home and tell me the number, then argue with me about how much was ours. He was always certain he remembered it. I made him count again."

She lifted the basket.

"If you hear where he is, you tell me. You do not tell someone who will tell someone else."

"I will."

"And if you do not know, say you do not know."

She walked up the steps. I watched until she reached the turn and disappeared between the houses.

The water had fallen below the mark on the posts. At the far end of the landing, a rope hung from the place where Hamzah had made his boat fast two mornings earlier. No one had untied its shoreward end. The loose length moved in the current and touched the wood each time the water rose.

I went to it and took it in my hand. The fibers were rough where the rope had dried. It was not the line the strangers had used to haul Hamzah's boat away. It was only a line left behind.

I could have tied it up so no one would trip over it. I could have taken it to Hamzah and asked whether he wanted it. I did neither. I stood holding it while the light faded.

I was already trying to find the moment when a different instruction might have saved them. If I had called Safi sooner. If I had told Hamzah to hurry through the cut instead of waiting at its mouth. If I had taken the small boat myself.

Every thought began with something I might have ordered.

The rope pulled once against my hand as the water moved. There was no boat at the other end.
