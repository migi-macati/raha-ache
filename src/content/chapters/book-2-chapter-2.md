The man who had heard my message was washing a shallow tray when I found him. He tilted it so the water ran from one corner, then set it in the sun beside the doorway.

“Has anyone asked to carry my words?” I said.

He looked at me without reaching for the strip of wood on the shelf.

“Have you brought another message?”

“No. The same one.”

“Then it is still here.”

I had learned to ask whether a boat was leaving before asking whether there was room on it. I had not yet learned how to bear an answer that did not change.

“Someone at the receiving house said a trader from the west may come in,” I told him.

“Someone at the receiving house hears about boats all day.”

“Have you heard of him?”

“I have heard that men are waiting for him.”

“Where will he go afterward?”

“If he comes, ask him.”

He lifted the tray again. A little water had collected beneath its rim. He shook it onto the ground, then took a cloth to the last wet place.

I had meant to tell him that I could find a carrier myself. It was a poor claim to make while I could not even name the vessel.

“Sulad has words to add,” I said.

“Then bring Sulad. We heard yours before two men. We will hear his from him.”

“He is working along the bank.”

“I did not send him there.”

I left before I asked the man to send for him. Outside, the steps were crowded with people carrying things into the house and people trying to discover who would receive them. A woman held two shallow baskets against her hips. A narrow cover had been tied over each one, but one of the bindings had come loose. She was trying to retie it without putting either basket on the ground.

I moved toward her. She saw me and turned her shoulder away, as though she thought I was reaching for the load.

“I can hold one,” I said.

“Can you hold it without opening it?”

“Yes.”

She gave me the smaller basket. It was lighter than I expected, though its bottom pressed sharply against my forearm. Beneath the cover something shifted when I moved.

“Keep it level,” she said.

I did. She tightened the binding on the other basket with her teeth and pulled the knot against her knee.

“Who receives these?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

“You came out of that room.”

“I was asking about a message.”

“Then you know where to ask.”

I had spent the morning sending claimants to the receiving woman rather than accepting their goods myself. I should have given her the same answer. Instead I looked toward the open passage and said, “What are they for?”

“Men working above the bend. They have been promised food while they finish a bank that keeps falling into the water.”

“Who promised them?”

“My brother arranged it. I brought what he bought.”

She took the smaller basket back. The covering moved again, and I saw through a gap that it held leaf-wrapped portions, packed close enough that the bottom ones had begun to flatten.

“The men are waiting for these?”

“They have eaten what was sent yesterday. If they stop working, the owner will say my brother failed to supply them. If they keep working without food, they will come looking for him.”

She looked past me to the passage.

“I was told a boat is being made ready. I need to know whether these can go with it.”

“Which boat?”

“The one they are loading beyond the outer steps.”

I could see only a strip of water between the buildings. A man came through carrying a pole on his shoulder, turned it to clear the doorway, and went on without looking at us.

“Who told you it would take them?” I asked.

“A man who said to bring them here before the light changed. He has gone to fetch the other load.”

She shifted her baskets.

“I can wait. I cannot wait until tomorrow and still call this today's food.”

I thought of the message still waiting on the shelf inside. The woman was waiting for me to answer.

“My grandfather's people are seeing to the boats,” I said.

“Are you one of them?”

“I am staying in his house.”

“That is not what I asked.”

A boy came out carrying a narrow board on which someone had made marks in rows. He stopped beside us to let two men pass. The woman turned to him.

“Do you know where the load for the upper bend is being gathered?”

He looked at her baskets. “What is in them?”

“Food for the men.”

“Have they taken it?”

“Would I be standing here?”

He made a face, but he did not go. I could see him looking at me, waiting to discover whether I knew more than he did.

“I can speak to the people loading the boat,” I said.

“Will they listen to you?” the woman asked.

“They will hear me.”

She took a breath, shifted one basket to the ground, then straightened with her hand against her back.

“If they have room, tell them I am here.”

“I'll see that your baskets have a place,” I said.

The boy's gaze moved from me to the woman. He seemed to hear something in my words that I had not intended.

“Both baskets?” he asked.

“Yes, both,” I said, meaning that I would ask about both.

The woman bent to retie the loose cover. “Then I will wait here. My brother will come looking for me when he returns.”

The boy looked down at his board. There was a row near the bottom with fewer marks than the others. He touched it with his thumb.

“I will tell them there are two more,” he said.

“Tell them I am asking,” I said.

“You said they have a place.”

“I said I would see to it.”

He stared at me. The difference had seemed plain when I spoke. It sounded less plain when I heard it back.

“Whose place?” he asked.

“I haven't seen the boat yet.”

The boy put his board against his side.

“Then I will tell them Ache says to keep room for two baskets, and they can ask you.”

“No,” I said.

He had already turned toward the outer steps. I followed, leaving the woman with her baskets.

“Wait. I have not told anyone to keep room.”

“You told her you would see to it.”

“I meant I would ask.”

“Then why didn't you say that?”

He was younger than I was, or looked it, and I resented how easily he had asked the question. He went on before I answered.

I could have called him back loudly enough for the people on the steps to hear. I could have told the woman that I had spoken too soon. Instead I walked beside him, trying to reach the boat before he did.

At the turn of the passage a man carrying a folded covering stopped us. He had to brace its weight against the wall while another man cleared the way.

“Are those baskets coming?” he asked the boy.

“Two. Ache says there is room.”

The man looked at me.

“I haven't looked,” I said.

“Then there may not be.”

He adjusted the covering. Its edge had worn thin where it had been folded against itself.

“Who is deciding?” I asked.

“Ask the men whose boat it is.”

He went past us. The boy hurried after him, holding the board clear of the covering. I stood where the passage opened toward the water.

The boat was farther away than I had expected. I could see people moving along its side and a pair of baskets already waiting beneath a roof. A man lifted one of them, tested its weight, and put it back. Nothing about the boat told me whether two more would fit.

The woman came around the corner behind me. She had carried both baskets rather than leave them where I could not see them.

“Did you find the place?” she asked.

I looked toward the men at the boat. The boy was speaking to one of them. The man turned and searched the passage until he found me.

“Not yet,” I said.

Her arms tightened around the baskets.

“You said they had a place.”

I opened my mouth. I could remember the exact words I had used. That did not make them any more useful to her.

The man beside the boat called for the boy to bring his board. Then he called my name.

I had wanted someone in this house to know it.

The woman set down her baskets and waited for me to go to him.


* * *

The boy reached the boat before me. He held up his board as if the marks on it would explain everything.

“There are two more baskets,” he told the man at the landing. “Ache said to leave room.”

“I said I would ask,” I answered.

The man looked from the boy to me. He had one foot on the steps and the other on a board laid between them and the boat. The board dipped when someone moved a sack behind him.

“Then ask,” he said. “But get off this board while you do it.”

I stepped back. The boy followed, holding his tally against his chest. The woman with the food waited beyond us. She had set her baskets down in a patch of shade, though she kept one hand on the loose binding.

A man on the boat called to the one at the steps. He wanted the covering lifted before the next load came aboard. The man answered that there were still people beneath it.

I had taken the boat for a large one from the passage. Close to it, the distance between the people working on either side seemed small. A narrow place ran down the middle, with bundles tied under a covering stretched above part of the load. A man was kneeling there, feeling along the edge of a woven mat. Every time another worker came through, he had to turn his knees aside.

The man from the steps called for someone named Jamil.

I had heard that name in Siripada’s house. It belonged to a man who could tell boatmen what they were to do, or so I had understood from the way others spoke of him. He came from farther along the landing with a younger man beside him. The younger man was carrying a folded length of cloth that had been patched in two places.

Jamil stopped before he reached us.

“Why is that still here?” he asked the man on the boat.

“The edge catches when we draw it over the sacks.”

“Did you try it?”

“Twice.”

“Then leave it open until you know where it catches. If you tie it again now, someone will have to undo it.”

He turned toward the boy and his board.

“Who has added a load?”

“Two baskets for the men above the bend,” the boy said.

The woman stepped forward. “My brother bought the food. The men have been waiting since morning.”

“Who agreed to carry them?”

She pointed at me.

“I told her I would ask about room,” I said.

“You told me they had a place.”

Jamil looked at me. “Have you been aboard?”

“No.”

“Then come and see what is already promised.”

He said it to me, not to the woman. She gathered the loose binding in her hand.

“Will you take the food?” she asked.

“I have not looked at it,” Jamil said.

She made a sound of impatience. “It is food, not stones.”

“The men who will eat it may care where it goes. The men carrying it will have to lift it first.”

He went down the steps. I followed him onto the board, waiting for the man ahead to clear it before I put my weight on it. The boat shifted beneath my foot. I caught the side with one hand.

The younger man with the patched cloth was already aboard. He set it down where it would not be stepped on.

“This is Safi,” Jamil said. “He has been asking the people whose goods are here what they expect of us.”

Safi gave me a short look. His clothes were finer than those of the men moving the sacks, but he had dirt on one knee and a red mark across his palm where something had rubbed against it.

“I know who Ache is,” he said.

I could not tell whether he meant it kindly. I had seen him once across Siripada’s courtyard. We had not spoken.

A man moved a bundle from the middle of the boat and laid it against the side. It looked to me as though he had opened enough room for the woman’s baskets.

“There,” I said. “Could they not stand beside that one?”

The man holding the bundle looked up.

“For how long?”

“Until you reach the upper bend.”

He pushed the bundle back with his knee.

“Then where will I put my foot when we take the long pieces aboard?”

I looked toward the steps. Three men were bringing poles down from a yard. They were longer than the space I had pointed to.

“They could lie across the top,” I said.

The man gave me the same look he had given the boy.

“On the food? On the bundles? Who will keep them from shifting?”

“I only meant—”

“I know what you meant. I am asking where you want them.”

Jamil held up his hand before I answered. He asked the man to show us what he had been trying to do.

The man drew one of the poles partway into the boat. Another worker caught its far end. Together they lowered it along the side, then lifted it again when it struck the edge of the covered load. The man who had been kneeling beneath the covering had to move out of the way. His mat remained half spread.

“Not there,” the second worker said. “The binding has to come over the top.”

He drew the pole back. The two men set it along the steps until they could move the other load.

The empty-looking place I had chosen was empty because they had been working through it.

Safi pointed to a stack beneath the covering. “Those sacks were accepted yesterday. The man who brought them has already paid for their handling.”

“Have they paid the boatman?” Jamil asked.

Safi looked toward the older man by the stern.

“He says they have agreed on payment after delivery. I have not heard it from the owner.”

“Then that is what you know.”

Safi's mouth tightened. He did not argue, though I saw that he disliked being corrected before me.

Jamil asked the man with the mat whether the covering could be secured with the poles aboard. The man pulled its edge down over the nearest bundle and showed him a worn place where the fastening would take the strain.

“If it tears here, what happens?” Jamil asked.

“We stop and tie it again, if there is enough left to tie.”

“Can you mend it before we leave?”

“If you give me the cloth and someone keeps people from walking on it.”

Safi picked up the folded piece he had brought. He held it out to the man.

“I found this in the yard. Is it enough?”

The man unfolded it, held the patches against the worn edge, and shook his head.

“Enough to cover the hole. Not enough to take the pull.”

Safi took it back without a word. I had expected him to know the answer before he asked. Instead he went to look for another piece.

I moved nearer the side of the boat. From there I could see the woman waiting with her baskets. She had begun to retie one of the covers. A boy who was not ours stopped beside her and asked whether she was waiting for the same boat. She answered him sharply. He went away.

Jamil stood beside me.

“Can you see the place you offered?”

“Yes.”

“Is it available?”

“Not while they are moving the poles.”

“Afterward?”

I looked at the sacks, the mat, the folded covering, the space through which the men kept passing. I wanted to say that the two baskets were small. I had carried one. I knew their weight better than anyone aboard.

“I don't know,” I said.

“Then we agree.”

He went to speak with the older boatman. I remained where I was. A carrier stepped onto the board with a sack across his back. He stopped when he saw me in his path.

I moved aside, but my foot caught the edge of the mat. The man who had been mending it pulled it free before I tore it.

“Stay where you can see without making another place for us to clear,” he said.

I stepped against the side and stayed there.

The carrier set down his sack, wiped his forehead, and asked the older boatman when the remaining load would come. He was being paid for the sacks he brought, he said, and had been waiting since the sun was lower. The boatman told him that another man had gone to fetch them.

“If I wait for him, I lose the next carrying job,” the carrier said.

“Then go,” the boatman answered.

“And leave these without knowing whether they will be taken?”

The boatman did not reply. The carrier stayed, but he sat on the steps instead of returning to the yard.

Safi came back with a different strip of cloth. The man under the covering tested it and nodded. Safi crouched to help him hold the edge steady while he worked. The red mark on Safi’s palm opened a little when he pulled. He shifted his grip and went on.

I moved toward them, then stopped. The man had already fitted the cloth against Safi’s hand.

Jamil returned from the stern.

“The baskets are for men working above the bend,” he said. “The woman says they cannot wait until tomorrow. Can this boat take them without putting off what was agreed?”

The older boatman looked at the cargo and then at the men waiting on the steps.

“Not until I know whether the last sacks are coming. If they are, I need the place by the side.”

The older boatman turned to Jamil. “If you tell me to take the baskets, I will take them. Tell me which load I am to leave.”

Jamil said nothing for a moment. I watched him look from the covered sacks to the waiting carrier, and then toward the woman.

“No one is leaving an accepted load because Ache spoke too soon,” he said. “Finish the covering. We will decide when the rest is here.”

He did not tell the woman to go. He did not tell her to stay.

I went back across the board. The boy with the tally followed me, but he stopped when I turned.

“Did you take the marks off?” I asked.

“Which marks?”

“The two baskets.”

He showed me the board. Near the bottom, he had made two short cuts apart from the others.

“They are still there,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because the baskets are still waiting.”

He had not written that the boat would carry them. I had been angry with him for repeating my promise, but his marks told less than my words had.

The woman watched me approach.

“Well?”

“They are seeing whether the rest of the load is coming.”

“And if it comes?”

“Then there may be no place.”

She stared at me for a long moment. “My brother was told to bring the food here.”

“I know.”

“You don't know who told him.”

“No.”

“Then find out before you tell me anything else.”

She lifted the smaller basket and held it toward me.

“Take this back to the shade. It will spoil sooner if it stands in the sun.”

I took it. I had wanted to carry it onto the boat. Instead I carried it the few steps back to the wall.

Behind me, a man called that the remaining sacks had been found. The carrier on the steps stood up quickly and asked how many there were. No one answered him at once.

Safi was coming off the boat with the torn piece of covering in his hands. He saw me setting the basket down.

“Jamil wants us both here when the last sacks arrive,” he said.

“For what?”

“To decide what can be taken without leaving someone else's work behind.”

“You already know the loads.”

“I know what people told me. That is not always what comes down the steps.”

He went past me toward the yard. I looked at the woman's baskets, then at the marks on the boy's board. I still wanted to find a way to put the baskets aboard before Safi returned.


* * *

The remaining sacks came down the passage on two men's shoulders. The carrier who had been waiting on the steps stood to meet them, then stopped when he saw the man behind them carrying nothing.

“Where is the third?”

“Still in the yard,” one of the men said. “Its binding has come apart.”

“Then why did you tell me the rest was coming?”

“I said we had found them.”

The carrier looked toward the yard, where another man was calling for a length of cord. He had already waited long enough to miss the work he meant to take after this. I could see him deciding whether to follow the third sack or stay with the two that were here.

Jamil called us away from the board before the men tried to cross it. Safi had brought the repaired covering, folded so that its new edge was on top. He put it on a dry step and held one corner down with his foot.

The older boatman came to stand beside Jamil. The boy with the tally board stayed close enough to hear, but not close enough to be asked to lift anything.

“How much is still to go aboard?” Jamil asked.

The older man pointed out the sacks on the steps, the one in the yard, and the poles that lay along the landing. He named two bundles already under the covering which had to be moved before the poles could lie against the side.

“Who is taking the poles?” Jamil asked.

“The two who brought them.”

“And the covering?”

The man who had mended it raised his hand. He had a needle stuck through the fold of his cloth.

“I will fasten it when the long pieces are down. Not before.”

Jamil looked at Safi and me. “You have both been looking at this boat. Tell us what can be done without putting off the load already accepted.”

Safi spoke first. I was grateful for that. It gave me time to watch the men and think of an answer that sounded less like a request.

“Move the two bundles to the stern while the poles come in,” Safi said. “Keep the passage clear until the last sack is aboard. Then put the bundles back and fasten the covering. No one should stand waiting with a pole while a man is still crossing the board.”

The older boatman shook his head.

“The bundles cannot go all the way aft. The man steering will need that place.”

Safi glanced toward the stern. “Then as far as the next crosspiece.”

“That leaves enough,” the boatman said, though he looked at the men before he said it.

Safi nodded and continued. He had counted the two men with the poles and the man with the covering. He had not counted the carrier who was waiting for his pay.

I had been looking at the woman's baskets. The two marks on the boy's board were still apart from the others. If I could make them part of the load, perhaps she would stop looking at me as though I had taken something from her.

“When the bundles are moved,” I said, “the baskets could go beside them. The poles can be held up while they pass.”

The man with the needle laughed once, without much pleasure.

“Held up by whom?”

“The men bringing them.”

“They have to bring them,” he said. “Their hands will be on the poles.”

“Then someone could steady the far ends.”

I looked toward the carrier. He was standing beside the sacks, listening.

“You have been waiting here,” I said to him. “If you helped with the poles, it would be done sooner.”

“Would it?”

He looked at the older boatman, not at me.

“Who pays me for that?”

“Perhaps the boatman—”

“No,” the older man said. “I have paid for the sacks to be carried down. I have not hired him for the poles.”

“I can ask whether he will do it,” I said.

“You have asked,” the carrier answered.

He rubbed the strap mark across his shoulder. “I will bring the last sack if they mend it before I go. After that I have other work. I have lost enough of the day here.”

He had not raised his voice. That made the space after his answer harder to bear.

Jamil asked me to finish my proposal.

I could have said I had none. Instead I pointed to the place beside the covered bundles.

“Two baskets do not weigh as much as a sack. They are food. The men above the bend need them today. If we leave them here while we argue about one pair of hands, what is the use of getting the other goods away?”

The woman was still in the shade, too far off to hear every word. I could see her looking toward us. Her brother had not returned.

Safi turned toward me. “You are asking the boat to carry goods it has not accepted.”

“I am asking whether it can.”

“You have already told the woman it will.”

“I did not say that.”

The boy's thumb moved across the two marks on his board. He said nothing.

Jamil lifted a hand. “Let him finish.”

For a moment I was grateful to him. Then I saw the older boatman's expression. Jamil had stopped Safi for me, but he had not made any room in the boat.

“My grandfather has taken me into his house,” I said. “I am trying to see that the people who come to him are not sent away without an answer.”

There it was: the name I had held back at the receiving house, now offered as though it could keep a pole steady.

The man with the needle looked down at the covering. The carrier shifted his weight and turned toward the yard. Safi did not look away.

Jamil said, “Your grandfather did not accept these baskets for this boat.”

“I know.”

“Then leave him out of the count.”

I wanted to say that I had mentioned him only to explain why I was there. That was not true. I had mentioned him because the men were listening less readily than I had expected.

The older boatman crouched and put his hand against one of the sacks.

“There is another thing,” he said to Safi. “You have the long pieces going in before the last sack. If that sack is brought after they are tied down, the carrier will have to lift it over them.”

Safi knelt beside him. He traced the route from the board to the place where the sacks would lie.

“Then the last sack comes first,” he said.

“If it comes,” the carrier answered.

“It will need a sound binding,” Safi said. “I cannot count it as aboard until it is.”

Jamil's face tightened. He had wanted the boat made ready before the light changed, and I could tell he disliked how much time our answers were taking. He looked toward the yard.

“Send someone to hurry them.”

The carrier stepped away from the sacks.

“If you send the men with the poles, they will not be here to bring the poles,” he said.

Jamil looked back at him. The carrier stood still, as if he had already said more than was wise.

“Who can go?” Jamil asked.

The boy raised his hand.

“You have a board to keep,” the older boatman told him. “Stay where the goods come past you.”

A man who had finished carrying the first two sacks offered to go. He said he would bring back the answer, not the sack, and no one mistook the two.

We waited while he went. The sacks stayed on the steps.

Safi turned the folded covering over so its repaired edge faced the man with the needle.

“Let him check this again before we put it on,” he said. “I found the cloth, but he knows where it will take the pull.”

The man tested the seam between his fingers. He asked for another stitch at one corner. Safi held the cloth while he made it.

Jamil asked the older boatman whether Safi's order would work if the last sack came in time.

“It will,” the man said. “We move the two bundles, take the sack, then the poles. We leave the passage open until everyone is clear. After that we can close the covering.”

“And the food?” Jamil asked.

The boatman looked toward the shade.

“Ask the owner of this boat. I have said what I can load with the men I have. I have not said he will carry more.”

Jamil turned to me. “Your order needs another worker who has refused it. Safi's does not. We will use his.”

Heat rose beneath my collar. Safi had corrected his plan when the boatman objected. I had brought my grandfather into an argument over one pair of hands.

Safi did not smile. He was already helping the man fold the covering again.

The messenger from the yard came back alone. The binding on the third sack had been repaired, he said, but they needed the carrier to bring it down.

The carrier picked up his strap. He looked at Jamil.

“When it is here, I want to know what I am owed. I will not wait another hour to be told the owner has gone away.”

The older boatman agreed to speak to the owner before the sack was moved. The carrier went to fetch it.

The boy held up his board to Safi. “Do I take off the two marks?”

“No,” Safi said. “The baskets are still here.”

“Then what do I write beside them?”

“Nothing yet.”

I could hear the woman calling from the shade. She had seen the men begin moving the accepted load and thought that meant her food was next.

Safi glanced at me, then toward her. He could have gone to tell her the boat was full. He had won the right to speak about its loading. Instead he left the two marks where they were.

“You spoke to her,” he said. “She will ask you what you meant.”

I went toward the steps. I had an answer now, but it was smaller than the one I had given her before.


* * *

The woman had moved her baskets out of the shade. One stood against the wall; she held the other by its rim, turning it every little while as though the movement could keep what was inside from spoiling.

“You said they would take them,” she called when she saw me.

I stopped beside the steps. The boy with the board had followed us from the landing. Behind him, men were shifting the bundles Safi had told them to move. I could hear a pole scraping against the side of the boat.

“They have not agreed to take them,” I said.

“You told me you would see to it. The boy asked whether you meant both, and you said yes.”

“I did. I had not asked the boatman.”

“Then ask the owner now.”

“I can ask. I cannot promise what he will say.”

“That is what you should have said when there was still time to carry these somewhere else.”

Her brother came through the passage as she spoke. He was a narrow man with a wet patch down one side of his tunic. He had been running, or carrying something that had leaked. He stopped when he saw the baskets on the ground.

“Why are they here?”

She pointed at me. “He said there was room.”

The man turned to the boat. “Who accepted them?”

“No one,” I said.

“Then who told you to bring them?”

She answered before I could. “The man from the upper bend said to come before the light changed. He said there would be a boat loading here.”

“He said there would be a boat,” her brother repeated. “Did he say whose?”

“He didn't know.”

The brother rubbed the wet side of his tunic with his palm, though it had already soaked through.

“I paid for the food,” he said to me. “I did not pay for it to stand in a passage.”

I thought of offering to carry one basket to the upper bend myself. I did not know how far it was by the bank, whether the path remained passable, or whether a man on foot could reach the workers before their day ended. My feet were free. That was nearly all I knew.

Safi came up the steps with the repaired covering folded over his arm. He stopped when he saw the brother.

“The two baskets?” he asked.

“Yes,” the woman said. “Your friend has been explaining.”

“He is not my friend,” Safi answered.

I looked at him. He kept his eyes on the baskets.

“They are still outside the accepted load,” he said. “The boy has kept the marks apart. No one will put them aboard while the owner is being asked.”

The brother stared at him. “Then the owner is being asked?”

“Jamil is speaking to him. I don't know his answer.”

Safi had learned to say those words without looking ashamed of them. I hated that I noticed.

The woman stood. “You have made a rule for the marks. Does that get food to the men?”

“No,” Safi said. “It keeps another man's goods from being taken off the boat while we decide.”

“If the sacks were promised first, they go first,” she said. “I am asking why I was told to wait here.”

Safi glanced at me. He did not answer for me.

The boy shifted his board under his arm. I could see him remembering the words he had carried down the passage. The man with the folded covering was coming toward us, having been sent for another fastening. He had heard the boy tell the boatmen that I wanted room held.

“Tell him,” Safi said.

“I have told her.”

“The boy told the men. They still think you sent him to reserve a place.”

The boy's face tightened. “I said what I heard.”

“I know,” I told him.

I raised my voice enough for the man with the covering to hear.

“When I told her I would see that her baskets had a place, I had not asked the boatman or the owner. I could not reserve that space. The two marks are for goods waiting to be considered, not goods accepted.”

The boy held the board out so I could see the cuts. “They were always apart.”

“Then leave them apart.”

The man with the covering shifted the cloth from one shoulder to the other. “We have not moved a load for them.”

“I know.”

“Good,” he said. “Don't make us move it now.”

He went into the yard.

The brother stepped closer. “If you had told us there was no promise, I could have gone back to the man who sent my sister. He might have known another way.”

“You can still ask him,” I said.

“He has gone above the bend.”

I looked toward the water. Boats were tied at other steps. A man was washing mud from the side of one of them, and two boys were dragging a length of wood across the landing. I could not tell which boats were being prepared to leave.

“Perhaps one of those—”

“No,” the woman said.

I turned back.

“You will not send me to another boat with perhaps. If you know a man who has agreed, tell me his name. If you do not, stop.”

I had no name.

Behind Safi, the carrier returned with the third sack on his back. The binding had been repaired with a length of cord that looked newer than the sack itself. He waited for the board to be cleared before he crossed. The older boatman came to meet him.

“Have you spoken to the owner?” the carrier asked.

“Not yet.”

“You told me you would.”

“I told you I would speak to him before the sack went aboard.”

The carrier lowered his load to the step without crossing. His shoulders moved as he worked them free of the strap.

“Then it stays here.”

Jamil appeared beside the boat. He had been speaking with a man near the stern. When he saw the sack on the steps, he called to the carrier to bring it across.

The carrier did not move.

“The pay,” he said.

Jamil came nearer. “It will be settled.”

“By whom?”

“The owner is here.”

“Then let him say it.”

For a moment Jamil looked as if he might order the sack carried aboard anyway. I saw Safi watch him, and the older boatman turn his face toward the stern. The man who owned the boat finally came forward. He and the carrier spoke over the sack, naming the number already delivered and what remained owed for the last one. The owner counted out the agreed payment before the carrier lifted it again.

Jamil had been willing to make the carrier wait once more. Safi said nothing about it. Neither did I. I was ashamed of my relief when the man finally carried the sack across.

The woman had watched the exchange.

“They can make a man stand there with his work until someone pays him,” she said. “I have stood here with mine. Who is supposed to answer me?”

Jamil heard her and came up the steps.

“Will the boat take the food?” she asked him.

He looked back at the owner, who was watching the last sack being set down, and called him over. The owner listened to the woman's account of the workers above the bend. He lifted the cover of the nearer basket just enough to see how the portions were packed, then lowered it again.

“Where do you want it landed?” he asked.

“Above the bend where the bank is being repaired.”

“Who will meet it?”

“My brother's men.”

“Are they waiting at the landing or at the work?”

“At the work.”

“Then someone has to leave this boat and carry it from the landing. I have agreed to take the sacks and poles. I have not agreed to deliver food to men I cannot see.”

The brother said he could arrange for someone to meet the boat if they would wait while he went.

“We are nearly loaded,” the owner answered. “I will not wait for a man who may or may not be there.”

The woman drew the basket toward her with her foot. “So it is no.”

“It is no for this boat,” he said.

Jamil did not contradict him. Safi was looking at the completed load as though he wanted to make certain nothing had been displaced. The boy took his board down and looked at the two separate marks.

“Do I cut them out?” he asked.

“Leave them until she takes the baskets away,” Safi said. “Then we know why they did not go.”

The woman gave him a sharp look. “You will have a record of it. That will be some comfort.”

Safi's face colored. “If they are still marked as waiting, someone may ask for them again.”

“Then say they were refused,” she answered.

The boy touched the marks with his thumb. “I can tell them.”

Her brother picked up the heavier basket. He tested the binding and put it down again to tighten it.

“I will go to the man who sent us,” he told her. “He may have someone on the bank.”

“You have been gone once already.”

“I know.”

“Then do not tell me he will help until you have spoken to him.”

I reached for the smaller basket. “I can carry this while you wait.”

“You carried it well enough before,” the woman said. “That was never the trouble.”

She let me take it. We moved it to the deeper shade beneath the wall, where the ground was cooler. She set the other beside it and loosened the coverings so the trapped warmth could escape. I had thought the leaf wrappings would keep the food safe. I could smell it now, a sourness beneath the herbs.

“Will the men eat it if it arrives late?” I asked.

“They may. My brother still has to pay the people who prepared it, whether they eat it or not.”

I wanted to say I would repay him. I had no money of my own to cover the baskets, and I would have been promising with my grandfather's house again.

She looked toward the water. “I could have sent my brother to look for another boat when I first came. Instead I stood here because you said both baskets had a place.”

“Yes.”

“Do you understand how long that was?”

The light had moved across the wall while she waited.

“I saw you wait,” I said.

“That is not what I asked.”

I could not tell her how much time she had lost. I had never asked when she arrived.

Safi called from the landing. The last pole was aboard. The men were drawing the covering over the load, and the mender had taken his place at the fastening. They would soon be finished.

The woman did not turn to watch them. She kept one hand on the smaller basket, holding the loose cover clear of the food. When her brother came back, she would have to decide what could still be delivered. I had nothing useful to tell her until then.

I went to the steps where the boy was waiting with his board.

“Those two marks,” I said. “When she leaves, tell them why.”

“Why what?”

“Why they were not taken.”

He looked at the baskets, then at the boat. “Because the owner said no.”

“Yes.”

“And because you said yes first?”

I could feel Safi listening behind me.

“Yes,” I said.

The boy drew his thumb across the empty space beside the marks. He did not add anything yet.


* * *

The boy was still looking at the two marks when the woman's brother came back.

He had nothing in his hands. I knew what that meant before his sister asked.

"The man who sent us has gone to the work," he said. "The people at his house don't know whether he found another boat."

She put the covering back over the smaller basket. The leaves had darkened where the food pressed against them.

"Then we take these home."

"They may still want them."

"They wanted them before the men stopped for food."

Her brother lifted the heavier basket. She took the other herself. When I offered to help, she turned her shoulder away.

"You have helped me carry them already."

The boy waited until they had passed the corner. Then he put his board on his knee and made a short cut beside each of the two marks. He held it up for me to see.

"Refused?" he asked.

"Yes."

"By the owner?"

"Yes."

"And the rest?"

I knew what he meant. He had heard me tell the woman why she had waited. He was asking how much of that answer belonged on his board.

"Ask Safi what the marks are for," I said.

The boy gave me a look I had begun to recognize. It was the look he wore when an older person told him to find another older person.

"You're the one who told me to put them there."

I could have told him to make another cut. I had already learned what happened when I used a board I did not keep to make a decision I did not own.

"Tell him what happened," I said. "Don't say I sent you to change it."

He went toward the landing with the board against his ribs.

The boat was almost ready. The mender had drawn the covering tight along one side and was feeling beneath it for the fastening he had missed. Safi stood near the stern, speaking to the owner. Neither looked toward me.

I went through the yard behind the receiving house, where a woman was counting small bundles on a mat. Each was tied with a strip of leaf. Another woman had opened a covered jar beside her and was drawing out dried fish in pieces no longer than her hand.

"Six for the lower steps," the first woman said.

"That is what you told me yesterday."

"Yesterday there were six men."

"Today there are eight."

The first woman looked at the bundles, then toward a figure standing at the far side of the mat.

I had seen her in the larger house, though not near enough to speak. She wore a plain outer cloth, with a narrow band at the edge that had been worked more carefully than the rest. She had been listening without moving the bundles.

"How many were promised?" she asked.

"Six," the first woman said.

"And who sent the other two?"

"Jamil asked for men to finish the fastening before the boat went."

"Then someone should have told me before I sent the food."

The woman by the jar folded her arms. "We have enough fish. The rice is for the evening household."

"How much rice can be taken without leaving the people here short?"

"I can tell you what is in the pot. I cannot tell you what the cook will permit."

The figure beside the mat looked up and found me standing in the passage.

"You have been watching for some time."

"I was passing through."

"Then pass through. We are counting food."

I might have done as she said if I had not recognized her. Someone had called her Laila in the house, and a servant had moved aside when she came through a doorway. I had imagined that someone with such a place would find it easy to ask for two more portions.

"Are those for the men at the boat?" I asked.

"Some of them."

"Jamil has finished deciding the load."

"That does not feed the men who finished it."

I looked at the six bundles. She saw me count them.

"There are eight men now," she said. "That is what I am trying to settle."

"I could carry a message to the kitchen."

"The woman standing beside the jar has already gone there."

I glanced at her. She looked tired enough to have made the journey more than once.

Laila lifted one of the bundles, tested its binding, and set it back. "If I take two portions from the evening food without asking, the cook will have to answer for them. If I leave the men hungry, the person who promised them food will have to answer."

"Who promised it?"

"I did."

She did not say it proudly. She sounded irritated that the answer had to be repeated.

"Can you tell the cook it is your decision?"

"I can. I would rather tell her how I intend to replace what I take."

The woman by the jar smiled at that, but only a little.

"I was looking for someone who might carry words to Maynila," I said.

Laila took her hand from the mat.

"From this yard?"

"From someone who knows the boats."

"I know who brings food to this house. That is not the same as knowing who will cross the sea."

"I thought you might have someone going west."

"Why?"

I had no answer better than her place in the house. I did not want to say it.

"My message has been waiting for days."

"Then it will still be waiting if I send it to a man who does not know where he is sailing."

"I am asking whether you know one."

"You came to ask me while I was counting food for men I have already promised to feed."

I felt my face grow hot. "I can wait."

"That would help."

She turned back to the woman with the jar. They agreed that the six bundles would go at once. The other two men would be told that food was being prepared, not that it was ready. Laila sent a boy to find the cook and ask what could be taken from the evening pot and what must be replaced. He repeated the request before he left.

I recognized the care with which she made him repeat it. I did not enjoy recognizing it.

When the six bundles were carried away, Laila stayed beside the mat. She rubbed a spot of dried rice from its edge with her thumb.

"Who told you I could find a carrier?" she asked.

"No one."

"Then you have been making guesses."

"Someone said a western trader might arrive."

"Has he?"

"Not that I know."

"Does he carry letters?"

"I don't know."

"Does he go to Maynila?"

"I don't know."

She looked toward the passage where the boy had gone.

"I have a small boat that crosses to my mother's people," she said. "It is used to carry food and people who have business there. If you ask me to send a message across that water, I can tell you who would take it and who would receive it. I cannot turn that crossing into a voyage to your mother."

I had not asked her to do so. I had wanted her to offer a way that I could not name.

A man came through the passage carrying a narrow board under his arm. His beard was threaded with grey. He stopped when he saw Laila.

"Hasan," she said. "Ache has a question about what was said at the landing. He can ask it himself."

I had seen Hasan speaking with men from more than one trading boat. He listened closely even when the speaker stumbled. I had thought that made him a man who could tell what people meant.

"I told a woman I would see that her baskets had a place," I said. "She heard that they would go. The boy who carried the words heard the same thing. I meant that I would ask."

Hasan set his board down.

"Which words did you use?"

I repeated them as nearly as I could. He asked what the woman had said before I answered. I told him that too.

"Did you say you had asked the owner?"

"No."

"Did you say the owner had agreed?"

"No."

"Did you tell her to wait?"

"She said she would wait. I let her."

"Then I can tell you what you did not say," Hasan answered. "I cannot tell her that she ought to have heard the words you kept in your head."

I waited for him to go on. He did not.

"The boy heard them differently."

"Did you ask the boy what he understood before he went?"

"No."

"Then we have three accounts, and you have brought me only yours."

Laila was looking toward the yard. She had not asked Hasan to defend me, and he did not seem inclined to do it.

"Was anyone present when you corrected the promise?"

"The woman, her brother, the boy. Safi heard part of it. A man with a covering heard me say I had no authority."

"Then those are the people who know what was corrected. I was not there."

He lifted his board again. A man called for him from the passage. Hasan answered that he would come when he had finished speaking, and the caller went away.

"Can you tell me whether a trader will take my message?" I asked.

"Not without speaking to the trader."

"Can you ask him?"

"When there is one to ask."

He moved toward the doorway, then stopped.

"Bring Sulad when you go back to the witnesses. You said he has words of his own."

"I know."

"Does he know when you intend to come?"

"No."

"Then begin there."

He went out before I could ask another question.

Laila watched him leave.

"He enjoys making people state a question twice," she said.

"He gave me very little answer."

"He had very little to answer."

The boy she had sent to the cook returned with a woman carrying a covered pot. She set it down beside the mat and lifted the lid. Steam came out, and I could smell rice beneath it.

"The cook will give two portions," the boy said. "She says the dried fish for tomorrow must come from your store, not hers."

Laila's mouth tightened. "She was told I would replace what I took today."

"She said tomorrow is when she will see whether you meant it."

The woman by the jar laughed once, then stopped when Laila looked at her.

"Take the two portions," Laila said. "I will speak to her before evening."

"You said you would send six," the woman answered.

"I sent six. These are for the men who were added."

The woman filled two leaf wrappings and tied them. Laila did not take them from her. She waited while the boy who had come from the cook counted both aloud.

I moved aside to let him pass.

"Do you want me to go with him?" I asked.

"No. He knows where the men are. You would be another person to feed if you stayed."

I could not tell whether she meant to be unkind. The boy was already carrying the portions out of the yard.

Laila gathered the loose ties from the mat and placed them in the jar for reuse. She had found food for the two men. She still had to account for it to the cook, and tomorrow's fish would come from her own store. I wondered whether the cook would believe her when she came.

"I could speak for you," I said.

"To the cook?"

"To whoever asks why you took it."

"Would you tell them what you saw, or would you tell them I had a right to take it?"

I thought of the man with the covering and the boy with the board.

"What I saw," I said.

"Then if someone asks you, answer that. Do not go looking for a person to persuade."

She took the jar of spare ties from the mat and went into the house. I stayed long enough to hear the woman who had counted the first six bundles ask whether there would be enough for tomorrow.

"Ask Laila," the woman with the jar said. "She has said it will come from her store."

The first woman did not look satisfied.


* * *

The boy had turned his board over to make room for the next load. I watched him draw three new marks, each one after a man had crossed the landing with a sack. When a fourth man came through without carrying anything, the boy held his hand still.

"Is he coming back?" I asked.

"Ask him."

The man had gone into the yard. I could see his bare feet beneath the hanging edge of a mat, but I could not see what he was doing there.

"You know him," I said.

"I know he carried one. I don't know whether he will carry another."

I left the boy to his work. Behind me, someone called my name.

Jamil stood by the wall with another covering folded over his arm. A long tear ran along one edge; this one had not yet been mended. Safi was beside him, holding a short length of cord with a knot at each end.

"The boat has gone," Jamil said.

"I saw."

"The owner took the load we agreed upon. The food did not go aboard."

"I saw that too."

"Then come with us."

I followed them into the yard. I had expected Jamil to take me to Siripada's house. Instead he stopped beside a smaller boat drawn up where the mud gave way to stones. It lay on its side, with one edge propped above the ground. A man sat beneath the raised edge, pulling at a fastening that would not come free.

"Is this the next boat?" I asked.

"It belongs to a man who has asked whether we can help him prepare it," Jamil said.

"For where?"

"He has not told me."

I looked along its length. A rolled mat rested inside. Near the bow, a coil of cord lay under a loose piece of wood. It seemed an easy vessel to ready after the one we had just watched leave.

"That fastening is worn," Safi said. He pointed with the cord in his hand. "The man asked for two hands to hold the side while he works."

Jamil set down the folded covering.

"How many hands have we?"

"Here?" I asked.

"That is one question."

Safi said, "The two who finished the covering have gone to eat. The mender said he might return."

"Did he say he would?" Jamil asked.

"No."

"The man beneath the boat is here," I said.

"He is working," Safi answered. "He asked for help. You cannot count him as the help he needs."

I looked toward the passage. A carrier I recognized was walking out with a strap wound around his wrist. He had been at the landing when we argued about the sacks.

"He is free now," I said.

The carrier heard me. He stopped.

"Free of what?"

"The last load is gone."

"Yes."

"Would you help lift this side?"

He looked at the propped boat, then at the strap in his hand.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Who pays?"

I looked at Jamil. He did not answer for me.

"The owner may pay," I said.

"Then the owner may ask."

He went on. I watched him turn into the passage, angry at the way he had made me sound as though I had offered him nothing.

The man beneath the boat pushed himself into the open. He pointed back to the wooden peg still caught in the hull.

"I need the side steady long enough to take this out without splitting the wood," he said. "I do not need a boy telling every man who passes that there is work."

"Will the peg come free?" Jamil asked.

"It may. The wood has swollen around it. I would rather wait for the owner than break it."

Jamil bent to look. His knee pressed into the stones. He reached for the peg, but the man raised a hand between Jamil's fingers and the wood.

"If you pull it now, the wood may go with it."

Jamil straightened. He did not seem pleased.

"Then leave it."

"I was leaving it," the man said.

Safi looked away. I thought he was hiding a smile. When he turned back, his face was plain.

Jamil picked up the covering again. "We have another length to mend before the next load can be covered. Safi, you know what has already been promised. Go and see whether the mender will take it, and what he wants for the work. Do not tell him he must."

Jamil handed him the folded covering. Safi wound the short cord around his palm.

"And the people who brought the poles?"

"Find out whether they need the covering returned. We cannot give it to someone else until we know."

Safi nodded. He had a task that could change what happened to goods already accepted. I stood beside a boat whose owner had not even told us where he meant to go.

"Come here," Jamil said.

He found a narrow offcut of wood beside the wall, rubbed dirt from one side with his thumb, and gave it to me.

"Find out what this boat needs before anyone offers men or cloth for it. Begin with the man who is working."

"What do I mark?"

"What he tells you. And what you see. Keep them apart."

I turned the wood in my hand. There was room for a few cuts along its edge. I could make the marks, but there were too many things to ask and no place to put the answers.

"How will you know which mark means which?"

"You will tell me. If you cannot remember, do not make the mark yet."

Safi moved toward the passage. Before he left, he glanced at the wood.

"Ask whether the owner has paid for the repair," he said.

"I know to ask that."

"You knew to ask the carrier too."

I wanted to answer him. Jamil was already walking away, and Safi followed with the folded covering. Neither waited to hear what I might say.

I crouched beside the boat.

The man had gone back under its side. I could see one hand braced against the wood and the other holding the peg.

"Who owns it?" I asked.

He gave me a name I had not heard before.

"Is he coming?"

"He said he would come when he had finished at the other landing."

"Did he say when?"

"No."

"What must be done?"

"Hold the side. Remove the peg. See whether the hole will take another without opening the split."

"Then the boat can go?"

He came out far enough to look at me.

"Then we will know whether the peg can be replaced."

I studied the edge he had shown Jamil. A thin dark line ran from the hole toward a place where the grain changed. It might have been a crack. I did not know enough to tell.

"Can you mend it?"

"Perhaps. If I can take the peg out whole."

"Do you have another?"

"No."

"Does the owner?"

"He says he does."

I almost made a cut. Instead I asked, "Have you seen it?"

"No."

The man watched my hand above the wood.

"That is why I am waiting."

He went back beneath the side. I stayed on the stones and tried to remember his answers. There was one worker here. He could not hold the side and remove the peg alone. The owner was absent. A replacement peg was said to exist, but no one had brought it. The repair might be small, or it might open the wood.

I cut one mark near the edge for the man who was here. Beside it I left a space for the hands he needed. I could have cut two more marks and called them expected.

Jamil returned after the light had shifted away from the wall. He had no one with him.

"How many?" he asked.

"One man working. He needs two to hold the side."

"Two more?"

"That is what he asked for. I have not found them."

"What else?"

"The peg is caught. He says the owner has another, but he has not seen it. He cannot tell whether the hole will hold until he takes this one out."

Jamil took the offcut and looked at the single mark.

"Why only one?"

"That is the man who is here."

"And these spaces?"

"Those are the men he needs."

"Can they be found?"

"I have not asked enough people to know. The carrier who passed said the owner must ask him about pay."

"Good."

I felt a quick satisfaction. Then Jamil added, "You asked him before you knew the work or the pay."

"Yes."

"He may not listen the next time."

The man beneath the boat called that the peg had shifted a little. Jamil knelt again. This time he kept his hands clear while the man showed him the movement.

Jamil looked toward the yard.

"I thought the men from the first boat would still be here."

"They went to eat," I said. "Safi said the mender might come back."

"I know what Safi said."

He rose and wiped his hand against his knee. He looked tired, and I wondered how many people had asked him to decide something before he had seen it.

"Tomorrow you will come to this yard before the first load reaches the landing," he said. "Ask the people working which hands they have agreed to bring, what work has been accepted, and what is still waiting for an owner to answer. Tell me before you speak to anyone else about a place aboard."

"Am I to do that for every boat?"

"For the ones I send you to. If you are not sent, you may watch. You may not promise."

I looked at the offcut in his hand.

"Will I carry the count?"

"You may bring it to me. I will check it against the people who made the bargains."

"And Safi?"

"Safi will see to the accepted loads and the covering. He has earned that work."

I did not want Safi to hear that. He had already gone, and the words seemed to follow him anyway.

"Will my grandfather know?" I asked.

Jamil studied me. For a moment I wished I had asked about the peg instead.

"He may ask what you have done. Then I will tell him."

He gave back the wood. I had expected something more for my day's embarrassment. A word from Siripada, perhaps, or a task that would take me near a ship bound west. I had a piece of wood and an instruction to arrive before the carriers.

"What of the message I need sent to Maynila?"

"Has a master agreed to carry it?"

"No."

"Then I have no voyage to put it on."

"Do you know a man who will carry it?"

"Today I do not. If I hear of one, I will tell you."

I wanted him to say he would try. He did not.

I turned the offcut over. There was room on the other side for more marks.

"Sulad has words to add before the witnesses," I said. "He is working farther along the bank."

"Then speak to Sulad."

"He may not come when I ask."

"Then you will know he has not agreed."

Jamil went toward the passage. The man beneath the boat asked whether he should wait any longer. Jamil stopped and told him the owner must answer for the extra hands and the peg. I stayed beside the propped boat while the man put away his tool. He drew a mat over the exposed fastening so no one would strike it in the dark. Then he took his things and went.

The boat remained where it was. I could see the gap beneath its side and the place where the peg had not come free.

On my piece of wood I had marked one hand. Beside it were two empty spaces.

I knew where Sulad had said he would be working. I could go there when the men were done and ask whether he wished to speak his own words. I tucked the wood beneath my arm. The empty spaces were still there when I reached the passage.
