Raya finished the line and told me to let go.

I had been holding the timber above the mud with both hands. When I eased it down, a bead of dark water ran from the rotten edge. Raya caught it with the point of her knife and pressed the blade into the grain. The wood gave way farther than either of us had expected.

"That much must come off," she said.

"Will the rest reach the bank?"

"Not if we keep cutting."

I looked at the gap where the upper step had been. The old stones below it were already taking water. A boat was moving beyond the bend, its bow high enough to show that it carried something under the covered middle. Two men stood at the stern. A third was kneeling over a line.

"They could use the stones while they show," I said.

"Have you asked their owner?"

"No."

"Then leave them to their river."

She put the knife away and turned the timber on its supports. There was a pale strip beneath the wet face. She tested it with her thumb.

"This side may hold a foot if we cut away the dark end. It will not hold a loaded basket at the place where the bank has gone soft."

"Could we move it higher?"

"Where will you set the far end?"

I pointed at a patch of firm ground above the mud.

"That is Dala's path," she said.

"I know."

"Then ask Dala before you point again."

The boat did not turn toward us. It followed the current past the mouth of our landing and took the channel nearer the far trees. A second vessel followed, smaller and lower in the water. A boy on the bank called to them, but the man at the stern only raised a hand.

I had seen boats pass before. I had not watched one go by with the knowledge that a man had told its owner to avoid us.

"Do you know where they are going?" I asked.

"To a place where the owner thinks he can put his boat," Raya said. "I am looking at wood. If you want to know about boats, go and ask the people on them."

She lifted the timber again, this time without my help. I went up the path to find someone who had been close enough to hear the boy call.

***

At the market a carrier was folding a wet mat over a stack of empty baskets. His right shoulder bore a narrow red mark where a carrying pole had rested. I had seen him once at the lower landing, waiting beside a pot boat. He recognized me before I could ask his name.

"Your step is still shut," he said.

"Raya is measuring what she can save."

"Then it is shut."

"Did you see the two boats pass?"

"I put the first one's baskets on board. I would have been in the second if its owner had needed another hand."

"What did they carry?"

"Three covered baskets of dried fish, a jar of oil and a bundle of woven mats. The second carried empty baskets for the return. That is what I saw loaded. There may have been something under the other covers."

"Why did they go by the far channel?"

"The first owner hired a place to land before he left. The man who keeps that place agreed to hold a dry patch until the water turned."

"How much did he pay?"

"I did not ask the owner what he paid. I asked what he would pay me to carry the baskets from the market. He named my share. I took it."

"Would he have paid less to land here?"

"Perhaps. Would he have landed here?"

"Not today."

"Then the price of this landing today is a morning spent waiting for a place that cannot take the baskets."

He pulled the mat tighter and tied it around the empty stack.

"What is your name?" I asked.

"Marun."

"Do you carry for the other landing now?"

"I carry for whoever pays me and has a load I can lift. I am not a man who belongs to a landing."

"Could you tell me who made the agreement for the first boat?"

"Ask the boat owner."

"Where is he?"

"On the river. He has already paid me. If you want me to follow him and ask, that is another day's work."

I had come expecting to find a man who had been persuaded to turn against us. Marun had carried the baskets to the boat because the owner paid him. The boat went where its owner had arranged a place to land. No one had asked Marun to choose a side.

"Will the owner come here when the step is repaired?" I asked.

"He might, if someone tells him it is repaired and if he needs this bank. He has a buyer on the other side today. The farther landing may suit him better even when your wood is sound."

"But this way is shorter."

"Shorter for whom?"

"For a boat coming from the bend."

"For the boat, perhaps. The buyer is farther up the other path. The carrier there can walk his baskets to the buyer without climbing your bank and turning back."

I had measured the river by the place where I stood. Marun had measured it by the buyer's door.

"Does that other landing take every boat?" I asked.

"No. I have seen an owner sent away because the dry ground was already full. I have seen a boat wait half a morning because the man who promised the place was still unloading his own goods."

"Then it is no better than ours."

Marun looked at me until I heard what I had said.

"It is better for the owner who was told before he left that he had a place," he answered. "If the other landing is full, the man there says so. The owner can choose another way before he hires me."

He lifted the empty baskets onto his shoulder. The mat slipped at one corner. I reached to tuck it in, but he shifted away.

"I have tied it," he said. "If it comes loose, I will tie it again."

"Where are you taking them?"

"Back to the woman who owns them. She will want them dry before she lends them out."

I watched him go. He was not carrying a grievance against me. He had work to finish, and the place where I stood had nothing to offer him that morning.

***

At the hall, the keeper was sitting with a man who had brought a small clay weight. It was the same weight I had seen at the landing the day before. The man held it in his palm while he spoke.

"The cord owner told me the other landing can hold three boats at low water," he said. "I went there and saw two. I do not know whether a third could fit. I want to know who will tell me before I hire one."

The keeper had not seen the other landing.

"I can tell you what I heard about ours," she said. "Raya has not opened the step. Dala has not agreed to let cargo wait on her path. No one has paid a watcher to hold a place for your boat."

"Then there is nothing to mark."

"There is nothing to promise."

The man turned the weight in his hand. It was dark at the base where it had rested on wet earth.

"The cord owner says he will bring the other traders here tomorrow," he said. "He wants to hear what can be done before the next market."

"Who are the traders?" I asked.

"One owns pots. One hires carriers for a woman who sells dried fish. I own this weight and a share in the jars it measures. We do not own one another's goods."

"Why come together?"

"Because we keep asking the same question in different places. Each of us has lost time finding out the answer after the boat is paid."

"Do you want me to set a price for waiting?"

"I want to know who is willing to wait. If someone asks for pay, we can decide whether to pay. If you name a price for a man who has not agreed to work, we will have the same quarrel again."

The keeper set the blank strip beside her knife but did not cut it.

"Then come when the people whose work you need can speak," she said. "I will hear what each says. I cannot make them come."

The man put the weight in a fold of his cloth.

"That is why the cord owner asked us to come together. If the people are not here, we can decide together whether to keep bringing boats to this bank."

He stood and went out. I had thought the cord owner was gathering men to blame me. He was gathering men who wanted an answer before they paid for work. I could see how easily they might agree to leave us, even if they disagreed about everything else.

The keeper looked toward the path.

"Did you ask Raya what the repair needs?" she said.

"She says the timber is rotten at the end."

"Did you ask whose wood can replace it?"

"Not yet."

"Then tomorrow you will hear men asking for a place you have not made."

"I can tell them the truth."

"You can. Will you be willing to hear what that costs them?"

I had heard Marun say a shorter river was not always a shorter journey. I had heard the man with the clay weight say three traders could ask the same question without owning the same goods. I had not yet asked Dala whether the step could be moved, or Tesa whether the carrying work she had offered would still be there tomorrow. Amaya was still waiting for her cloth.

Outside, the smaller of the two boats came back past the bend. It was riding higher, its empty baskets stacked beneath a cloth. It did not turn toward our landing. The man at the stern kept his eyes on the water ahead. He had made the journey he was paid to make, and our closed step had not been part of it.

***

The cord owner arrived with the traders before the sun had dried the mats outside the hall. He carried his measuring cord over one shoulder. The man with the clay weight came behind him, holding a covered jar with both hands, and a woman I knew from the fish stalls followed with an empty basket under her arm. A fourth person waited at the door: a boat worker with a short pole worn smooth at the place where her hands met it.

The keeper was sorting two strips of bamboo. She set them aside when she saw the visitors.

"We came together as we said," the cord owner told her.

"Who has come?" she asked.

He frowned at the question. "You can see us."

"I can see four people. I do not know whose goods each one speaks for."

The man put his jar on the floor. "I own a share of what is in this jar, and the weight that measures it. My brother owns the rest. I told him I was coming to hear what the landing can offer. I have not promised him a boat."

The woman laid down her basket. "I buy dried fish from three households and sell it at the upper market. I own what I have paid for. The rest is theirs until I pay. My name is Sela."

The cord owner nodded toward the boat worker. "She knows what waiting costs."

"I know what my waiting costs," the worker said. "My name is Hiran. I was hired to pole a boat this morning. The owner let me come here while he has his stern rope repaired. He has not sent me to agree to anything for him."

"Then you speak for yourself," the keeper said.

"That is what I said."

The cord owner unwound a little of his line and laid it across the threshold. A wet knot darkened the packed earth. He pulled the cord straight, as though the distance between us could be measured and settled.

"Here is what we need," he said. "A place where a boat can land, a way to bring goods up, and someone to tell us whether the place is free before we pay the men who carry them."

"You know the step is shut," I said.

"I know it today. Yesterday a man came from beyond the bend because he was told there had been a crossing. He did not know the step was shut until he saw it. He has a choice of landings. Next time he will choose before he leaves."

Sela touched the rim of her basket. "I can lose fish by waiting. The salt keeps it, but it does not keep a buyer in the market. A woman who buys from me walks away if I arrive after she has bought from another boat."

"Then hire a watcher," the cord owner said. "I have been saying it since the first quarrel."

"Where will the watcher stand?" the keeper asked.

"Here, where the water can be seen."

"On whose ground?"

He looked at the opening. Beyond it, Dala's net was spread where the path widened, the weights set apart to dry. He had seen the net. He had also seen Dala send a carrier back down the bank when the man's basket caught one of its lines.

"On ground agreed with its owner," he said at last.

"And who will ask?"

"I will, if there is a reason to pay a watcher."

Hiran set her pole against the doorpost. "Will the watcher tell the truth if his pay comes from the man who wants a boat to come?"

"He will lose his pay if he lies."

"He may keep his pay until the boat arrives. I have been promised a dry landing by men who had never walked down to it. When the boat came, the water was over the stones. They said I could pole farther up. I had been hired to bring it here."

The cord owner looked at her. "Did you take the boat farther?"

"I asked the owner what he would pay. He said the journey was already paid for. I stayed with the boat. We waited for the tide and unloaded when the stones showed. He lost his buyer. I lost the work I might have taken that afternoon."

Sela gave a short laugh without pleasure. "That is what I am trying to avoid."

The keeper moved the bamboo strips farther from the damp cord.

"Raya has not opened the step," she said. "Dala has not given a place to wait. I can tell someone those things. If I tell him before he leaves, will that help?"

"It will help him go somewhere else," the cord owner said.

"It may help him keep his wages," Hiran answered.

The man with the clay weight lifted the jar and shook it gently. Something inside moved with a dull sound. "I would pay to know the truth before I send my goods. But I cannot pay a different man at every bend to repeat it. If we all use this landing, perhaps each boat should give one measure toward the watcher."

"Each boat?" Sela asked. "Even when the watcher is here for your jars?"

"For every boat that comes."

"Then I will use the other channel. My buyers are already nearer that path."

The cord owner raised a hand. "You have not heard what he means."

"I heard a measure from every boat. I do not own his jar. He does not own my fish. Why should my basket pay for his man?"

"Because you would also be told when the landing was ready."

"Then ask me to buy that news. I may refuse. I do not owe him a share of my cargo because he has found a convenient way to count boats."

The man with the weight lowered his eyes to the jar. "I did not say a share of cargo. I said one measure."

"Of what?" Hiran asked. "Oil? Grain? The weight of a pot? You have not said who receives it or who stands in the rain. You have named a payment before finding the person who agrees to work."

The man held the jar closer to himself. "I was offering a way to divide the cost."

"Then find the cost first," she said.

I saw the keeper's knife beside the blank strip and felt how easily the argument could be made to look finished. A mark for each boat. Another for the watcher. I could carry the strip down to the landing and show it to anyone who complained. The people standing before me might even agree to its shape if I gave them time to forget the questions they had asked.

"I could keep the names of those willing to pay," I said.

The keeper turned toward me. "For what work?"

"For watching."

"Who has agreed to watch?"

"No one yet."

"Then what would their names mean?"

I had no answer that would survive Hiran's next question.

The cord owner rolled up the loose end of his line. "We have come to ask whether anything can be arranged. If the answer is that nothing can be said until every person has agreed, then we have lost another morning."

"You chose this morning," Sela said. "I came because you told me the people who use the bank would be here. Raya is not here. Dala is not here. You have brought us to a place with no step and no one who can promise the path."

"Raya is working on the timber," I said.

"Then we should ask her what the work will take."

I started toward the door. Hiran caught her pole before I could pass.

"Do not bring her here to answer for a step while her hands are holding it," she said. "We can go to her. If the wood falls because we called her away, none of us will pay for the foot beneath it."

The keeper rose. "I will come as far as the path. I have heard what each of you says. I want to see what the wood can bear before I put anything beside those words."

***

We went down together, though the cord owner kept several paces ahead. He walked quickly until he reached the bend where the net began. There he slowed. Dala was crouching beside the spread mesh, teasing a knot loose with a bone point. She watched the cord in his hand.

"Keep that off the net," she said.

He gathered it against his chest.

"We have come to see the step," I told her.

"You can see it from there."

"Some of them want to know where goods could wait when it is repaired."

Dala pulled a torn loop toward her. "Not here."

Sela looked at the narrow firm strip between the net and the bank. "Could a person stand here with one basket?"

"A person can stand where I say there is room. A basket left while its owner goes away is another thing. I need this ground when I lift the net. The weights cannot be moved every time a boat arrives."

"What if we paid you?" the man with the jar asked.

"For what?"

"For the use of the ground."

Dala set down the bone point. "If I take your measure today, will you tell the next boat that this place belongs to you? I have heard men say a place is theirs because they paid to stand there once."

"I would pay for one morning."

"Then ask me about one morning when you know which morning it is. Do not sell tomorrow's path with today's jar."

Sela looked from the net to the mud below. "I could wait with the basket in my hands."

"You could," Dala said. "You might find I need to bring the net up while you wait. Then you would have to move."

"That is worth knowing."

"It is what I have been telling people."

Farther down, Raya had braced the timber on two stones. The cut she had marked was deeper now. A wedge of rotten wood lay at her feet. She had drawn a fresh line along the sound part and was testing the end with a small weight.

"Do not step there," she said before we reached her. "The lower stone has turned."

We stopped. Hiran moved first, planting her pole against firmer ground and leaning on it to test the mud. She kept her weight back.

"Could a boat put a bow against the stones when the water is low?" she asked.

"A light boat might touch them," Raya said. "If its owner wants a broken bow, I will not help him. The stones are not fastened for that. The sounding stake is not a mooring post."

Hiran nodded. "I would not take a loaded boat there."

The cord owner bent to examine the timber. "How long until you mend it?"

"With what wood?"

"The rest of this piece looks sound."

"It is too short when I cut the bad end away. I can make a step for one foot if I put it higher. Then the far end lies on Dala's path, and she has not given it."

Dala, still behind us, called, "I heard that."

Raya looked at the cord owner. "I need a longer sound piece, and I need someone to help lift it after I have cut the seat. I will not name a day until I have both."

"Could we bring wood from the other bank?" the man with the jar asked.

"If its owner sells or lends it, if a boat agrees to carry it, and if I see that it is sound. Do you have such wood?"

"No."

"Then you have a journey in your mouth," Raya said. "I have a piece of rotten timber in my hands."

The cord owner straightened. His jaw tightened, but he did not argue with the grain Raya showed him.

Sela stepped nearer to me. "I will not bring fish down this path while it is like this. I may come back if Raya says the step is safe and Dala agrees where I can stand. I want the news before I hire carriers."

"Who will carry it to you?" I asked.

"Someone I trust, or someone I pay. I am not asking you to command them."

Hiran lifted her pole. "And if a boat owner asks me to come, I will ask what he pays for waiting and where I can refuse to land. I do not promise him the use of a path I have never walked."

The man with the jar looked at the two of them. "Then there is no single measure we can agree on."

"There may be a price for each thing," Sela said. "You wanted one price before you knew what you were buying."

He took his weight out of his cloth and turned it once in his palm. "I still want a way to know the place will be ready."

"So do I," she said. "I am willing to hear how. I am not willing to pay for a promise that belongs to someone absent."

The cord owner had been silent. Now he looked at me.

"You have heard them. If I send a boat here next market, what can you promise?"

I looked at the timber on the stones, at Dala's net, at the clay weight, and at Hiran's pole planted in the mud. I could promise to carry a message if someone paid me or accepted my time. I could promise to ask Raya when she expected to finish. Neither promise would make the bank ready.

"I can tell you what Raya says today," I answered. "The step is shut. There is no agreed waiting place. I can ask her again when she has sound wood."

"And if I bring a boat anyway?"

"You will have chosen knowing that."

He drew the cord across his palm until the knot reached his thumb. "Then I will tell the owners what you said. I will also tell them the other landing has offered space for the next market. They will decide where to go."

He had come to hear a promise. He was leaving with the truth, and with a better offer from someone else. I wanted to follow him and say there would be wood by morning. I had not asked its owner. Raya was still cutting rot from a beam too short for the place it needed to reach.

Hiran took her pole out of the mud. "My boat owner will want me back," she said. "I have given you the morning he allowed. If you need me another day, ask him whether he can spare me, and ask me whether I will come."

She went ahead of the others. Sela followed with her empty basket. The man with the jar stopped once to look at the old stones, then took the path behind them. The cord owner was last. He carried his cord coiled, no part of it touching the ground.

Dala returned to her net. Raya gave me the wedge of rotten wood.

"Take that away from where I stand," she said.

I carried it up the bank. It was light in my hand. The timber we needed would not be.

***

By the time the traders had left, the water had climbed over the lowest stone. Raya kept working above it. She had laid the shortened timber across two supports and was shaving the rotten edge back to sound grain. Each cut fell beside the wedge she had made me carry away.

"How long must the new piece be?" I asked.

She gave me the end of a measuring cord and pointed to a firm patch beyond the hollow. I held it where she said. She pulled the cord across the gap, then told me to move my hand back.

"That ground belongs to Dala's path," she said. "I am measuring where a step could rest without taking it."

I moved my hand. The cord no longer reached.

"Then we need a longer piece."

"We needed one before you brought the traders."

"Would Madan have it?"

"He may know where to find it. Ask him. Bring him here to see the ground if he wishes. I will not cut a piece I have not inspected."

The cord caught against a root. I bent to free it and felt the mud give beneath my heel. Raya caught the other end before I pulled her off balance.

"Leave it," she said. "That is why I do not want a man promising a step from the hall."

She pulled the cord free with her knife and wound it around her wrist. I wanted to tell her I would return with wood before the next market. I had already heard her answer to promises made without timber.

At the top of the path, Dala was gathering the dry weights from her net. A boy helped her lift the corners, keeping the hooks from catching. She stopped when I approached.

"The man with the jar asked about this ground," she said.

"I heard you refuse."

"I did not refuse every man who might need to stand here. I refused a place he could tell other people was his. If I have to move my net because a boat is waiting, I lose the time when I can mend it."

"Would you consider a place farther up?"

"Show me where."

I pointed toward the patch Raya had measured. Dala walked to it and put one bare foot beside the root. She looked at the water below, then at the net in the boy's hands.

"When the tide is high, I bring the weights up this way," she said. "If a loaded basket is standing here, which of us moves?"

"I would ask the carrier."

"Would you be here to ask?"

I looked toward the market, where the cord owner had already gone.

"No."

"Then do not make an agreement that needs you to stand beside it every hour."

The boy shifted the net. One weight slipped from his hand and struck a stone. Dala took it from him and inspected the line. She said nothing until she had seen the knot was sound.

"If a person asks me on a day when the net is not here, I may say yes," she said. "I do not know which day that will be. I cannot give the path to a watcher who has not yet been hired."

"I will tell them."

"Tell them I answered you. Do not tell them I have answered their next question."

She went on gathering the weights. The boy followed with the folded mesh against his chest. I left without asking him to carry the timber for us.

***

Madan was not at his wood yard. His niece was there with a coil of cord over one arm and a narrow board laid across two baskets. I remembered her holding the three knots that marked his promises when I had first asked him for timber.

"Is Madan coming back?" I asked.

"When the men finish bringing the last lengths down. What do you want?"

"A piece long enough for Raya's step."

"How long?"

I told her what Raya had measured. She laid the cord along the board and marked the distance with her thumb. The board was too short. She did not pretend otherwise.

"Do you want the wood my mother is waiting for?" she asked.

"No. Her boat needs its crosspiece."

"Then you remember that much."

"I remember the beam for Amaya's roof too."

"That beam is already hers. You came once to ask whether it could be moved. Madan told you what it had cost her. Has she offered it to you?"

"No."

She put the board back on the baskets. "Then I will not bring it to the landing."

"Are there unpromised pieces?"

"There are short pieces and one long one that Madan has not cut. He was going to look at it for the house above the bend. They need a roof support before the heavy rain. He has not agreed to sell it to them, but he told them he would come and look."

"Could he show it to Raya first?"

"Before the people who asked first?"

"I could help bring it down."

"Who pays the men who cut it? You may carry one end. There will be more than one end to bring through the trees."

I looked at the baskets. One had a split rim bound with fresh fiber. A tool lay beneath the board, its handle dark from use.

"Could I work for the cutting?"

"Perhaps. Madan will decide what work he needs. But he cannot pay you with wood that another house has already bought. Nor can he sell a piece before he knows whether it is sound."

She looked past me toward the market. I turned. Madan was coming up the path with two men carrying a length of timber between them. They lowered it onto the ground beside the baskets. The men sat down without waiting for an order.

Madan examined the end of the piece and asked his niece what I wanted. She told him, including the length I had given her.

"The step again," he said.

"Raya says the old piece cannot reach sound ground."

"Then she knows her work. Have you brought anything to buy another?"

"I can carry it. I can help cut it."

"You have hands. My men have hands. What will they eat while you work beside them?"

"I could bring grain after the next market."

"Whose grain?"

I did not answer.

One of the men on the ground rubbed his shoulder. "We carried this one from above the bend. The house that ordered it gave us food before we lifted it. If another man wants us to carry for him, he must feed us too."

Madan looked at him. "You may go when you have rested. You have finished what I paid you for."

The man nodded and reached for a water vessel. The other remained seated.

"Come with Raya to see the uncut piece," Madan said to me. "If she thinks it will serve, I will name a price. I will not move the house's promised work until I have asked them. If they say they cannot wait, you must find another tree or another day."

"When can we go?"

"After the men have rested. I have a delivery first."

"I could go with them."

"Then ask them whether they want another man on their path. Do not tell them I have hired you."

The niece took the coil from her arm and wound it tighter. "And do not use the cord for a promise," she said. "I have not measured the tree."

I thanked them and stepped away. I had learned the shape of the missing wood. I had bought none of it.

***

Amaya was waiting at the market path. The old cloth she had left with me was still tied beneath my arm. Its patched corner rubbed against my ribs when I stopped.

"Tesa's bundles came this morning," she said.

I stopped.

"I was at the landing."

"I know where you were. Tesa sent for a carrier before the boat arrived. The boy who carried the first load told me he saw you go down with the traders."

"Did she hire him?"

"She hired two people. They carried the bundles and spread the cloth. She paid them for the work they did."

I looked at the fold under Amaya's arm. The measure was still there. The cloth we had chosen had already been sold. Now I had lost the work that might have helped buy another.

"I told her I would come," I said.

"She heard you."

"Can I go now and ask for the next load?"

"You can ask. She may have work. She may not. I am not going to ask her to hold it for you."

I started to tell her about the step and the merchants, about the wood Madan might let us inspect. Amaya saw the words forming.

"Did the step open?" she asked.

"No."

"Did you bring wood?"

"No."

"Did you earn anything this morning?"

"No."

"Then I do not know why you are standing here explaining the landing to me."

She rubbed one palm with the other. I felt the folded measure beneath my arm.

"I have work at home," she said. "The cloth I lent you is still missing from that work. When I need to wrap the jars, I use a smaller piece and make two journeys. When rain comes, I have to bring them in sooner. You may be able to ask a boat to wait while you mend its step. I cannot ask the weather to wait while you find another way to pay."

"I could carry your jars now."

"Where would you carry them? They are at my house. I need cloth to cover them, not another man to move them without asking."

I reached under my arm for the measure, then stopped. It belonged to her. She had placed it in my hands so I could show Tesa what I owed. I had no right to sell it, and keeping it tied against my body had brought her no nearer to a replacement.

"May I take it to Tesa again?" I asked.

"Go with me now. Ask her whether there is paid work. If she has none, you will hear it from her. If she has some, tell me what she offers before you call it payment for my cloth."

We walked to the weaver's awning. Tesa was lifting a damp length from a mat. The young woman beside her was winding thread again. Two bundles stood near the doorway, tied and ready to be taken uphill.

"I have finished with the boat," Tesa said when she saw me.

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

"To ask if you have another load I can carry."

"These two are for a woman above the market. She has already paid the carrying price. I was going to send the young woman, but she has thread to finish. You may take one if she agrees to give you the work."

The young woman looked at the bundles. "The nearer house has a steep path. He must carry it without dragging the cloth."

"I can," I said.

"Will you bring the woman's answer back? She wants the ends measured again before she cuts them."

"I will."

Tesa looked at Amaya. "That work will earn him a little rice. It will not buy the cloth you showed me. I have no length of that measure ready."

"I know," Amaya said. "I want him to know it too."

The young woman lifted one bundle and put it in my arms. It was lighter than Madan's timber, but its binding cut into my wrist when I took the weight badly. She showed me how to carry it higher so the lower edge would not drag.

"Do not stop at the landing," she said. "The house is above the market. If you put it down in the mud, the woman will send it back."

I set off with the bundle against my chest. At the turning I could see the river through the trees. Raya would still be at the step, measuring the place the new wood had to reach. Madan had offered to show her an uncut piece after his delivery. I could not be there for both journeys. I had agreed to this one.

The cloth rubbed against my forearm. I shifted it where the young woman had shown me and climbed toward the house that had paid for its arrival.

***

The woman who had bought Tesa's cloth did not take it from me at the door.

She made me carry it around the house to a shaded place where three lengths were stretched on a line. One had been rinsed and was still dripping into a shallow trough. She looked at the bundle in my arms, then at the wet ground between us.

"Did you set it down?"

"No."

"Show me the bottom."

I turned the bundle as the young woman at Tesa's had taught me. The outer binding was damp where my wrist had sweated against it, but the cloth inside had not touched the path. The buyer loosened one knot, felt the folded edge, and tied it again.

"Good. Put it on the mat."

I put it where she pointed. She asked me to hold the far end while she measured it against a cord she kept rolled around a peg. We found the length she had paid for. Then she made me wait while she decided whether the edge would fall straight after drying. I could hear water dropping from the line beside us. At the landing I had spent mornings asking other people to wait for things I could not yet provide. Here the waiting belonged to the woman who had bought the cloth.

When she was satisfied, she gave me a short piece of thread with two knots.

"Tell Tesa the measure is right. Tell her I want the next length without the pale patch. If she cannot make it, she can say so before I send the rice."

"Will you tell her yourself?"

"I have just told you. If you cannot remember, give me back the thread and I will find someone else."

I repeated the message until she nodded. On the way down I kept my hand closed around the knots. They did not prove what she had said. She had trusted me to bring the words back with them.

Tesa listened without interrupting. She took the thread and asked which end the buyer had measured first. I told her. She turned to the young woman winding thread beneath the awning.

"Give him his rice."

The young woman measured a small portion into a leaf. I had expected more. I remembered what Tesa had said when she offered the work, and I did not ask her to change it.

"May I bring this to Amaya?" I asked.

"It is yours for the carrying," Tesa said. "If Amaya takes rice toward what you owe, that is for her to decide. It does not make this the price of her cloth."

I thanked her and took the leaf. I had kept one promise after failing another. The rice was warm where my hand closed around it. I was already thinking how to tell Amaya that it was a beginning when I saw Salin standing outside the hall.

He was holding a basket with a cover tied across its mouth. A strip of pale cord hung from one handle. I knew him from Naru's household in Tondo, where I had once been made to explain a claim I thought had already been settled. He saw me and moved aside to let a woman pass with two water jars.

"I have been waiting for the keeper," he said.

"She is inside."

"I have spoken to her. I was waiting to see whether you would come."

"Has Naru sent you?"

"He sent me to ask about a load he may buy. He did not send me to settle the landing."

I looked at the basket.

"Is that the load?"

"An empty basket. I brought it so the seller could show me how much would fit. Naru wants oil, if the owner can deliver it without losing another morning. The seller says the trouble is yours."

"What trouble?"

Salin did not answer at once. He watched me shift the rice from one hand to the other.

"Are you working for Tesa now?"

"She paid me to carry a bundle."

"Then I will wait until you have put your pay away."

"I can hear you."

"You are holding rice that someone gave you for work. If I make you drop it while I tell you what I heard, I will have made the morning worse for no reason."

I tucked the leaf into the fold of my cloth. Salin looked toward the hall.

"The oil seller says there is a mark here for every boat that passes. He says a man with cord has offered to collect a measure and pay watchers from it. He says the keeper agreed to cut the marks."

"She did not."

"Which part?"

"She has not agreed to a charge. The cord owner proposed watchers. Another trader proposed one measure from each boat. Sela refused. Hiran asked who would be paid and what work the payment bought. No one agreed."

"Then why does the oil seller say there is a mark?"

"There are marks for a crossing and for a correction about a path on the other bank. They are not for every boat."

"Have you seen what he was shown?"

"No."

"Then neither of us knows whether he saw a strip, or a man's hand moving in the air while he spoke of one."

I disliked how carefully Salin kept the two things apart. I wanted him to carry my answer back and make the seller ashamed. Instead he had come to ask what could be shown.

The keeper called us inside. She was sitting with the clay-weight trader from the morning meeting. His jar stood beside his foot. The cord owner was not there. Two bamboo strips lay on the table, their cuts turned away from the door. The keeper had placed a third, unmarked piece beside them.

"I have told Salin what I heard," she said. "He asked whether you were present."

"I was."

The trader with the weight looked up. "He has already asked me. I told him I offered a measure toward a watcher. Sela said she would not pay it. I did not say the keeper had agreed."

"Did you tell the oil seller?" Salin asked.

"I told my brother what I offered. I do not know whom he told."

"Did you show anyone a marked strip?"

"No. I had my jar. I saw the keeper's knife on the table. I did not touch it."

The keeper put a finger beside the unmarked bamboo.

"These are the things I can remember. The cord owner said he might ask Dala for a place to stand. He has not brought me Dala's answer. The trader proposed a measure. Sela refused to share a charge for someone else's jars. Hiran did not accept work as a watcher. Raya said the step is unsafe. I cut no mark for a charge."

Salin asked to see the two older strips. The keeper lifted them one at a time and told him who had spoken when each cut was made. She did not put either into his hand.

"Can I take a copy of the words to Naru?" he asked.

"I can tell you the words. I cannot cut a strip that says Naru heard them. He has not been here."

"I would tell him I heard them from you."

"Then he will know whom to ask if he doubts you."

The clay-weight trader made a sound in his throat.

"That may satisfy a household," he said. "It does not tell a boat owner where to put his bow."

"No," the keeper said.

"I came here to find a way to send my jars without paying men to stand in water. I have lost a morning, and now a man in Tondo thinks I have bought a charge I never collected."

"Would you take back your proposal?" I asked.

"Why should I? I offered to pay a watcher if others wished to share the cost. I still would. If no one shares it, I may hire one for my own goods. That is my choice."

Salin looked at the weight in the man's hand.

"Does your brother know you have made no agreement?"

"I told him what I offered. He may have heard only that I offered."

"Then perhaps you should tell him the rest."

"I will speak to my brother. I will not walk to every buyer who has heard a story. I have jars to move."

He lifted the jar, stood, and went out. He had corrected his part without giving up the arrangement he still wanted. I could not make him dislike the proposed charge merely because I did.

***

Salin stayed after the trader left. He asked the keeper to repeat the words about the two strips, then repeated them back with one mistake. He called Bina's report a second crossing. The keeper stopped him.

"She brought a correction from Nali. She did not carry a load across for us. She spoke of a journey that had already ended."

Salin began again. This time he got the difference right.

"I can tell Naru that," he said. "I cannot tell him the landing is open."

"It is not," I said.

"And I cannot tell him who put the false charge into the oil seller's mouth."

"No."

"Then the seller may send his jars by the other channel."

"He may."

Salin took the empty basket from the doorway and tested its handle. One of the fibers had come loose. He tucked it beneath the binding.

"Naru asked me to find out whether he could buy the oil before the next market. The seller wants payment before he hires a boat. If I tell Naru there is no charge here, he may still choose the other landing because it has a dry place to unload. Will you tell people he has joined against you?"

"I did not say he had."

"You were looking at me as though I had come to carry the cord owner's words."

I looked down at the rice hidden in my cloth. Salin had waited while I put it away. He had listened to a trader he did not trust enough to repeat without checking. I had heard his first question and thought of Tondo as a single mouth speaking against us.

"What will you tell Naru about the step?" I asked.

"That Raya says it is unsafe. I did not hear her say it myself. I will tell him the keeper told me."

"Would you come to hear Raya?"

"If Naru wants to pay for another morning. I have already spent this one. He sent me to ask about oil, not to inspect timber."

"I could go to him."

"Then what would you bring? A repaired step?"

"No."

"A boat owner who has agreed to wait?"

"No."

"Then bring him the truth you have, if you wish. He may still buy the oil elsewhere."

He shifted the basket to his other hand. I thought of asking him to stay until the cord owner came back. Salin had no reason to give up the rest of his day so I could hear another man's denial.

"Did the oil seller tell you who showed him the mark?" I asked.

"He said a man with cord. He did not give me a name. I have seen your cord owner in the market. That does not prove it was he."

"Will you ask the seller?"

"I will tell Naru what I found. If Naru still wants the oil, he may ask me to return. I will not promise the seller another visit on your behalf."

He went toward the upper path. The basket bumped against his hip. It was empty, and still he had to carry it home.

When I turned back, the keeper had set the disputed report beside the unmarked strip.

"Should we cut a mark that says there is no charge?" I asked.

"For whom?"

"For anyone who comes asking."

"If I cut it, what will a man say when he sees it?"

"That we refused the charge."

"Or that a charge was offered and I had power to refuse it for every owner. I heard a proposal. I heard objections. I can remember those things. I cannot refuse work on behalf of a man who has not been hired."

"Then the story will go on."

"It may. Bring me someone who heard the cord owner tell a buyer that I had agreed. I will hear that person. Until then I will keep Salin's report as his report."

She took a fresh strip and made one small cut while I watched. She said aloud what it stood for: Salin had heard an oil seller claim there was a marked charge. Salin had not seen the mark. The keeper had heard no agreement to collect one. She set the new strip apart from the crossing records.

I wanted her to destroy the story by cutting it down to what was true. She had made room for it to remain disputed. A buyer might come tomorrow and say the same thing. The cord owner might come and deny it. Each would still have to answer for the words he had spoken.

Outside, a boat passed toward the farther channel with a covered jar tied near its stern. I could not tell whose oil it carried. I knew only that it did not stop. The leaf of rice under my cloth had begun to tear at one corner. I took it out carefully, folded the edge over the grain, and went to find Amaya before I could spend another afternoon explaining a landing that was still closed.

***

I found Amaya beside her jars. She had spread the old cloth measure on a dry mat and was comparing it with the smaller covering she used when the weather changed. The patched square lay between her hands.

I held out the rice Tesa had paid me.

"She gave me this for carrying one bundle. I took it to the buyer, waited while she measured it, and brought her answer back."

Amaya looked at the leaf.

"Did Tesa say it was toward my cloth?"

"She said it was mine for the work. She said you must decide whether to take it toward what I owe."

"Then I will take it."

She untied the leaf, poured the grain into a small bowl, and set the empty wrapping beside the jars. She did not call the debt smaller. I had brought her something she could use that evening. It did not cover the jars she still had to protect.

"I will tell Tesa you took it," I said.

"Tell her if you see her. Do not spend another morning carrying that news when you have work to do."

I could hear the river beyond the houses. Someone was calling for a pole. Amaya folded the old measure.

"Are you going down again?"

"Only to see whether Raya has found Madan."

"Then go. If you promise her your hands, keep them there."

I went by the market path. The calls grew louder as I descended. At the landing a boat lay a short distance from the stones, held against the current by a pole at its stern. Its bow rose and fell where the water met the lower bank. Three baskets sat on the firm ground above the mud, each covered with damp leaves. Two carriers had brought them from the lower fish stalls and gone back for their next loads. Sela stood beside them, arguing with a man in a short wet garment. Hiran held the pole in the boat. I recognized her from the meeting at the hall.

"The buyer is waiting," Sela said.

"The buyer is on land," the man answered. "My boat is in the water. I told you I would bring it here if there was a place to put the baskets."

"I was told the crossing had been marked."

"By whom?"

Sela turned when she saw me. She did not look pleased.

"Ask him," she said to the man. "He was at the meeting."

The man looked from me to the blocked upper step. He had a narrow scar above one eyebrow and a piece of cord tied around his wrist.

"Is that your landing?"

"I live on this bank. The step belongs to Raya's work, and she has closed it."

"Then it is closed."

"Yes."

"Why did the cord owner tell me there was a mark?"

"There are marks for one crossing and for a correction about it. There is no mark opening the step."

Sela lifted the covering on one basket. Inside were fish packed with leaves, still cool from the shade where she had bought them. These had not been dried. The buyer at the upper market had agreed to take them while they were fresh. She had paid for two baskets; the third belonged to a woman who had asked her to carry it on commission.

"The owner of that basket is waiting for her price," Sela said. "If the fish turn, she will lose more than I do."

The man in the wet garment looked at the baskets, then at the water.

"My name is Laman," he said. "Sela hired my boat to take them upriver to the market landing. I said I would load them here if there was a safe place for the boat and the baskets. I have brought the boat. There is no safe place to load. I will not put my bow against those stones."

"Could you load them farther up?" I asked.

"Where?"

I pointed to a narrow strip beyond the bend. He looked at it for a long moment.

"I have not sounded it. I will not bring my boat into mud I do not know because you can see a patch of ground from here."

Hiran shifted her pole. The boat moved an arm's length downstream before she steadied it.

"I was hired to pole this boat to the agreed loading place," she said. "I have done that. If Laman wants another loading place, he must ask whether I will work the farther journey. He cannot promise my hands because he owns the hull."

"I have not promised them," Laman said.

"You were about to ask him where to take us."

"I asked where there was ground."

"Then ask me before you decide what my arms will do."

Sela looked toward the upper path. A boy was coming down with an empty tray, sent by the buyer to see whether the fish had arrived. He stopped when he saw the boat still in the water.

"She says she will wait until the sun is above the palm," he told Sela. "Then she will buy from the next woman."

Sela covered the basket again.

"Can the fish go by another loading place?" she asked Laman.

"They can, if you pay carriers to take the baskets there first. The farther place is away from your buyer's house. I will need another payment for the time and water."

Hiran planted the pole in the mud and held it still.

"And you will need to ask me whether I take that journey. I have work after this tide."

"Will you?" Sela asked.

"I do not know the price yet."

"Then tell me."

Hiran named an amount of grain. Sela repeated it as if she had not heard correctly.

"That is nearly what I will earn on the two baskets."

"Then you may choose another way. I am telling you what it costs me to miss the work I have already been offered."

Laman did not lower his price. He was holding a boat in moving water, and the rope around his wrist was dark with spray. Sela had expected the upper step to take the baskets before the buyer's patience ended. She had hired two people to make a journey that could not finish where she wanted it to finish. None of them could get back the morning.

"Could we carry the baskets up the old stones?" I asked.

Raya answered from behind me.

"No."

She had come down carrying the short piece of timber across one shoulder. She lowered it on the firm bank and pointed to the lower stone.

"That stone turned when I put my weight on it. A man with an empty hand might step there and get away with it. A man carrying fish cannot see where his foot goes. I will not say it is safe."

"I was not asking you to say it was safe," I told her.

"You were asking whether you could do it. You asked where I could hear."

Sela pressed the leaf covering flat. She had stopped arguing with Laman. She was watching the boy who had brought the buyer's message.

"Could one basket reach her by the upper market path?" she asked.

"From here?" I said.

"From the place where we stand. On land. Without using the step."

"Yes, if someone carries it."

"How long?"

I knew the path from Tesa's awning. I had just walked it. I could carry one basket without putting it down, but the fish were heavier than the cloth and the path rose sharply beyond the jars.

"Longer than the boy's walk," I said. "I would have to stop at the turning to change my grip."

"Can you do it before the sun reaches the palm?"

"I think so."

"Thinking is not carrying."

I looked at the basket. I had made the same mistake with Madan's timber, offering hands without asking who would pay the other men. I could lift one basket. I could not carry three at once, or give the buyer more time than she had offered.

"I can carry one," I said. "If you want me to. I will not promise the other two."

Sela asked the boy which basket the buyer wanted most. He said the smallest fish, if they were still firm. Sela opened the second basket and showed him the catch. He touched one fish with a clean finger and nodded.

"Take that one," she said. "I will pay you from the sale if she accepts it. If she refuses it, I will give you a small measure for the carrying. I cannot pay the full price for a load that earns me nothing."

"I accept."

Laman looked at the basket.

"That is part of the cargo I agreed to carry," he said.

"Have you been paid for this part of the journey?" Sela asked.

"I have been paid to bring my boat here."

"Then your boat is here. I am choosing whether the fish go aboard."

He looked at Hiran. She was still holding the pole. Neither of them spoke for a moment.

"I will take my boat back to the lower bend," Laman said at last. "That return is within what I agreed with Sela. She must find carriers for the other baskets or another buyer."

Hiran nodded. "I will pole the return to the lower bend. I will not go to another loading place for the same pay."

Sela accepted the return. The third basket's owner would have to hear that her fish had not reached the upper market. Sela paid a waiting carrier to take the two remaining baskets back to the fish stalls and said she would tell their owners herself.

I lifted the chosen basket. Its weight settled against my shoulder, and the binding pulled across my palm. The boy went ahead to warn the buyer. I climbed the path with the leaves brushing my cheek. Twice I stopped where the ground was firm, keeping the basket above the mud while I changed hands.

When I reached the buyer's house, the sun had touched the lower leaves of the palm but had not passed its crown. The woman looked at the fish before she looked at me. She opened the basket, pressed the flesh near the gills, and held one to her nose.

"I will take this," she said. "Only this. I have already bought the rest."

She measured out Sela's price into a leaf and tied it carefully.

"Take this to Sela," the buyer said. "She will decide your wage. I am paying for fish."

I took the measure back without opening it. The rice inside belonged to Sela until she counted what she had earned and decided what to pay me.

***

The boat had gone when I returned. Sela was waiting beside the wet patch where the baskets had stood. Raya was farther down the bank, examining the lower stone again. The cord owner had arrived and was speaking with her. She had put the short timber between them as though it were the only answer she wished to give.

Sela counted the buyer's payment twice. She took out the carrying amount she had promised me and gave it to me in a fresh leaf.

"You did what you said," she told me. "One basket reached the buyer. Two did not. The woman who owned the third will ask why. I will tell her."

"Will you bring fish here again?"

"I may bring fish to this bank when I know where to put them. I will not hire Laman on the strength of another man's mark."

The cord owner heard her. He came toward us with his measuring line wound tightly against his arm.

"I told the owner the keeper had recorded a crossing," he said. "I did not tell him Raya had finished the step."

"You told him there was a mark," Sela answered. "He heard a place to land."

"Then he heard too much."

"Or you said too little."

He looked at me, perhaps expecting me to defend the distinction.

"The keeper will hear what each of you says," I told him. "She cannot give Sela back her buyer, or Hiran the work she has missed."

Hiran had already gone with Laman. I could not ask her what she had lost by the time the boat reached the lower bend. I could say what I had heard: she had refused the farther channel without new pay, and Laman had refused to use the unsafe stones. Sela had chosen one basket to carry by land. None of those decisions belonged to me.

Raya called to the cord owner.

"If you want a step, bring sound wood and ask me what it will cost. Do not bring another boat to test the rotten one."

He gathered his line and went to her. Sela took her empty basket up the market path. I was left with a small leaf of grain in my hand and the knowledge that the buyer had accepted only one of three baskets.

I had earned that grain by carrying. The boat had carried nothing. Tomorrow the woman who owned the third basket would have a question for Sela, and Sela would have an answer that included my name. I could not make it a better answer by telling her the truth had been preserved in the hall.

***

The next morning I found the cord owner tying three knots in a fresh length of line.

He was at the market path where it met the river, not at the hall. The clay-weight trader stood beside him with a jar covered in cloth. Sela had come without a basket. Laman was there too, his boat pulled high enough onto the firm bank that the water could not reach its bow. Hiran was nowhere in sight.

I had gone down to see whether Raya and Madan had found time to inspect the uncut timber. Instead I found four people waiting for me.

"We have spoken among ourselves," the cord owner said.

"About the step?"

"About where our goods will go next market. The step is part of it."

Sela crossed her arms. "Tell him what we agreed. Then tell him what we did not."

The cord owner loosened the first knot.

"We want to know before we hire a boat whether the landing can take a load. We want a place for the baskets to wait without ruining another household's work. We want a man to give an answer when the water changes."

"A man?" Sela asked.

"Someone," he corrected. "A person who can see the bank."

"Who will pay that person?" I asked.

The clay-weight trader lifted his jar. "I have offered a measure from each boat."

"I have refused that," Sela said.

"And I have not refused to pay for news about my own boat," Laman said. "I will pay a man I hire to watch for me. I will not pay for the clay-weight trader's jars unless I choose to carry them."

The cord owner tightened his line again. The knots had been tied before I arrived. They had not tied the people together.

"Then we are not agreed," I said.

"We agree that your bank cannot take a load now," Sela answered. "We agree that we have lost work because men were told it could. We agree that we will ask before we come again. Those are enough reasons to come together."

"Will you ask the keeper?"

"We will tell her what we have said," the cord owner answered. "We have not asked her to decide it for us."

I followed them to the hall. On the way, we passed Dala's net spread beside the upper path. She was working a new cord through its edge. She watched the group go by without rising.

"Are you bringing baskets?" she called.

"No," Sela said. "We have come to speak."

"Then speak without standing on the net."

The cord owner moved his line clear of the weights. He had learned that much.

The keeper was at the doorway. She had heard us approaching and had brought her knife and three blank strips outside, where there was room for people to stand without crowding the hall. She did not invite anyone onto Dala's ground. We gathered on the firm patch near the old turning.

"What is the question?" she asked.

The cord owner repeated the three things he wanted: an inspected step, a waiting place and a person who could say whether the landing was usable. This time he did not mention a charge until the keeper asked what would pay for the work.

"I would pay a watcher for my boats," he said. "The others may join if they wish."

"That is different from a measure from every boat," Sela said.

"It is what I have been trying to arrange."

"It is not what the oil seller heard."

The cord owner's face hardened.

"Salin came yesterday and said a man had been shown a marked charge. I have shown no one such a mark. I told the oil seller there was a recorded crossing and that I wanted a watcher. If he made the two things into a fee, he made it himself."

"Did you tell him the step was closed?" Sela asked.

"I told him Raya had not finished it."

"Did you tell him there was no place to unload?"

"I told him there was a crossing."

"Then you left out the part he needed."

The cord owner turned toward me.

"Will you say I lied?"

"I heard you offer watchers. I heard you say you had told owners the passage was watched. I did not hear what you said to the oil seller."

"Then say only that."

"I will."

The keeper took up a blank strip but did not cut it. She looked toward the net.

"Dala, will you hear what they want of your path?"

Dala kept working for a moment. Then she laid down the bone point, rose, and came as far as the edge of the weights.

"I can hear from here."

"They want a place for baskets to wait," the keeper said.

"They have asked before."

"They say they will ask before each market."

"Then I will answer before each market, if I know what day and what load. I will not give them the place every time a boat comes."

"Could a watcher stand here?" the cord owner asked.

"Where?"

He pointed at the dry patch beyond the net.

"When the water is high, my weights go there," Dala said. "You may ask me on a day when I do not need it. You may not hire someone to stand there every day and tell me afterward."

"Could you name a place farther down?"

"Raya says the lower stone moves. If you want a man to stand in water and call that a place, ask the man. I will not tell him it is safe."

Raya came up from the landing with wet mud on her shins. She had heard her name and stopped beside the keeper. I asked whether she had inspected Madan's timber.

"Not yet. Madan sent word that the house above the bend needs him first. He will come when he has done what he promised them."

"Could the shortened piece serve for one market?" the cord owner asked.

"No."

"Could a lighter boat use the stones?"

"A boat owner may judge his boat. I am telling you the step will not carry a loaded person. If you ask me to open it, I refuse."

She showed us the end of the timber she had cut away. The dark grain broke under her thumb. The clay-weight trader took a step back.

"I would not set my jars on that," he said.

"Then do not," Raya answered.

Laman crouched to look at the piece. He had refused the stones yesterday, but now he could see what the repair needed. He lifted the rotten end and let it fall back onto the ground.

"How long until you have sound wood?"

"I do not know. Ache has asked Madan. Madan has other work. I will not tell you a day until I have seen the piece and know who will lift it."

The cord owner pulled at his knots. He wanted an answer he could carry to the owners before they hired crews. Raya had given him one, but it was the answer he could not sell.

***

Hiran came along the river path while the clay-weight trader was arguing about the price of a watcher. She had a pole across one shoulder and a small bundle in her other hand. She stopped when she saw Laman.

"I have finished the boat I took this morning," she said. "Are you hiring me again?"

"Not today," Laman answered.

"Then I will hear what you are asking before I go."

The cord owner explained the proposed watcher. Hiran listened until he reached the part about a measure from every boat.

"Who is the watcher?" she asked.

"We have not hired one."

"Then whose wage are you dividing?"

The trader with the jar looked down. "We could agree on a measure now and hire someone afterward."

"You could. If the measure is too little, you will ask the watcher to work for less than you promised the boats he would cost. If it is too much, the boats will pay for a man who has not yet stood in the rain."

"Then name a wage," the cord owner said.

"For what hours? In which water? Who tells him to leave when Dala needs her ground? Who feeds him if no boat comes? I can name what I charge to pole a boat because I know the journey. You have not named the work."

Sela turned to the keeper.

"That is what I want remembered. I will pay for a true answer before my fish leave the stalls. I will not pay a fee to every boat that passes. Yesterday I paid carriers to bring three baskets down. One reached the buyer by land. Two went back. Their owners lost a sale. I want to know whether the step is open before I hire anyone again."

The keeper looked at Laman.

"And you?"

"I want the right to refuse a place I have not sounded. I will not put my boat against a rotten step because someone has cut a mark. If I hire Hiran, I will tell her the journey before she agrees. If it changes, I will ask her again."

"And the trader with the jar?"

"I still want someone to tell me when the landing is usable. I would share the cost if the others wished. They do not all wish it. I will pay for my own news if I can find a man willing to bring it."

The cord owner looked at me.

"Then what can Ache give us?"

The keeper did not answer for me. I could see Amaya coming down the market path with a small basket. She stopped a short distance away. She had not been summoned. She had come to collect jars from a house near the river.

"I can carry loads when I have agreed to carry them," I said. "I can help Raya lift timber if she asks me and if I am free when she needs the hands. I can tell a boat owner what I have seen of the water. I cannot open Dala's path or Nali's. I cannot hire Hiran for Laman. I cannot give you a mark that makes the step safe."

The cord owner stared at me.

"You can do what any carrier can do."

"Sometimes I fail even at that."

Amaya had come close enough to hear. She did not smile.

"He failed Tesa's first carrying morning," she said. "He kept the next work he took and brought me the rice. I accepted it. He still owes me a cloth."

The clay-weight trader looked toward her.

"Would you let him work on the landing until the step is ready?"

"He may choose his work. He may not tell you that my cloth is paid because he has carried timber for someone else."

I had wanted to leave Amaya's debt out of the meeting. She had made it part of what these people could judge. I could not ask them to trust my next promise while asking her to keep waiting without an answer.

The keeper held the blank strip flat against her palm.

"I can mark who was here and what each person said. I cannot mark an agreement that did not happen. Shall I tell you what I heard?"

She repeated the terms one by one. Sela wanted notice before hiring carriers and refused a universal charge. Laman would not land against unsafe stones and would negotiate any changed journey. Hiran would not accept unnamed watcher work or an altered pole journey without a new agreement. Dala would consider particular requests for her ground, not permanent occupation. Raya would not open the step before inspection and repair. The trader with the jar would pay for news about his own cargo. The cord owner still wanted to hire a watcher for boats that chose his service.

When she reached me, she said, "Ache offers to carry work he can actually perform. He has not offered wood he owns, a repaired step, or the use of another household's path."

I nodded.

"Does anyone say I have heard them wrongly?" she asked.

The cord owner raised his line.

"You have not said that we will take our boats elsewhere."

"Have you all agreed to that?"

"We have agreed we cannot use this step."

"That is not the same as agreeing where to go."

Sela looked at Laman. He said he would take his next hired load by the farther channel if the owner chose it and paid for the journey. Sela said she would hire carriers on the upper path if the fish could reach the buyer in time. The clay-weight trader wanted to inspect another landing before deciding. They had come together to demand a reliable place; they would leave by different routes.

The cord owner pulled his three knots apart. He had tied them to make a single demand. Now he put the line over his shoulder without asking the keeper to keep it.

"Next market I will bring owners to the other landing," he said. "There is dry ground there at low water, and a man willing to say when it is full. I will ask them to pay him for that news. I will not bring them here on your word."

"You should not," Raya said.

He looked at her, then at me.

"When the step is repaired, tell the owners. Do not expect them to come because you have remembered why they left."

He went up the path. The trader with the jar followed, still undecided. Laman went back toward his boat. Hiran left by the lower market path without offering to pole it. Sela stayed long enough to ask Dala whether a basket could stand on the dry patch two mornings later. Dala asked which basket and at what tide. Sela said she did not yet know. They agreed that Sela would ask again when she did.

The keeper cut marks for the things she had heard, leaving space between them. She did not make one for the charge. She did not make one for the other landing. I watched her put the strip beside Salin's report of the rumor. The two were close enough that a stranger might mistake them for one account. She moved them apart.

Amaya lifted her basket.

"Will you come with me to bring the jars?" she asked.

"Yes."

"They are at the house above the bend. We can carry one each. I am not paying you to do it."

"I know."

We went together. Behind us, Raya was already returning to the timber she could not yet replace. Dala bent over her net. The water moved against the old stones, covering the lower one first. I could still hear the cord owner's voice in my head, telling the owners he would bring them elsewhere next market. He had not won every trader's agreement. He had enough to make a journey without us.

Amaya stopped where the path narrowed.

"Are you coming?"

I caught up and took the basket she offered. It was empty now. I would have to bring it back full without letting the jars strike one another. That was work I could begin before the water changed.
