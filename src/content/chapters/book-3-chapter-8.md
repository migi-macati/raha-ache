The keeper had already put her knife away.

Ilan's boat was passing the far side of the turn, its stern lifting and falling in the wake of a larger hull. I could still see the pole-handler standing with his feet apart. He had finished the journey Ranu paid him for. He was going somewhere I had not asked him to go.

"Ask who is willing to go," the keeper had said.

I looked at the strip in her hand.

"You could carry it."

"Then who will stay with the claims here?"

"I could."

"You can hear them. You do not know which marks belong to which witnesses unless I tell you, and I cannot make Nali accept a thing because you carry it."

She turned the strip over. There was one cut for the journey, another for the return witnessed from our bank, and a small notch beside the place where Iya's words had been repeated across the water. It was a poor thing to send without the woman who had made it. The cut for the return did not show the broken rack above Nali's path. The notch did not show the moment Ilan had turned away from the stronger current before trying again.

"Then I will ask Iya," I said.

"She is across the water."

"She can come again."

"If she wishes."

The keeper placed the strip on the shelf with its cut edge outward. She kept it apart from the older one for the disputed two-basket journey. I had watched her do that so often that I could have told a stranger which was which. I could not have told him why the second mark had been made unless I had heard the people who spoke.

Dala was folding her net farther up the path. She had carried it clear of the wet channel before the tide turned. I went to her.

"Would you take a message to Nali if someone paid your passage?"

"No."

"I have not said what the message is."

"You have said I must leave my net."

"Only long enough to cross."

"How long was Ranu's crossing?"

"Longer than we expected."

"Then you know why I said no."

She lifted the net. A knot caught on the edge of her basket and pulled a strand tight. She worked it free with her thumb. I stood beside her with nothing to carry.

"The keeper wants someone to hear the words on both banks," I said.

"She can ask someone who has heard both. I was here when Ranu stepped onto the stones. I did not hear what Nali said when she got home."

"Iya did."

"Then ask Iya. Do not send me to repeat what I did not hear."

Dala moved past me. She was not angry. She had a basket to bring home before the air grew wet, and I had asked her to give me an hour that belonged to her.

At the old landing a man was waiting with a small boat. I knew him by the coil of cord on his shoulder. He had supplied cord when the marker was placed and had argued for paid watchers. His boat carried no cargo. A younger man held it against the higher bank, where the stones were dry.

"I heard a woman crossed today," the owner said.

"One woman with a sample, and she went back."

"Then the step is open."

"The upper step is still closed. Raya let her use the old stones while they were exposed."

He looked at the barrier above the water.

"I have two boats coming with pots in the morning. If I tell them to come here, will your keeper show them the way?"

"She can tell them what was agreed. She cannot open Dala's path or Nali's."

"They do not want Nali's path. They want this one."

"Then they must ask Dala, and Raya must judge the stones when they arrive."

He rubbed the cord against his palm.

"I have told the owners the passage is being watched."

"By whom?"

"By the men who stand here when a boat comes."

"No one has been hired to stand here."

"Then perhaps I will hire them myself."

"You may hire men for your own boat."

"And if they help another boat?"

"That owner can decide what to pay them."

"Every boat will wait for another to pay. I have seen it happen."

He was not wrong about the waiting. I had stood beside a boat while a pole-handler's wrist went red from holding it. I had also heard the same owner propose a passing charge that no one had accepted. I looked at his cord. It was useful cord. He had given useful things before.

"If you hire watchers," I said, "tell the owners who pays them. Do not say the keeper has marked a charge."

"Then give me something I can carry to the owners."

I thought of the strip on the shelf. He would have carried it. He would also have explained it in the words he preferred.

"The keeper can tell you what she marked," I said.

"She has told me before. I need something a man can show when he is asked why he should trust this bank."

"I cannot give him a mark that promises more than one journey."

The owner looked at the boat he had brought.

"Then he may send his pots by another channel. You will have told him why."

He turned away. The younger man let the bow come free of the bank and climbed in. I had not asked their names. I could not say whether the pots would come tomorrow. The owner had brought me an opportunity to make a larger promise, and I had refused it. He had brought his own boat to hear the answer. I watched it go.

The keeper was closing the hall when I returned. She had heard the end of our exchange.

"He may tell people I turned him away," I said.

"He may. He was here."

"He will leave out the part about the fee."

"Then someone who heard it must be willing to say that part."

I looked at her knife, put away beside the shelf. I wanted her to cut one more mark for what the owner had said. She had not been there to hear it. I could tell her, but that would be my account.

"Tomorrow I will find someone to go across," I said.

"Tomorrow you said you would go with Amaya."

I had already begun to forget.

***

Amaya came for me before the sun reached the roofline.

She carried the old cloth with the dark square patch. It was folded into four and tied with a thin cord. She did not ask whether I had slept. She looked at my hands.

"Have you brought anything to pay with?"

"Nothing that would buy a cloth."

"Then you will hear what one costs."

We took the upper market path. A woman passed us with a shallow tray of fish balanced on her head. Behind her two boys were leading a small goat with a fiber cord. They kept stopping to let it bite at leaves. Amaya walked past them without changing her pace. I had to lengthen my stride to stay beside her.

At a house above the market path, a weaver named Tesa was sorting cloth beneath an awning. She had three lengths folded on a reed mat, each held at one end by a smooth stone. A young woman sat beside her winding thread around a spindle. Tesa looked at Amaya's old cloth before she looked at me.

"You want one of that measure?"

"One fit to be used," Amaya said. "He owes it to my household."

Tesa unfolded a length of coarse cloth. It was strong, but one edge had a narrow place where the weave pulled apart under her fingers. Amaya touched it.

"Not that one."

"It is cheaper."

"It will not hold at the edge."

Tesa put it aside without arguing. She brought out another, pale where the light struck it and darker in the folds. Amaya held it against her measure. She tested the threads at one corner and turned it to look at the opposite edge.

"This would do."

Tesa named the price in rice and wax. I had neither amount. I asked whether she would take work instead.

"What work do you do?" she asked.

"I can carry loads from the river."

"Everyone says that until the load is wet."

"I carried jars yesterday."

"Did they reach the house unbroken?"

"Yes."

"Whose jars?"

I told her the basket maker's name. Tesa knew her. She sent the young woman to ask whether the jars had arrived and whether I had kept my word. I stood in the shade while she went. Amaya did not speak for me.

When the young woman returned, she said the basket maker had received the jars, but I had failed to come the day before and another carrier had been paid. Tesa nodded.

"I have bundles coming from the lower market two mornings from now," she said. "If you carry them and help dry the cloth after the crossing, I can count that work toward this piece. It will not pay the whole price. I will tell you the rest when I see what arrives."

"Will you hold the cloth?" I asked.

"Until the next market morning. If someone pays for it first, I will sell it."

"Then how can I promise Amaya that I will bring it?"

"You cannot promise what I have not sold you."

Amaya folded her old cloth.

"I have not asked him to promise another day," she said. "I asked him to see what he owes."

I looked at the length Tesa had laid aside. It was a thing I could touch, unlike the money I kept expecting from the landing. If I worked for her, I would miss another morning at the river. If I stayed at the river, I would have no better answer for Amaya.

"I will come for the work," I said.

Tesa looked at me for a moment.

"Come before the bundles arrive. I will not keep the boat waiting because you have other business."

"I understand."

"You have said that before," Amaya said, quietly enough that only I heard.

We went back along the market path. I wanted to tell her that the work would bring the cloth closer, that I had begun paying in the only way I could. She had seen the price and the seller. She knew I had not paid.

At the hall the keeper was standing beside a woman with wet sandals. I recognized Iya before she turned. She had come across the river without sending for me.

"I waited," Iya said. "The keeper told me you were away."

"I was with Amaya."

"I know. She told me you owed her a cloth."

Amaya looked at the keeper. The keeper held her hands open.

"I told Iya only that you had gone to seek it," she said.

Iya was holding the end of a narrow cord. The fibers were dark where the water had touched them.

"Nali sent me," she said. "A man came to her yard with two bundles this morning. He said your crossing yesterday gave him leave to use the path. She sent him away. He says he heard the permission from a man at your landing."

"Who?"

"He would not give her a name. He said the permission had been marked."

The keeper turned toward the shelf. The strip was still there, untouched since Ranu's return.

I had wanted to be the person who explained what it meant. Iya had already heard a version that made the broken rack's path open to another load. I had been away doing work I owed. For once, the river had not waited for me to come back.

"Will you tell me exactly what Nali said?" I asked.

Iya looked at Amaya, then at the strip.

"I will tell you what she told me," she said. "You may decide whether that is enough."

***

Iya put the cord on the table.

It was the same dark fiber I had seen in her hand at the doorway, but there were two fresh knots near one end. Water had made the loose strands swell. She spread them with her fingers until the cord lay straight between the keeper's knife and the strip from yesterday's crossing.

"That is what Nali gave me," she said.

"What do the knots mean?" I asked.

"They mean I must remember two things. I will tell you what they are. Do not ask me to show them to someone else as proof."

The keeper moved her knife away from the cord.

"Then tell us."

"First, the woman with the cane came back by the path Nali allowed. She did not put down a load or ask a man to carry one for her. Second, that permission ended when she reached Nali's yard. My sister did not give the path to every boat that followed."

"That is what we marked here," I said.

"Then why did a man arrive this morning with two bundles and say your mark gave him leave?"

"He may have heard the owner who wanted watchers."

"May have?"

"I do not know who told him."

Iya looked at the strip on the keeper's shelf. It lay beside the older claims, its cut edge turned toward the light. She had seen the keeper cut it after Ranu returned. She had not been here when the keeper set it away.

"Did you make a mark that says Nali refused the next load?" she asked.

"I marked what we heard of Ranu's return," the keeper said. "Iya called across that Nali allowed only that journey. I did not hear Nali herself at this table."

"Then you marked what I said."

"Yes."

"And if I had said it wrongly?"

"Someone who heard Nali would have to correct me."

Iya lifted the cord.

"That is why I came back. Nali says the man with the bundles had heard that the marked crossing was a way open to both banks. She sent him away. He left one bundle on the wet ground while he argued, then took it up again. He did not break another slat. He made her children stop their drying work to stand by the path until he left."

"Did he say who sent him?" I asked.

"He said he had been told at Maynila. Nali asked for a name. He would not give one."

"Did she know his boat?"

"He came on foot from above the bend. She did not see his boat."

I had been imagining a merchant coming across from our landing. The man might have come from another house entirely. The words had traveled farther than the boat that first carried Ranu.

"Then how can you say the permission came from here?" I asked.

"I have not said it did. I have said he claimed it did."

The keeper put her hand on the shelf. She had made the marks in front of witnesses, but the strip had already acquired a meaning no one present had agreed to give it.

"Let me tell you what I can say," she told Iya. "Ranu paid Ilan for one journey from Nali's path to our stones and back. The pole-handler accepted food for his work. Raya allowed one person to use the exposed stones while the water was low. Dala allowed that woman to pass above her net. Nali allowed her own path for that same journey. We did not make a new agreement for other loads."

"Who heard all of those words?" Iya asked.

"Not one person," the keeper said. "I heard the words spoken here. You heard the words on Nali's bank. Some of us heard what you called across."

Iya sat on the low bench. She did not touch the strip.

"I was asked to come because my sister is tired of strangers explaining her own ground to her," she said. "If I take your strip back and tell her it means only one journey, the next man will say I am speaking for Maynila. If I tell the next man Nali said no, he will say I am only her sister. Both things are true. Neither gives him leave."

I wanted to tell her that being Nali's sister made her the right person to speak. But I had heard Dala refuse to carry words she had not witnessed. Kinship could make a person care about a claim. It could also make someone else refuse to believe her.

"Would you take a witness from here?" I asked.

"Who?"

"The keeper."

"Then who hears the people who come to your hall while she is away?"

I had asked the same question yesterday and disliked the answer then.

"Someone else could sit here."

"Who knows what she has heard?"

I looked at the shelf. The older strips were not all mine. Some belonged to people who had brought claims while I was away. I could have held the knife and cut a mark, but I would not know what question the mark answered.

"Could you take the strip without her?" I asked.

Iya looked at me.

"Would you like me to say what you tell me?"

"Only what was agreed."

"Then say it to me."

I repeated the keeper's account. I named the boat, the woman with cane, the old stones and the two paths. I said the passage did not make a charge for passing boats or open Nali's yard to other cargo.

Iya listened until I finished.

"You left out the first time Ilan turned back," she said.

"That was because the current was too strong."

"It was part of what happened. A man who hears only that a woman crossed may think the river can be used whenever he wishes."

"Then tell him the first approach failed."

"And you left out the three pieces of cane the maker bought."

"That was their trade. It did not change the path."

"It changed what Ranu would say the journey was worth. If you ask her to come again, she will remember the cost."

I could feel the keeper watching me. I had told Iya the words I thought mattered to the landing. She was telling me what the people who paid for the journey might remember.

"I did not mean to hide those things," I said.

"I know. That is why I will not carry only your words."

She put one finger on the first knot.

"Nali asked me to tell you that she will hear a person who comes to ask about the path. She will not receive a man who arrives with bundles and says he has already been given leave."

"And the second knot?"

"She wants someone from this bank to hear her say it, so the next time a stranger claims your hall gave permission there will be more than my word against his."

"Would she come here?"

"She came yesterday. It cost her a canoe journey and half a morning. She has a broken rack to mend. You cannot ask her to keep crossing because you do not wish to cross yourself."

The keeper took a blank strip from the shelf. She did not reach for the knife.

"If a person from here goes, I can tell that person what I heard. But Nali must be free to say something different. I will not cut her answer before she gives it."

Iya nodded.

"That is what she asked."

Outside the hall someone shouted for the old stones. A man came to the doorway carrying a narrow basket covered with leaves. He had been told that a woman crossed yesterday and wanted to put a bundle ashore before the water rose. The keeper asked who owned his boat.

"My brother," he said.

"Has your brother agreed to wait while you ask Dala and Raya?"

"He told me to find a landing."

"Then ask him whether he will hold the boat here. The upper step is closed."

The man looked past us toward the shelf.

"Is that the strip for the crossing?"

"It is a reminder of one journey that finished," the keeper said.

"Then why keep it where anyone can see it?"

"So anyone who was there may tell me if I have remembered wrongly."

He frowned. He had expected a mark that would tell him where to put his basket. He had found three people who could tell him what someone else had done yesterday. He went back to his boat without asking to use the stones. We heard him call to his brother and then heard the pole strike the water.

Iya watched him leave.

"He will tell someone the landing turned him away," she said.

"He was not turned away," I answered. "He was asked to find out whether his boat could wait."

"That may be the same thing when the water is moving."

I could not make him wait by explaining the difference.

The keeper set the blank strip on the table.

"Will you tell Nali I have heard her question?" she asked Iya.

"Yes. I was here when you heard it."

"Will you tell her I have agreed to her path?"

"No. You cannot agree for her."

The keeper smiled a little, then stopped. Iya had not been joking.

"And will you carry a message from Ache?" I asked.

"I will tell her you were here. I will tell her you asked me to carry words and that I refused to carry only your account. If you want more than that, find someone who can hear both of us."

I looked at her wet sandals. She had crossed for her sister's sake and had no reason to make a second journey on mine. Amaya had already taken me to a seller whose cloth I could not yet buy. I had promised work on another morning. A crossing would cost someone food, time and a boat, even before a word was spoken.

"Who would you trust to come?" I asked.

"Nali will decide whom she hears. You must decide whom you ask. Those are different questions."

She retied the cord. The two knots remained where Nali had put them.

"Do not send a man who expects me to open the rack path because he carries your strip," she said. "Send someone who is willing to hear me say no."

She rose to go. I could have offered to walk with her to the bank, but that would not make a boat appear. I asked whether she had arranged her return.

"The woman who brought me will take me back when she has sold her fish," Iya said. "I have paid her for both ways. She has not promised to carry anyone else."

I walked with Iya as far as the higher path. From there we could see the boat she had hired, pulled onto dry ground above the turn. Its owner was counting fish into a customer's basket. Iya waited at a distance rather than call her away from the sale.

The keeper came to stand beside me with the blank strip.

"Will you mark that Nali asked for a witness?" I said.

"I can mark that Iya brought the request and that I heard it. I will not mark that someone has agreed to go."

I looked across the water toward Nali's trees. The path was hidden from this side. Iya's two knots were a better reminder to her than our strip would ever be, but they were hers to explain.

"Then keep it blank until we find the person," I said.

The keeper did not cut it. Iya waited for her boat, and the river kept moving between the two accounts.

***

The woman who had brought Iya across the river was packing the last of her fish into a shallow basket.

Iya stood beyond the shade of her awning. She had paid for the return and was waiting without calling the woman away from her customers. A boy sat on the canoe's overturned bailer, picking scales from the wet edge with a piece of reed. He looked up whenever someone approached the boat.

I went to the woman, keeping out of the path of a man carrying a basket of shells.

"Are you taking Iya home?"

"When I finish here." She tied the basket's lid with a strip of fiber. "If you have something to say to her, say it before I push off."

"I want someone from this bank to hear what her sister says."

"Then go."

"I have work I promised."

"So have I."

She turned the basket to test the knot. One fish had slipped beneath the lid. She pushed it back with the flat of her hand.

"I am Ache," I said.

"I know. I am Bina. I heard your name when a man wanted to land a basket and the keeper asked him whose boat it was."

"He left."

"He had no agreement to wait. His brother had a buyer upriver." Bina lifted the basket onto a plank. "They went where the buyer was."

I had thought of the man as someone who had refused our landing. He had been a brother trying to make a sale.

"Would you hear the keeper's account before you go?" I asked.

"Of what?"

"Ranu's crossing yesterday. And the man who says it opened Nali's path."

"I brought Iya here this morning. I have not been hired to settle anyone's quarrel."

"You would only need to listen."

"Only." She pointed to the boy at the canoe. "That is Kito. He poles when the water is too strong for me to manage the basket and the steering together. I pay him whether I sell the fish or not. If I stop to listen, he stops too."

Kito heard his name and stood. He had a red scrape across the back of one heel.

"Would you wait?" I asked him.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Then ask Bina. She tells me when I am working. I tell her if I can do it."

Bina put her hands on her hips.

"You have found two people to ask. Have you found anything to pay them?"

I looked at the baskets. One held fish that had not sold. A third was empty except for a small weight stone. I could carry them, but carrying them now would not replace her morning.

"Could I work for you tomorrow?"

"What work?"

"Carry your baskets from the canoe to the market."

"Who carries them today?"

"Kito?"

"He poles the boat and helps me when it is tied. If you take his carrying, I do not get to keep his wage."

"I did not mean to take it."

"Then tell me what you offer."

I looked toward the hall. The keeper was there, holding the blank strip. I could have asked her for rice from the hall stores. Those stores belonged to people who had left them against claims, and she would have asked which of them had agreed to pay my witness.

"I can carry your baskets to the upper sellers tomorrow," I said. "After Kito is paid for the pole, if there is work left."

Bina looked at my hands.

"Those are hands that carried jars."

"Tesa asked the basket maker whether I had kept a bargain. She learned I had missed a day."

"Then I know what kind of promise you make."

"I will be here."

"That is what a man says before another man comes with a boat."

She did not turn away. I waited.

"I come to this market when the catch and the water allow," she said. "I cannot promise a morning's fish merely to give you a chance to work. If I come tomorrow, I may have baskets for you. If I do not, your promise buys nothing."

"Then what would you accept today?"

"Tell me what you want me to do, from beginning to end. Do not say 'listen' as though the river will do the rest."

I asked Iya to come closer. She approached but did not speak for Bina.

"The keeper will tell you what she heard here," I said. "You can take Iya back, as you have already agreed. If Nali is willing, you can hear her own words. When you next bring fish to this bank, you could tell the keeper what you heard."

"And if Nali will not speak?"

"Tell us that."

"And if I hear that you have marked something falsely?"

"Tell us that too."

Bina untied the basket she had just secured. She counted the remaining fish, touching each with a wet finger.

"If I listen here, I miss the last buyers. If I wait for Nali, Kito waits in the boat. If I return to tell you, I spend time away from the market. You have named no price for any of it."

"I have no rice enough to hire a day."

"Then do not hire a day. Ask me whether I wish to carry a thing I can tell truthfully. The price of that is mine to name."

Iya watched us from the edge of the awning. She had made two crossings for her sister and paid Bina for both. She was not offering the fare a third time.

Bina lifted one unsold fish by the tail.

"This one will not be better tomorrow. I can sell it for less at the drying shed. If you carry this basket there now, the shed woman may take it before the heat spoils it. I will hear the keeper while Kito washes the canoe. That is what I can give today. No promise to return before my next market day."

I looked at Iya. She had already waited through our bargain.

"Will that delay your return?" I asked.

"Some," she said. "I paid Bina to take me home. If she agrees to hear you, I can wait while she hears. I will not ask her to wait half a day for Nali after we arrive."

"Nor will I," Bina said.

I took the basket. Its bottom was damp enough to soak my cloth. The drying shed stood above the market path, farther than I had thought. By the time the woman there had examined the fish and named her price, the sun was on the open stones. She gave me a small piece of smoked fish as a carrier's portion. I had expected nothing, but I took it. Bina's fish had sold for less than it might have earlier.

When I returned, Bina was tying the canoe's painter. Kito had washed the scales from the bailer and set it upright to dry.

"You are late," Bina said.

"The woman argued over the price."

"She always does. Did she take the basket?"

"All of it."

"Then the carrying was worth something. It has paid for the time I lost here, not for a second journey."

"I understand."

"You may eat that fish. It is your wage, not mine."

I put it in my cloth. I had come to buy a witness and had earned a mouthful of food.

The keeper was at the doorway when we reached the hall. She brought out the strip from Ranu's crossing, the blank one, and the older strip from the two-basket dispute. She laid them apart.

Bina stayed standing.

"I can hear until Kito calls," she said. "He knows when the water will turn."

The keeper described the journey from our bank: the failed first approach, Raya's inspection, Dala's limited path, the piece of cane carried by Ranu, the three pieces bought by the basket maker, and the return boat. She said what she had seen. When she came to Nali's permission, she stopped.

"I heard Iya call that her sister would allow this one return," she said. "I did not stand beside Nali when she spoke."

Bina looked at the strip.

"Does that mark tell me where Ranu put her feet?"

"No. It tells me what I wanted to remember of the journey."

"Then why did you put it where a man could point to it as permission for his bundles?"

The keeper's fingers tightened around the strip.

"Because the people who were there should be able to correct it."

"That is an answer for people who were there," Bina said. "The man with the bundles was not."

I wanted to tell Bina the man had misused the mark, not the keeper. I had seen the man who wanted watchers point to the same shelf. He had wanted something he could show another owner. I had refused to give it to him, but I had left the strip where he could describe it as he pleased.

"Would you carry the strip?" I asked.

"No."

"You have not heard the rest."

"I have heard enough to know the cuts will not speak when I am absent. I can carry my account. The keeper may keep hers."

The keeper put the marked strip back on the shelf. She did not reach for the blank one.

"Tell us what you heard, then," she said.

"I heard that Ranu made one crossing and returned. I heard you say the upper step was closed, the old stones were used only with Raya's leave, and Dala allowed one woman above her net. I heard Iya say Nali had allowed that woman on her path for that journey. I did not hear Nali."

"That is accurate," the keeper said.

Bina looked at me.

"And I heard Ache ask me to carry his account. I refused to promise it would be his when I brought it back."

"I asked you to hear both banks," I said.

"You asked for a witness before you knew what I would charge."

I had no answer that improved what I had done.

Kito called from the canoe. The water had begun to draw away from the higher stones. Bina picked up her empty baskets.

"At the other bank," she said to Iya, "I will take you where we agreed. If Nali is working, I will not take her from it for a hearing she has not accepted. You may ask whether she wishes to speak. I will listen if she does. If she does not, I will go home."

"And the report?" I asked.

"When I next come to sell fish, if I have heard anything worth bringing, I will tell the keeper. If you want me to come sooner, you must make a different bargain and pay Kito for his time as well."

Kito was already aboard. He had not heard the keeper's words. Bina called down to him to hold the bow while Iya climbed in. He said he could wait until she sat, and no longer.

Iya turned once on the bank.

"I will tell Nali that Bina has listened here," she said. "I will not tell her she must listen there."

I held the canoe while Iya put her foot against the plank. Bina stepped in after her and took the steering oar. Kito set the pole against the riverbed. The bow moved away before I could ask another question.

The keeper stood beside me with the uncut strip. I had found a woman willing to hear me, a boat already going to the other bank, and no promise of a return message. I had also promised Tesa my labor two mornings from now. If Bina came to market on that same morning, I could not carry both women's bundles.

The fish in my cloth had begun to smell of smoke. I took it out and ate it while the canoe moved toward the far bend.

***

Bina came back with fish the next morning.

I had not expected her. I had gone to the landing to see whether Raya had opened the step. Her staff still lay across the upper path. The timber beneath it had been lifted clear at one end and propped above the wet hollow. A small patch of fresh earth showed where Raya had begun to clear the loose mud. The step could not bear a foot. I turned away before she could find me standing over work I did not understand.

At the market Bina was lowering a basket from her canoe. Kito stood in the stern with his pole upright. His scraped heel was wrapped in a dry strip of cloth.

"Did Nali speak?" I asked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Good morning."

"Carry this first. I must sell the fish before I can spend time with your keeper."

I took the basket she indicated. She had already set a second one on the bank, where the ground was firm. I carried the first to the shade she used yesterday and came back for the other. Kito kept the canoe clear of the stones while Bina counted the fish.

"Does that pay for your account?" I asked.

"It pays for carrying two baskets. Yesterday you carried one to the drying shed. Today you have carried two to the market. We have made no bargain about a third."

I wanted to ask whether she had remembered every word. I had not been there to hear them, and she had already warned me what my asking would sound like.

"Will you come when you have sold?" I asked.

"If the keeper is willing to hear me. If a buyer comes, I will serve the buyer first."

I left her to the sale. The keeper was opening the hall. I told her Bina had returned.

"Then let her come when she is ready," she said.

"Should I put the strip on the table?"

"Which strip?"

"The one for Ranu's crossing."

"I will take it down if she asks to see it. We are not going to make her answer a piece of wood."

I sat on the low bench. A man came with a broken jar and asked whether the keeper knew who had carried it from the landing. She did not. He left the jar beside the door while he went to ask the carrier. It was still there when Bina arrived.

Bina brought no strip and no cord. She carried a small fish basket under her arm, empty now except for the smell of the morning's catch. Kito did not follow. He was taking the canoe to a place upstream where the current would not drive it against the bank while she spoke.

"How long can you stay?" the keeper asked.

"Until Kito calls or a buyer finds me. I am not here for a hearing that lasts all day."

The keeper took the marked strip from the shelf and placed it beside the blank one. Bina looked at them without sitting.

"I took Iya to her sister's bank," she began. "Nali was lifting the rack's broken end onto a dry stone. Iya asked whether she would tell me what she wanted said here. Nali asked first who had hired me."

"What did you answer?" I asked.

"I told her Iya had paid for the journey home. I told her you had carried my fish to the drying shed in exchange for the time I spent hearing the keeper. I said no one had hired me to bring back a particular answer."

"Did she believe you?"

"She asked Iya. Iya told her the same. Then she asked whether I would take a payment from her to say the words differently. I told her I would not."

The keeper laid her palm flat on the table.

"What did Nali want corrected?"

Bina looked at me.

"She said the path she allowed Ranu to use was the higher ground behind the roots. She did not let Ranu pass through the rack path."

I had pictured Ranu stepping along the open strip beside the damaged rack. From our bank I had seen her return to the canoe. The roots hid the part between her yard and the water.

"Iya said Nali allowed her path," I said.

"Iya did. Nali says there are two paths there. One runs above the roots to the place where the canoe can be held at low water. The other passes the drying rack and the place where the children carry their fish. She allowed Ranu to return by the first. She did not open the second."

"Did you see Ranu use the first?" the keeper asked.

"No. I did not carry Ranu. I saw Nali point to the higher ground yesterday and say that was the way she had shown Ranu. I saw the rack path from where I stood. The broken end was still tied up. I cannot tell you where Ranu's foot went the day before."

The keeper picked up the strip.

"I cut one mark for the return," she said. "I did not cut one for which path."

"Then the mark is missing something," I said.

"The mark was never meant to show a path. I wrote no words on the wood. I told you what I had heard."

Bina waited until the keeper set it down.

"Nali said another thing. The man with the two bundles came on foot from above the bend. He did not come from a boat at her landing, as far as she saw. She sent him away because he claimed permission for the rack path. She did not accuse your boatmen of bringing him."

"Iya told us that," I said.

"She did. Nali wanted you to hear it from someone who had heard her say it, because people have begun speaking as though a boat from here must have carried the man."

I remembered my own question to Iya. How could she say the permission came from our landing? She had answered that she had not said it did. I had still been imagining a boat.

"Did Nali name the person who broke the rack?" I asked.

"No. She said the end was broken before Ranu came. She does not know who broke it. She has not asked Ranu to pay for it."

"And did she agree to another crossing?"

"No."

Bina said it without waiting for me to explain why another crossing might be useful.

"She said she will hear a person who asks before coming with a load. She will decide whether to let that person walk. She has not agreed to keep the root path open whenever a boat arrives. She has not agreed to let anyone use the rack path."

The keeper took the blank strip. She turned it in her hands but did not cut it.

"May I mark that you heard Nali say those things?" she asked.

"Mark that I told you I heard them. If someone asks me, I will say what I heard. If I have remembered badly, I want Nali free to correct me."

The keeper made one short cut on the blank strip and placed it apart from the older one. She said aloud what the cut would remind her of: Bina's return with Nali's correction, not Nali's agreement to anything.

"Do you want me to take your first strip back?" Bina asked.

"No. The people who saw Ranu leave may still correct my memory of that day. I will keep both. I will tell anyone who asks why they are different."

I had been waiting for the keeper to cut a mark that would end the argument. Now there were two pieces of wood and more to say when a stranger asked what they meant.

"Could you point out the two paths to someone from here?" I asked Bina.

"I could point where Nali pointed. That would not give the person leave to use either one."

"I know."

"Then you can tell him I saw them. Do not say I have shown him a landing."

She moved the empty basket from one arm to the other. She had given us the time she agreed to give, and I could see her thinking of the fish she had left in the shade.

A voice came from the doorway.

It was the owner with the coil of cord. The same younger man who had held his boat yesterday stood behind him. The owner looked at the two strips on the table.

"Which one will you show the pots coming tomorrow?" he asked.

"Neither will give them a landing," the keeper said. "The first reminds us of Ranu's one crossing. The second reminds us of a correction from Nali."

"What correction?"

Bina told him. She did not leave out the higher root path, the rack path, or the man who had come on foot. She did not tell him that every account had been settled.

He listened until she finished.

"I have told two owners they can bring their pots to the marked water," he said. "One has already paid men to carry them down to his boat."

"Did you tell them they had a right to put ashore?" I asked.

"I told them I had seen a crossing and that someone here was keeping the account."

"Then tell them what that crossing allowed."

"Will they hear the rest of your account before their boat is in the current?"

He looked at the keeper. She did not answer for me.

"I will pay men to watch the pots," he said. "If a boat arrives and the step is closed, they can keep the cargo dry while I find another place. That is my work to arrange. But I will not bring a boat here if you will say only what it cannot do."

Raya's staff was still across the upper step. The old stones would be covered by the time a heavy boat came. I could not offer him a place to land.

"I can tell you who must be asked," I said. "Dala for her path. Raya for the landing. The boat owner for the journey. If the pots must go across, Nali for her ground."

"Four answers," he said. "And the boat is paid for before I hear the first."

"Then ask before the boat leaves."

He shook his head.

"I came to hear what I could tell the owners today. I will tell them the step is closed and no other landing has been promised. They may keep their pots or choose another channel."

He did not leave at once. He looked at Bina.

"Would you carry that answer to them?"

"No. I have fish to sell."

"Even for pay?"

"Not today. You have heard it yourself."

The younger man touched the cord at the owner's shoulder. They had a boat to move and men waiting for an answer. The owner took the cord down, wound it once around his hand, and went out.

Bina watched him go.

"Was he here when the keeper told you the first account?" she asked.

"He was here yesterday," I said. "He wanted something he could show the owners."

"Then he will tell them what he heard today. You cannot make him repeat all of it."

I wanted to follow him and make certain. Instead I asked Bina whether I had kept my part of our bargain.

"You carried the fish," she said. "I came back when I chose and told the keeper what I heard. That is finished."

"And if Nali has another answer tomorrow?"

"Then you must ask who is willing to hear it."

Kito called from the path. Bina lifted her basket.

I had been ready to pay someone to carry my words across the river. A woman who owed me nothing had carried back a correction. The owner with the cord had heard it, and two boats of pots might never come. I had no way to stop him leaving with a shorter version than the one Bina gave us.

The keeper put both strips on the shelf with space between them. She left the blank wood she had not used beside her knife.

***

The pot boat did not come the next morning.

I knew this because I had gone down before the light reached the far bank. The water was still low enough to show the old stones. Raya's staff lay across the upper step, and beneath it the timber she had lifted from the mud was drying on two smaller pieces of wood. She was crouched beside it with a wedge in her hand.

"Are you opening it?" I asked.

"Does it look open?"

The timber's lower face had gone dark where the water had entered. Raya pushed the wedge against the end. A thin piece came away in her fingers.

"That part will not hold," she said. "The rest might. I will know when it is dry enough to see."

"I could bring you another length."

"Whose?"

I had not asked. I had been looking at the space where a step ought to be.

"Then do not bring me one yet," she said. "Someone has to cut it, and someone has to give it up."

I left her with the wood. A boat came around the bend carrying a woman with a basket of charcoal and a man with his feet in the bilge. They passed our stones without turning. I watched until the bow went out of sight.

By the time the market was open, the owner with the cord had returned. He came on foot. His younger companion was not with him, and there was no coil on his shoulder. He stopped at the hall door and looked at the keeper's shelf.

"Did the two pot boats turn back?" I asked.

"One did not leave. The other went by the eastern channel."

"Did they hear the correction?"

"They heard what I told them. They asked whether they could land here. I told them no one had given them that leave."

"You told them about Nali?"

"They had no wish to go to Nali. They wanted this bank, and the step was closed. Do you think they paid me to learn the name of a path across the river?"

The keeper put down the small basket she was sorting. It held two pieces of cord and a broken fastening from a load brought the day before.

"Who paid the carriers?" she asked.

"One owner paid his men to bring the pots to the boat. The boat was hired for a morning. He had to pay the men to carry the pots back again."

"Did he pay the boatman?"

"He paid for the time he had taken. The boatman lost a journey he might have made for someone else."

"And the second?"

"The second owner found a landing he had used before. He paid for the longer passage. He did not ask you to make it shorter."

I was relieved to hear that the pots had gone somewhere. The owner saw my face.

"Do not look pleased," he said. "A seller has to name a price. The extra passage will come out of something. Perhaps his buyer pays more. Perhaps the men who shaped the pots are asked to take less."

"I did not close the step."

"No. Raya did. You have told me that often enough. But I told the owners you were making a place where they could ask for a landing. Now I have to explain why asking did not bring one."

"Because there was no safe place for their boats."

"That is an answer about the wood. I have heard it. What do I tell a man who paid for a boat because I said your keeper had marked a crossing?"

The keeper did not reach for either strip.

"Tell him the crossing was Ranu's," she said. "I did not mark one for pots."

"I know what you marked now."

"You heard it when I made the mark."

"I heard one woman crossed and returned. I heard that people had agreed to let her pass. I thought it meant the landing was being made useful."

"It is useful to some people," I said.

"Not to the man who carried his pots down and back."

He spoke without raising his voice. I wanted him to be angry. Then I could have said he had made a promise no one authorized and left the matter there. Instead he had brought me a loss I could count.

"Will you tell the owners you misunderstood?" I asked.

"I have. One asked whether I had been careless or whether you had changed the terms after I left. I told him I had not asked enough questions."

"That is fair."

"He did not ask whether I was fair. He asked who would pay for the wasted morning."

The keeper turned the broken fastening over in her hand.

"What did you answer?"

"I said I had no money to pay it. He said he would remember that when I came asking for cord work again."

I had seen the owner bring cord to the landing when we needed it. He had argued for paid watchers, but he had also given us something we used. I had been content to let him carry away a refusal. It had cost him more than his temper.

"Could I speak to him?" I asked.

"You could. He will be at the lower market until the pots are loaded for another day."

"I will tell him there was no permission for the landing."

"He knows that now."

"I will tell him it was not your fault."

"It was partly mine. I spoke before I had asked. Do not go there to take that from me."

The keeper looked up at him.

"Would you like your own account kept here?" she asked.

"Would a mark pay the carriers?"

"No."

"Then I will keep the words myself."

He went out. I followed at a distance because I had said I would speak to the pot owner, and I did not want to arrive with the man whose work I had helped spoil.

***

The lower market was crowded with baskets being lifted from the mud. I found the pot owner under a roof of woven leaves. His vessels stood in two rows on a plank, each separated by twists of dry grass. A boy was testing a handle with one finger. The owner took the pot away before the boy could lift it.

"Are you Ache?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then do not touch them."

I stopped beside the plank.

"I came about the boat that did not leave."

"Which part? The boat, the pots, or the men who carried them?"

"All three."

"Then you have come to the wrong person for two of them. The boat belongs to a man who waited at the water. The carriers have gone to other work. I own the pots."

He picked up a vessel with a narrow mouth. He turned it slowly until a pale line near the base caught the light.

"This one was sound yesterday," he said. "A carrier set the basket down hard when I told him to bring it back. It may still hold oil. It may not. I will have to test it, and if it leaks I cannot sell it for the same use."

"How much did you lose?"

"I have not sold them yet. I know what I paid the carriers and the boatman. I do not know what the cracked pot will cost me. Do you have that much?"

"No."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I should know."

He set the pot down carefully.

"I asked the cord owner whether the new landing could take a load. He said a crossing had been marked and people were watching the water. I took that for an answer. I should have asked who had given leave. He should have asked before he spoke. And you should have told people plainly that your step was still shut."

"Raya told us it was shut."

"Raya was right. I am not asking her to put my pots on rotten wood."

I had prepared to defend her. The owner had taken the words out of my mouth.

"Would you bring them when she repairs it?" I asked.

"Perhaps. If I can find a boat, if the buyer still wants them, if Dala allows the path, and if I am told the truth before I hire men."

"That is what I can offer."

"You can offer to ask those people. You cannot offer their answers."

A customer approached and examined the small pots. The owner turned to him at once. I waited while they bargained over two vessels. The customer left without buying either. When the owner faced me again, his expression had not changed.

"I heard there was another path across the water," he said. "The man with the cord says your witness brought back word that it was closed."

"One path was allowed for one woman. The rack path was not."

"Then why is that my concern? I wanted to sell pots here."

"It became your concern when the crossing was told as leave for other boats."

"Someone used a woman's journey to sell me a morning's work. That is what I understand."

I looked toward the river. From where we stood I could not see Raya's step or the keeper's strips. The pot owner could not see them either. He had hired a boat on the strength of a sentence spoken in the market.

"I will go back and say this before the men who come to ask," I said. "No strip gives a landing. A person must hear from those who own the boat, keep the path, and judge the stones. The answer may be no."

"Then some men will go elsewhere."

"Yes."

"Are you willing to have them say it was your answer?"

I thought of the cord owner, who had already taken blame for speaking too soon. I thought of Bina refusing to carry my preferred words. I had wanted someone else to explain the closed path so that the refusal would not be attached to me.

"I will say it myself," I answered.

"Then say also that I paid men for a morning I could not use. You cannot make that loss vanish by telling the next man earlier."

He turned to the next customer. I had nothing more to offer him. I left while he was still weighing the price of his pots.

***

The keeper was hearing a quarrel about a broken basket when I returned. I waited outside. The woman with the basket wanted the man who had carried it to replace the missing bottom. He said the basket had been weak before he touched it. Neither had brought the maker. The keeper told them she could remember what each said, but she could not see the basket before it broke.

I had heard her say such things before. This time I stayed until both had finished.

When they left, I told her about the pot owner. I told her the cracked vessel might be worth less, and that I had no payment for it. I told her he had not asked Raya to open the step. He wanted a warning before he hired a boat.

"Did he ask us to mark his loss?" she said.

"No. He said he would remember it."

"Then let him. We cannot cut a mark for a bargain he made elsewhere."

The owner with the cord was still at the landing. He had come back with his younger companion and was checking the length of a rope against a boat's side. I went to him where others could hear.

"I spoke to the pot owner," I said.

He looked up. The younger man kept his hand on the rope.

"Did he ask you to pay?"

"He asked whether I could. I cannot. He said you spoke too soon, and so did he. He said I should have made the closed step plain before either of you hired a boat."

The owner nodded once.

"Then he told you what he told me."

"He also said he will not hire another boat until he has heard from the people whose agreement he needs."

"That will take time."

"Yes."

A woman standing with a basket of salt asked whether the old stones could take her boat that afternoon. I could have pointed to the dry edge and told her that Ranu had crossed there. The stones were visible, and the water was quieter than it had been the day before. The boatman beside her was listening.

"Raya must judge the stones," I said. "Dala must agree to the path. Your boatman must agree to wait while you ask. No one has agreed for you yet."

The woman looked toward Raya's barrier.

"Then I will go to the upper landing," she said. "My brother has carried salt there before."

The boatman took up his pole. They left before I could ask whether they would return another day.

The cord owner watched the boat move away.

"You have sent another one," he said.

"I told her what I knew."

"You did. I heard it."

"Will you tell the next owner?"

"I will tell him what I heard, and what happened to my pot boats. I will not promise him that your step will open."

The younger man tightened the rope around the cleat. He had been waiting while we spoke.

"Will you pay him for this time?" I asked the owner.

"He works with me. We settled his morning before we came."

The younger man glanced up.

"He paid for the rope work," he said. "If we stay to watch another boat, that is another bargain."

The owner looked at him, then at me.

"There. Another person who knows how to ask."

I had expected the cord owner to accuse me before the people on the bank. Instead he began to coil his rope. The salt boat was already beyond the bend. I could not call it back with a better explanation, and I had no right to send it where its owner did not wish to go.

Behind me the keeper had come to the hall door. She had heard enough to tell anyone who asked what I had said. I did not ask her to cut a mark for it. The cord owner could repeat the same words, or remember the lost pot morning more sharply. Both accounts would follow me into the next market.

***

I found Bina two mornings later with her canoe drawn above the wet line. Kito was sitting on a flat stone, picking a thorn from his foot. She had sold the last of her fish and was washing the scales from a basket. When she saw me, she set the basket down.

"I have no answer from Nali today," she said.

"I have not asked for one."

"You are looking at the canoe."

"I wanted to thank you for bringing back her correction."

"You carried my fish. I brought back what I heard because I chose to. We finished that bargain."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

A man stood a little farther up the path with a covered bundle on his shoulder. He had been watching us. He stepped forward before I could answer.

"I am the one who asked Ache to find you," he said. "My brother has two jars to send across. Nali sent away the last man who asked to use her rack path. We want to know whether the upper path can be used."

Bina looked at the bundle.

"Have you already loaded the jars?"

"No. We have learned to ask first."

"Good. Ask Nali."

"We cannot leave the jars and the buyers for a whole day. We were told you carried her words."

"I carried them once. I was already taking Iya home. I have no reason to go across for you today."

"We can pay."

"You can pay for a boat, if its owner agrees. You cannot pay me to say Nali will answer."

"I only want you to ask."

"And if she does not wish to answer me? I will have taken a day from my fish for a question she never asked me to carry."

The man shifted the bundle. Its cover slipped, showing two small woven mats rather than jars. He had brought samples for a buyer, and the jars were still somewhere safe. He had done the thing the pot owner wished he had done. I wanted him to succeed.

"What would make the journey worth your time?" I asked Bina.

She lifted the empty basket and turned it to let the water run out.

"Nothing today. The river is turning. Kito's foot is hurt. I have sold my fish and I am going home by this bank. I will not cross because you have found a man with money."

Kito looked up.

"I could pole," he said, "but I would rather get the thorn out first."

"Then get it out," Bina told him. "You do not owe the man a journey either."

The stranger looked from one of us to the other.

"Who carries the answer, then?"

"No one has agreed to," I said.

He frowned.

"Your keeper has two marks for the crossing. I was told one came from a witness."

"One reminds her what she saw. The other reminds her that Bina reported Nali's correction. Neither is a promise that Bina will travel again."

"Then the marks do not help me."

"They can tell you what not to assume."

"I already know what not to assume. I need to move jars."

Bina returned to her basket. She had given us an answer, and I had nearly made her repeat it until she agreed to something else.

"Will you tell the keeper you refused?" I asked.

"Tell her yourself. You were here when I said it. If you tell her I would not carry anyone's words, you will be wrong. I have carried them. I will not carry these today."

The stranger went up the path with his samples. He had not hired a boat or lost a wage, but he had lost the time he had spent looking for Bina. I watched him go, thinking that our narrow truth had still cost him a morning.

***

At the hall, I told the keeper what had happened. She had the two strips on the shelf, apart from the one for the disputed baskets. She asked whether Bina had refused all future journeys.

"No. She refused this one."

"And the man?"

"He had not loaded the jars. He went to look for another way to ask."

"Then there is nothing to cut for his crossing. There was none."

"I thought we should make it easier for him to know whom to ask."

"Whom can we promise him?"

"Someone willing to hear Nali."

"Name that person."

I had no name. I could tell a stranger to seek Iya, but Iya had paid to cross for her sister and had refused to be my mouth. I could tell him to seek Bina, but Bina had just declined. The keeper watched me look toward the river.

"Can we tell him what was said before?" I asked.

"We can. He must still ask whether the people concerned will say it again. If I mark a journey that never happened, the next man will have one more thing to mistake."

Amaya came to the doorway carrying a narrow bundle. I knew the dark square patch on her old cloth where it showed beneath the cord. She had been to Tesa's house and had brought the old measure with her.

"Tesa sold the length we looked at," she said.

I stood.

"Before the next market?"

"A woman brought wax yesterday. Tesa told us she would sell it if someone paid first. She did."

"I meant to carry her bundles."

"You meant to. You went to hear about pots instead."

"I was going to come when the bundles arrived."

"She did not promise to hold work for you. She promised to tell you what it might count toward if it was there."

The keeper did not move the strips. She had heard me say I would work for cloth, and now she was hearing what that promise had been worth.

"Is there another length?" I asked.

"Not one I have chosen. Tesa has work for a carrier tomorrow if you go before she hires another. She will tell you what she can pay. I will decide whether what you bring is fit for my household."

I had wanted to give Amaya a cloth and close the claim. She had brought me a smaller thing to do.

"Will you go with me?" I asked.

"No. I have shown you the measure. You can carry it to Tesa. I am not going to stand beside you each time you ask for work."

She untied the bundle and laid the old cloth in my hands. Its patched corner was stiff. I could see where the threads had been drawn together by many washings.

"Do not sell this one," she said. "It is mine."

"I would not."

"You have been offering things that are not yours to offer. I want you to hear yourself before you do it again."

She left me with the cloth. I held it until the keeper asked whether I wanted a place to put it. I shook my head and tied it under my arm.

A young woman came into the hall with a reed basket of dried leaves. She wanted to know whether the crossing strip meant that someone had accepted responsibility for Nali's rack. The keeper told her it did not. The young woman said her cousin had heard that Ache would pay for repairs because the crossing had damaged the rack.

"The rack was broken before Ranu crossed," I said. "Nali does not know who broke it."

"Then will you repair it anyway?"

"I have not asked Nali whether she wants my work."

The young woman looked at the cloth beneath my arm.

"People say you have work enough for every shore."

"I have work I have not paid for," I said.

She nodded as though this was the answer she had expected. She took her basket and went out. I wondered which part she would repeat.

***

The keeper called Iya when she saw her at the market that afternoon. Iya had come on her own business, bringing two baskets of dried fish to exchange for rice. She had not crossed for our hearing. She sat only after she had set the baskets where she could watch them.

"I heard Bina refused a journey," Iya said.

"She refused a man who wanted her to ask Nali about jars," I answered.

"That is her choice."

"The man had not hired a boat. He wanted to know whom he could ask."

"Then tell him he may come to Nali without a load and ask her himself, if he can arrange a journey. She may hear him. She may not. I cannot promise for her."

"Could you take his question next time you go home?"

"I could, if I choose. I do not know the man or his jars. I will not promise to make my sister listen."

"Then what can we agree to remember?"

Iya looked at the two strips on the shelf.

"Remember that you heard me say Nali would hear a person who came to ask. Remember also that she has not promised an answer. When someone asks about the crossing, say who saw the journey and who only heard about it."

The keeper took the first strip down.

"I saw Ranu leave this bank and return. I heard you call across that Nali allowed that journey. I did not see which path Ranu took beyond the roots."

She set the strip down and took the second.

"Bina told me she heard Nali name the higher path and refuse the rack path. I did not hear Nali say it. Bina did. I will not tell a stranger I heard it myself."

Iya touched neither strip.

"And if I come with a different account?" she asked.

"I will hear it," the keeper said. "I will ask who was there. I will not cut away Bina's words because you are Nali's sister, nor keep them because Ache likes them."

"Do you like them?" Iya asked me.

"They cost a man a boat's morning. They cost another man his way to sell pots. I wanted the first account to be enough."

"And now?"

"Now I know what Bina heard. I will tell it when I am asked, including the part that sends people away."

Iya picked up her baskets.

"Then I have heard you say that. I can tell Nali if she asks what happened here. I am not agreeing to come again."

"Nor am I asking you to."

She lifted one basket onto her hip and left the other at her feet while she adjusted the strap. I went to help, but she shook her head. A boy she had paid was waiting at the doorway. He took the second basket when she called him, and they went toward the market together.

The keeper put the strips back with space between them. She had not made a new mark. I had not appointed a witness. We had heard one another, and none of us had agreed to more than that.

***

At the landing, the cord owner was speaking to two men I had not seen before. One wore a short cloth stained with the red earth of the upper market. The other carried a measuring cord and a small clay weight. They had come to ask about moving jars and pots when the water rose.

I stopped near enough to hear. The owner saw me and did not lower his voice.

"This bank has people who will tell you why a boat cannot land," he said. "If you want a place ready when you arrive, find the people who will promise the work before you hire your carriers."

The man with the clay weight looked at Raya's closed step.

"Can she mend it?"

"Ask her. She knows the wood. She will not take a promise from Ache for timber that belongs to another house."

"And the watchers?"

"I can hire men for my own boat. Ache has no charge for every boat, and neither do I. If you want someone to wait for your cargo, you must pay that person yourself."

He was telling them what he had learned. He was also telling them why he would no longer risk his name on our landing. I had wanted a witness whose words would travel. Here was a man carrying words I could not stop.

"That is accurate," I said.

The owner turned.

"Which part?"

"All of it. And the woman who carried the correction from Nali did so once. She is not bound to carry the next one."

The man with the red-stained cloth looked between us.

"Then who stands for this landing?"

"Raya judges the step," I said. "Dala answers for her path. The keeper remembers what people said. I can speak for what I have heard and for work I agree to do. No one here can promise the others."

He weighed that answer, then turned to the cord owner.

"You were right. We should speak to the other landing before we bring the jars down."

They went together toward the market. The owner did not ask me to follow. He had lost one pot owner's trust, and he meant to keep the next owner's by taking him somewhere else. I could hear him naming the other channel and the men who worked there.

Raya was still beside her timber. She had turned it over and was marking the rotten edge with a point of charcoal. The tide was coming in. Soon the old stones would disappear.

I went to her.

"Is there work I can do before the water reaches you?" I asked.

"Hold this end while I mark it," she said.

I put my hand where she showed me. The wood was heavier than it looked. She moved the charcoal along the dark line and stopped when she reached sound grain.

"Do not lift it yet," she said.

I kept my hand still. Behind us the cord owner was leading the two men away, and I could not tell whether they would ever return. I had no mark to keep them. Raya finished her line, and only then did she tell me to let go.
