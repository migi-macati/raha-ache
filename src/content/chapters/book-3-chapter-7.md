Amaya did not turn around at once.

She was standing where the hall's shade ended. Beyond her shoulder I could see the basket maker's boy going uphill with an empty carrying pole. The pole knocked against his calf every third step. He had found someone else to help with the jars, or he was still looking. I had promised him a morning and given it to Dala instead.

The woman beside the keeper had asked who would tell me when I had waited too long to pay Amaya. I could hear a boatman calling for a line outside. No one in the hall answered him.

Amaya folded the old cloth over her forearm.

"I will tell him," she said. "I have told him already."

The woman looked at the keeper's shelf, where the new strip lay apart from the others.

"Then what is that for?"

"So he cannot tell me later that he owes a different cloth."

Amaya lifted one corner. The patch was square, sewn with thread a little darker than the weave. I had watched her measure the piece before the keeper cut the mark. I could not say I had misunderstood.

"If he comes to my house with half a cloth," Amaya continued, "I will send him away with it. If he comes with a good one, I will look at it. If he asks again for rice or cord from my stores before he has paid, I will decide whether I can spare it. He cannot make that decision for me."

"I did not ask you to lend more," I said.

"You were going to."

She had not raised her voice. The keeper looked down at the strips.

I had been thinking about the upper step. Raya would have to inspect it before another loaded boat could use it, and the bracing might need new cord. There was cord at Amaya's house. I had not spoken of it yet. That was all the defense I had.

"How many days will you give him?" the neighboring woman asked.

"Why should I give him a day when he has no cloth to bring?"

"Because otherwise he may wait until we have all forgotten."

Amaya came back to the table. She set the old cloth beside the strip without covering it.

"Do you think I will forget?"

"No."

"Then you need not remember for me."

The woman held her gaze. She was shorter than Amaya and had a scar near the base of her thumb, pale against the skin. I had seen her from the water before, working on the opposite shore below a stand of leaning trees. I had never gone to her house. She did not look away.

"I asked because I have come with a thing I was told to forget," she said.

Amaya glanced toward me.

"Then let her speak. I have said what I will do."

"May I ask for a day?" I said.

"You may ask when you have found the cloth. Then I will know what day you mean."

She lifted the old piece again. The neighboring woman stepped aside to let her pass, but Amaya stopped beside her.

"And if you are here when he brings it," Amaya said, "you may come and see whether I accept it. You need not take my word or his."

The woman nodded once. It was not a promise that she would come. Amaya went down the path with her cloth, moving quickly enough that I knew following her would make the matter worse.

The keeper put the strip back on the shelf. She did not cut a new mark for a day that no one had agreed to.

The woman waited until Amaya was out of sight.

"My name is Iya," she said.

I had heard someone call that name from a boat, but I had not known it belonged to her.

"My sister's house is on the bank beyond the narrow bend. We keep a rack there when the water is low. I came yesterday, but your men said you were holding a boat and could not speak."

"They are not my men."

"They said you were the one who would answer."

"That may be true. It does not make them mine."

She looked at me as though deciding whether to spend more words on the distinction.

"Will you hear what happened?"

"Yes."

"And if you hear it, will you make the man who did it come?"

"I can ask him. I cannot make him come from your bank."

"Then I will tell you why I came, and afterward I will decide whether that is worth anything."

She untied a length of fiber from her waist. Inside it lay the end of a thin wooden peg, split along the grain. There was dried mud in the crack and a strand of dark cord caught around the broken tip. She set it on the table carefully, as if the wood might break again.

"What held it?" the keeper asked.

"A rack for drying nets and small fish. My sister's children put the pieces out in the morning. We take them in when the air turns wet."

"Where was the rack?"

"Above the mud on our side. Not in the water."

I picked up the peg. The end had been rubbed smooth by repeated tying. The split was fresh enough that the wood inside was still pale.

"What struck it?"

"A carrying pole, I was told. I did not see the blow."

"Who told you?"

"My sister's daughter. She was bringing water from the house. She heard the rack fall and saw two men with sacks on their shoulders."

"Did she know them?"

"No."

"Then how do you know they came from here?"

Iya put her hand over the peg. I let go.

"I did not say they came from here. I said one told her that your landing had given them leave to bring goods by either shore."

The keeper looked up.

"Who gave them that leave?"

"That is what I have come to ask."

Outside, a line snapped against a boat's side. The keeper went to the doorway and told the caller that the upper step had not been cleared. He answered that he only wanted water. She directed him to the older path and asked him to leave the sounding stake alone. He went away muttering.

Iya listened until the sound of the paddle faded.

"Do you tell everyone where they may land?" she asked.

"Only where we have been asked to keep the way clear," I said.

"Then someone has been speaking more widely than you."

I wanted to ask for the names of the two carriers again. She had already said her niece did not know them. It was easier to look for strangers than to admit someone might have used my name as a reason to cross another household's ground.

"What goods were in the sacks?"

"I do not know. My niece said they were heavy. She saw one man put his sack down when the rack fell. She went to lift it and he told her to keep away."

"Did they pay for the damage?"

"They said the rack was too close to a path."

"Was it?"

"It was close to our path. We had not invited them to use it."

The keeper brought a low stool, but Iya remained standing.

"Why bring the broken peg here instead of going after them?" I asked.

"We did go after them. They had taken their sacks to a boat. The man with the pole said the goods had already been accepted on this side, and they could pass where the water was easiest."

"Who accepted them?"

"He would not say."

Iya lifted the peg by its unbroken end.

"My sister wants the rack repaired. Her daughter wants to know whether the men will come back tomorrow. I want to know who is telling boat people that our path belongs to a promise made in your hall."

I could not see her bank from the doorway. The river turned before it reached the leaning trees. I had passed the place in a boat and thought only of how narrow the current became there. I had never noticed a rack.

"Will your sister come and speak?" I asked.

"She has fish to dry and a broken rack. If she leaves, who will do that work?"

"Then may I come to her?"

Iya gave a small, tired laugh.

"Would you know which house was hers?"

"No."

"Then you would need me to show you. That is another morning I would lose."

The keeper came back to the table.

"Would your niece know the men if she saw them again?"

"Perhaps. She saw their backs and one face. She heard the man with the pole speak. She could tell you what he said better than I can."

"Then we should hear her words before cutting a mark," the keeper said.

Iya examined the shelf. There were several strips there now, each set apart by a bit of cord. None bore a name that she could read by looking at it.

"Is that what you do? Cut wood when someone has lost something?"

"Only when those concerned agree what it is meant to remember."

"My sister has not agreed to anything."

"Then I will not make a mark for her."

Iya pressed the split peg against her palm. The scar below her thumb whitened.

"Good."

I had thought she would be pleased to have her complaint kept beside the others. Instead she was relieved that the keeper had not taken it from her. I wondered how much of the talk about our hall had already reached the opposite bank, and what shape it had taken there.

"We could send someone to ask at the boats," I said. "There were sacks carried through here yesterday. The keeper may remember who paid for a landing."

"I can ask the people who came to the upper path," the keeper said. "I cannot tell you that every sack passed our table. Most do not."

Iya nodded. She had heard the limit plainly.

"Will you say, if anyone asks, that you have given no leave to cross our bank?"

"I will say that," I told her.

"And that you cannot give it?"

"Yes."

She put the peg back in its fiber wrapping, but did not tie the knot.

"Then come to the water and say it where a boat can hear."

The keeper looked at me. I could already imagine men on the landing hearing my voice and believing it reached farther than it did. I wanted to tell Iya that I would have the men brought to the hall and make them answer. I had no names, no boat, and no leave to summon them from her side.

"I can speak from our bank," I said. "Your sister must decide what is said on hers."

"That is what I have been telling you."

She went ahead of me down the path. The keeper stayed behind to mind the hall. At the old landing, Dala had stretched a small net between two stakes. Iya stopped when she saw it.

"Is this the path?"

"It is Dala's ground to use for her work," I said.

"Then why are there footprints across it?"

I looked down. Several bare feet had pressed the mud beside the net, and one heel had cut through a shallow groove that Dala had made to drain the water away. I had used the path myself when I carried the jars. So had other people.

"She gives leave for some crossings," I said.

"Then ask her whether she gave leave for these."

Dala was farther along the bank, mending a mesh with her back to us. She did not look up when I called. Iya stood beside the old post, the broken peg held in both hands. A boat was moving slowly from the far bend toward us. Its bow carried two bundles covered in leaves. The man at the stern lifted his pole and pointed toward our upper step.

Iya watched him. I watched the pale wood of the step, still wet after the last high water. Raya had not yet returned to say it could bear a load.

The boatman called, "They told us we could come through here."

"Who told you?" I called back.

He did not answer at once. He was bringing the boat against the current, and the pole slipped in the mud. He caught it before it fell. The bundles shifted beneath their covers.

Iya took one step toward the water.

"Ask him which bank he means," she said.

The man had heard her. He looked from her to me, then toward the path where Dala's net hung. He kept the bow in the current rather than turning for the step.

I could have told him to come in and explained afterward. The bundles were already here. The man was waiting for a word that would spare him another turn against the water.

Instead I called, "Who owns the goods, and who gave you leave to put them ashore?"

He looked behind him, toward someone sitting low in the boat where I could not see.

Iya held the split peg up so that the pale broken wood caught the light.

"That is what we have come to find out," she said.

The boat remained in the water.

---

The man at the stern drove his pole down again. It went deeper than he expected. His hands slid along the wet wood, and the boat turned broadside before he could free it.

"Keep the bow out," I called.

"I am trying."

A woman rose from behind the bundles. She had been sitting on a low plank, her knees pressed against the covered cargo. The movement of the boat made her put one hand on the gunwale. With the other she held the edge of the leaf covering in place.

"Are you the man at the hall?" she asked.

"I was there."

"Then you know why we have come."

Iya gave me a look. I had not heard of this boat until it appeared around the bend.

"Tell me," I said.

The woman lifted one of the leaves. Beneath it were lengths of split cane tied in flat bundles. Some had been scraped pale; others still carried their green outer skin. They were not heavy like jars, but they took up nearly the whole bottom of the boat. If a bundle shifted across the planks, there was little room for a foot.

"I am Ranu. I am taking these to a maker who has asked to see the finer strips. We came from the bank beyond the bend. They told us we could use this landing."

"Who told you?"

"A man carrying two baskets yesterday. He said the people here were hearing boat claims and would let a load pass if we told them whose it was."

I knew of several men who had carried baskets yesterday. I had been one of them. The description was no help.

"Did he say the step was ready?"

"He said the way had been repaired."

"That is true," I said. "It has not yet been cleared after the high water."

The man at the stern worked his pole free. The boat came nose-first toward the old post. I stepped down, meaning to keep him from tying there, and my heel slipped in the wet groove beside Dala's net. I caught the post with one hand. The wood was rough against my palm.

"Do not tie yet," I said.

He planted the pole again.

"Then where do you want me?"

"Where you can hold without touching the step."

"That is not a place."

Iya moved to the edge of the dry path.

"Who gave you leave on our bank?" she asked Ranu.

Ranu blinked.

"I came from there."

"That is not what I asked."

"The woman whose yard we loaded in knew I was taking the cane."

"Was it hers?"

"No. It is mine. I paid for it."

"Did she give you her path?"

"We used the water path beside her yard."

"Which yard?"

Ranu described a low house with a rack of pots beneath its eaves. Iya shook her head.

"That is above my sister's place. You came through the lower path to reach the water."

"The man with the boat took us there."

The pole-handler pulled the pole up and held it across the stern. He had a strip of cloth bound around his right wrist. The knot was wet and tight.

"That is where we loaded," he said. "I did not see a net rack."

Iya held up the split peg.

"Someone broke one."

He leaned forward to look, but the boat swung away before he could make out the wood.

"I did not carry sacks," he said.

"Did you see men carrying sacks yesterday?"

"I saw men carrying all morning."

"Did one tell a girl that a promise made here gave him leave to cross our path?"

"I heard a man say he could take his load by either bank."

"Which man?"

The pole-handler pointed upriver with his chin.

"Near the boats at the bend. He was speaking to someone else. I did not ask his name."

Ranu turned toward him.

"That is what you told me. You said we could land by either bank."

"I said a man had told me that. I did not say the bank belonged to him."

Ranu stared at him for a moment. Then she sat down on the plank, drawing her feet away from the cane.

"I paid you to bring it here," she said.

"You paid for one journey."

"To this landing."

"To this bank," he said. "I was not paid to carry the cane into a house."

"That was never asked of you."

"You asked where I would put the bundles. I said at the landing, if they let us."

"Who are 'they'?"

He looked at me.

I had been ready to say that we would hear the matter in the hall. Ranu had paid for a boat, not a hearing. The pole-handler was keeping the cargo off the mud while we tried to decide whether a stranger's words had promised something no one could give.

"Who owns the boat?" I asked him.

"Ilan. He has another one upriver."

"Did he tell you to come here?"

"He told me to carry Ranu's cane to the Maynila bank. She chose the landing."

"And if the step is closed?"

"He told me to find a safe place or bring the load back. I will not put his boat against rotten wood."

"Is he paying you to wait?"

"He paid me for the journey. We did not speak of waiting."

Ranu looked from him to me.

"How long before the maker goes to her work? She asked me to bring a sample while the sun was still low."

"Which maker?"

"The one who works baskets beyond the hall."

I knew her. I had left her boy without help with the jars. If she had found someone to carry them, she might already have gone to the potter's yard. I could not promise that she was waiting.

"I can send a child to ask," I said.

"At whose cost?"

I had not expected that question. A child sent on an errand might miss his own work. Ranu was right to ask, though she had asked as if there were a price for everything.

"I can go," I said.

"Then the boat will still be here when you return."

The pole-handler shook his head.

"Not if the water rises further. I can hold it a little while. Then I must take it into the slower channel."

"Can you put the bundles on the old landing?" Ranu asked.

"Not without someone to receive them."

Iya bent to inspect the wet groove where I had slipped. It ran along Dala's net line. There were fresh footprints in it from my own heel and from the people who had passed earlier.

"Nor without Dala's leave," she said.

"Who is Dala?" Ranu asked.

"The woman whose net you can see," I answered.

Ranu leaned to look. The leaf covering slipped from the bundle beside her. She caught it and tied it down with the loose end of a cord.

"I have bought the cane. I have hired the boat. I have come to the bank. Must I now buy a path from someone whose name I did not know?"

"You must ask her whether you may use it."

"And if she says no?"

"Then we look for another way."

"Who is paying for that?"

I had no answer. Iya watched me long enough that I felt she could hear the one I wanted to give: that I would put the matter right, because the landing was mine to make useful. I could not make Dala's ground mine by wanting the boat to stay.

The pole-handler began moving the boat sideways into the quiet water near the old landing. His shoulders tightened under his shirt. Ranu took one bundle in her lap to keep it from sliding.

"Do not let the bottom touch," he said. "There is a stone under the mud."

He was speaking to the boat as much as to us.

Iya stepped back from the water.

"On our bank," she said, "my sister's path was used without her leave. Someone told her daughter the hall had given permission. Here a boat arrives because someone else said either bank could be used. It is a good way for a man to be welcome wherever he wishes to walk."

Ranu looked at the broken peg.

"Do you say we broke that?"

"No."

"Then why show it to us?"

"Because the words that brought you here may be the words that brought them through our rack."

The pole-handler stopped working the pole. For a moment the boat drifted toward the post. He caught the current again before it struck.

"I heard the words," he said. "I did not say they were true."

Iya looked at him.

"Will you say that to my sister?"

"If she comes here."

"She is at work. You have a boat. You could go there."

"I have been paid to come here."

"Then the man who paid you has more claim on your day than the woman whose rack was broken."

Ranu's face changed. She had been looking for a place to unload; now she heard herself accused of buying the right to ignore another household. She set the bundle down carefully.

"I did not pay him to break anyone's rack," she said. "I paid him to carry cane."

"I know," Iya answered.

"Then ask me what I am willing to do before telling me what I have done."

Iya was silent. The two women faced one another across the water, one standing on the bank, the other in a boat that had nowhere to land.

Ranu lifted a short length of cane from the top bundle.

"This is the sample. I can carry it in my hands. I do not need a path wide enough for the whole load. If your sister has a broken rack, I will look at it when I return. But I will not say I owe for it unless I learn who struck it."

Iya studied the cane.

"That is fair," she said. "And I will not say your boat struck it when I have not seen that."

I had been thinking of sending for Raya and Dala. Now I had a smaller question to answer. A woman with a single piece of cane wanted to step onto our bank. She had offered to carry it herself. The pole-handler could bring her to the old landing, but the water had covered the lower stones and Dala's net lay across the shortest path. I could not tell from where I stood whether there was dry ground beyond the post.

"Let me ask Dala," I said.

Ranu looked at the sky.

"How long?"

"I do not know."

She laughed once.

"Then I will decide how long I can wait."

The pole-handler pulled his cloth binding tighter. A narrow red mark showed where the knot had pressed his wrist.

"I can hold until the shadow of that tree reaches the old post," he said. "After that I go to the bend. If she wants to stay, someone else must bring her ashore."

Ranu glanced at the tree. The shadow was already moving over the wet ground.

"Will you take me back if we cannot unload?"

"That is the journey Ilan told me to finish."

"With the cane?"

"With whatever remains in the boat."

She looked down at the bundles. It would cost her a morning to bring them back, and perhaps another to try again. I had thought the new landing would bring work to Maynila. It had brought a woman whose work was already slipping away while I stood beside a post.

Iya wrapped her broken peg and tucked it into her waist. She did not offer to carry Ranu's sample. She had come to ask for an answer, and her own work was waiting across the river.

"Go to Dala," she told me. "I will stay where I can hear what the boatman says."

I started up the path. Behind me Ranu asked the pole-handler to repeat exactly what Ilan had told him before they left. I heard him begin, then lost the words as I passed the hanging net.

Dala was crouched beside a stake, drawing a needle through wet mesh. I stopped before stepping over the line.

"There is a woman in a boat with a piece of cane," I said. "She wants to carry it up to the maker. May she pass here?"

Dala pulled the needle free. She looked toward the water, then at the groove my heel had cut.

"Is she the one who made that?"

"I did."

"Then repair it before you ask me to let someone else through."

She put the needle back through the mesh. The boat waited beyond the post. I could hear the pole-handler counting his breaths as he held it against the current.

---

I knelt beside the groove and put my fingers into the mud.

Dala had cut it to let water run away from the place where she mended her net. My heel had broken one edge. The water no longer followed the narrow channel. It spread beneath the nearest stake and left the cord hanging over a shallow pool.

"Push that back," I said, gathering a handful of mud.

"Where?" Dala asked.

"Against the side."

"Then it will wash into the channel. Take the loose earth out first."

She set her needle on a dry leaf. I dug with my hands while she watched. The mud was warm at the surface and cold beneath it. When I lifted the loose part away, the water found the cut again, but the edge crumbled under my thumb.

"There is a root mat above you," she said. "Do not pull it up. Take the mud beside it and press it against the outer wall."

I reached for the mat anyway, thinking to lay it across the cut.

"Leave it."

I took my hand away. She had made this small channel before the landing work began. I had passed it often without seeing where it carried water. The boat at the old post could not wait while I learned.

"I only need one person to pass," I said.

"You need to mend what you broke."

"I am doing it."

"Then do that."

I worked until the water ran between two walls again. It carried a thin cloud of silt down toward the river. Dala made me take out a stone I had pressed into the lower end.

"It will catch the mesh when I lift the net," she said.

I put the stone on the dry bank. She tested the channel with water from a small bowl, poured slowly at its head. The stream ran clear of the stake. A little still escaped where my heel had cut the side.

"It will settle," I said.

"Perhaps. Do not step there again to find out."

She picked up her needle and resumed the knot she had left unfinished.

"Now may Ranu carry her sample?" I asked.

"From where?"

"The boat."

"That is Raya's question. If she comes onto the path without a load, I can show her where to walk. I have not agreed to a boat scraping against the net, or to men laying cane across it."

I looked toward the river. The pole-handler had moved farther into the slower water. Ranu sat with one knee against the bundles. Iya stood where I had left her, the broken peg in its wrapping at her waist. She was listening to the man in the boat. I could not hear their words.

"Would you come and tell Ranu yourself?" I asked Dala.

"When I have made this knot."

"The boatman gave us until the tree's shadow reached the post."

"Then perhaps you should not have stepped on my channel."

I stayed until she drew the cord tight. She lifted the net to make sure the new knot would not catch on the stake, then stood and washed her hands in the water running from the channel. She did not ask me to help her rise.

We walked down the higher side of the path, leaving the wet strip untouched.

The shadow of the leaning tree had reached the old post. The pole-handler was using both hands now. He held the boat away from the bank with the pole angled beneath his arm, his feet set wide on the stern plank.

"Have you finished?" Ranu called.

"I have asked Dala."

Dala answered before I could add anything.

"You may walk above my nets if you come ashore safely and carry only what you can hold in your hands. The bundles stay in the boat. No one lays a pole or a load on my stakes."

Ranu stood. The boat shifted beneath her.

"I can carry the sample."

"Then you must first find a place to put your foot."

The pole-handler pointed to the upper step.

"There."

"Not until Raya has looked at it," I said.

He shut his mouth and moved the pole back into the current.

"Who is Raya?" Ranu asked.

"The maker who repaired the step."

"Where is she?"

I had no answer. I had not sent for her. She had said she would inspect the work after high water, but she had not promised to come at the hour a stranger needed her.

Iya took the peg from her waist and held it between two fingers.

"On our bank," she said to Ranu, "a man told my niece that a hall across the water had given him leave. I came to find who said it. You have been told a landing is open, and now you are waiting for its maker. If you come again, whose word will you believe?"

"The person who can show me where to land," Ranu said.

The pole-handler laughed once. He was not mocking her. He had spent the morning holding the answer beneath his feet.

A voice called from the path above us.

"Do not put her on that step."

Raya came down carrying a short staff and a shallow basket. A girl followed with a bundle of cord. Raya looked first at the boat, then at the pale edge of the repair. She did not greet me until she had seen where the current ran beneath the old post.

"I was coming to look," she said. "I heard the pole from the upper path."

"Can it take one person?" I asked.

"Let me find out before you offer it."

She handed the basket to the girl and stepped onto the firmer ground above the repair. She pushed the end of her staff into the mud beside the upper edge. The point went in farther than it had when we finished the work. She withdrew it. A narrow pocket of water remained where the staff had been.

"The bank is softer," I said.

"I can see that."

She crouched and reached beneath the step without putting her weight on it. Her fingers came back coated with silt. She felt along the short brace, then pressed it upward with the flat of her hand.

The wood moved.

I had watched her set that piece above the water when we made the repair. It had not moved under the two jars and the basket. Now the edge rose a little and fell again when she released it.

"Is it loose?" I asked.

"It is no longer held where I left it."

"Could we put a stone beneath it?"

"Which stone? And whose hand will hold the wood while you set it?"

I looked at the stones above the path. None was shaped for the hollow she had found. Raya took my silence for an answer and turned to the boat.

"Do not come against this step. Do not put a foot on it. I must take the edge up and see what the water has taken from beneath."

Ranu gripped the gunwale.

"Even for one woman with a piece of cane?"

"Especially for one woman who has not seen it move."

"How long will the work take?"

"I have not opened it yet. I cannot tell you."

Ranu sat down. She kept the sample in her hand.

The pole-handler shifted his weight. The cloth at his wrist had gone dark where the pole rubbed it.

"I cannot stay," he said.

"I know," Ranu answered.

"Then tell me whether to go to the bend."

She looked at me. I wanted to ask Raya whether a board could be laid from the boat to the old path, or whether the girl could take the cane without Ranu leaving the hull. I could see Raya's face as I thought it. She had refused a foot on the step; I was already inventing another way to put someone in the water beside it.

"Is there a dry place farther along?" I asked Dala.

"For a small canoe at the lower water, perhaps. Not for this boat now. The nets are there."

Raya pointed with her staff toward the sounding stake.

"And that is still no place to tie."

The pole-handler took the pole out of the mud and laid it across the stern. For the first time since he arrived, the boat was free to drift. He caught the current with the steering oar before it swung toward the post.

Ranu turned to him.

"If we go to the bend, will you wait there?"

"Until I can put you safely ashore on the side where we began. That is the journey Ilan hired me for."

"And if I ask you to bring me back here later?"

"Then you ask Ilan for another journey. I cannot promise his boat."

She looked down at the cane. One of the finer bundles had begun to loosen where she had lifted the sample. She pulled the cord tight around it. The leaf covering tore at the knot, and she folded the torn edge beneath another leaf.

"I bought this cane for a maker who may be gone by the time I return," she said. "I have lost a morning."

I opened my mouth. She raised her hand.

"Do not offer to pay me with a landing you cannot open."

I had been about to say I would find her another boat. I did not know whose boat was free, or what they would charge, or whether the maker would still be working. I could not even give her the morning back.

"Can I ask the maker when she will see you?" I said.

"You can ask. Do not tell her I have left the cane here."

"I will not."

"Nor that I agreed to come again."

"I will not."

Ranu turned to Iya.

"I have not used your sister's path today. If you want me to hear her account of the rack, I will listen when I come to your bend. I cannot say I owe for what another man broke."

"My sister will decide whether she wants to speak to you," Iya said. "I will tell her what you offered."

The pole-handler had begun to turn the bow. Ranu sat low so the bundles would not shift. The current caught the hull and drew it away from the post. He worked the steering oar once, then twice, until he had room to use the pole again.

"Tell Ilan why you came back," Ranu said.

"I will tell him the step was closed," he answered.

"Tell him it was closed after you brought us."

He looked over his shoulder.

"I will tell him we were not allowed to put ashore. I did not see when the step moved."

Ranu considered that. Then she nodded.

The boat went toward the bend. I watched until the green strips beneath the leaves disappeared behind the trees. The maker had received no sample. Ranu had received no answer about another journey. The pole-handler would be paid for taking her home, if Ilan kept his bargain, and no one had agreed what the waiting had cost him.

Raya was still beside the step. She had placed her staff across the upper path so that anyone coming from the hall would have to see it before reaching the wood.

"Will that stop people?" I asked.

"It will make them look. Someone should stay here until I have lifted the edge."

"I can stay."

"You said you would ask the basket maker about the cane."

"Then I will send someone."

"Who has agreed to go?"

I looked up the path. The girl with Raya's basket was listening.

"Not her," Raya said. "She is carrying my things home when I finish looking."

Dala came to the edge of the repaired channel. She tested the mud once with her toe and stepped back onto the higher ground.

"If you stay here," she told me, "you can keep people from walking over my work while Raya opens the step. That would be useful."

"Will you allow the path when it is safe?"

"For a person carrying a small thing, if I am asked. I have not given you leave for every load that follows."

Iya had been quiet since the boat turned. She looked from Dala's channel to the staff across the path.

"My sister's rack is still broken," she said.

"I know."

"Then I will go back and tell her what happened here. She will hear that the woman with the cane was sent home, and that no one from your hall gave her leave to walk our bank."

"That is true."

"And that you have not found the men who broke our rack."

"That is true too."

She retied the fiber around the peg. The knot took her two attempts because the cord had swollen.

I wanted her to carry a better answer. All she had was a broken piece of wood, a name for the maker, and the sight of a boat turned away. She had come to learn who spoke for her sister's ground. I had been able to say who did not.

Raya lifted the end of the step a finger's breadth with her staff. Water ran from the hollow beneath it and carried a little more mud into Dala's channel. Dala saw it and moved her net farther back.

"Hold that side," Raya said to me. "Only the sound edge. If it slips, let it fall."

I set my hands where she showed me. The wood was heavier than I remembered. Behind us Iya began walking toward the bend, keeping to the higher path and carrying the broken peg herself.

---

Raya told me to keep my hands beneath the sound end of the step.

She had lifted the loose brace out of its hollow. A strip of mud hung from it and fell in pieces against my forearm. The girl who carried Raya's basket stood farther up the path, holding the cord out of the wet. Dala watched the water running toward her channel. Each time Raya moved the wood, Dala moved the net another little distance from it.

"Do not raise your side," Raya said.

"I am holding it."

"You are trying to help me lift. I need it to stay where it is."

I let the weight settle into my palms. The end of the timber pressed a sharp edge against the skin beneath my thumb. Raya put her staff into the hollow and turned it once.

"There is nothing firm enough to take the brace now," she said. "We must leave the step closed until the ground is cleared and the support is set again."

"Can we do it before the next boat?"

"Which boat?"

I had no answer. I had watched Ranu's cane disappear around the bend. Another boat would come whether I named it or not.

A man called from the higher path. He waited while Raya lowered the timber onto the mud and told me to take my hands away. He wore a narrow cloth wrapped over one shoulder and carried a small woven case tied shut with two cords. A boy stood behind him holding the painter of a canoe that had been drawn onto the firmer bank upstream.

"Is this the landing where Ache speaks?" the man asked.

"I am Ache."

"I was told there was a keeper who could show what had been agreed."

"She is at the hall."

"Then I will go there. I have come from the household of Naru on the Tondo side. My name is Salin. Naru sent me to ask what charge your men are taking from boats at the turn."

Dala looked up from her net. Raya kept working. I wiped my hands on my cloth and left a smear of wet earth across it.

"We take no charge from boats passing the turn," I said.

Salin opened the woven case. Inside lay a small piece of cord, doubled and knotted at one end. He held it between his fingers.

"A man came to our house this morning with a load of salt pots. He said two watchers would be paid from every boat using the marked water, and that anyone who refused would have to answer to Ache."

"Who said that?"

"He did not give me the name of the man who told him. He said it was spoken at the Maynila landing."

"That was proposed. It was not agreed."

"Then the salt man heard a proposal and took it home as a demand. He has already sent his boat by the farther channel."

"Was his boat stopped?"

"No. That is why I am asking before another one is."

The boy behind him let the canoe's painter slip through his fingers. The bow moved a little against the bank. He caught the line and drew it back without being told.

"Does Naru own the salt?" I asked.

"No. He stores pots for two households. They wanted to know whether to send the next load by this passage. He asked me to bring back words he could repeat."

"Does he ask you to agree for the other houses?"

"He asked me to listen. They will decide when they hear."

I looked at the wet step. If I had told him we could protect his cargo, the timber beneath my hand would have made a liar of me before the first pot was unloaded.

"Come to the hall," I said. "You can hear what was actually marked."

Salin glanced toward Raya.

"Will the step be open?"

"No," she said without looking up. "I have taken the brace out."

He watched her set the wood above the water.

"Then I will not bring Naru's boat here today."

He followed me up the path. The boy stayed with the canoe. I had intended to return to Raya as soon as the man had heard an answer, but she told me to leave the timber alone. She would need a helper when the ground dried, and she had not asked me to choose one.

The keeper was measuring a length of cord against a blank strip when we entered. She laid both aside. Salin introduced himself and repeated Naru's question without changing it.

"Who says the watchers must be paid by every boat?" she asked.

"The salt man says he was told it here."

"Then he was told something that was never accepted."

She took down the older strip. Its edge had darkened where many hands had touched it. She pointed to the marks for the sounding, the witnesses and the claims still open.

"This was agreed for the marker at the turn," she said. "A passing boat owes no new payment. A boat that unloads at our steps owes only the landing share it agreed to for that use. We have not hired two watchers or set a charge to pay them."

Salin looked at the marks, then at her.

"Can you show me which cut says there is no payment?"

"The cuts are reminders to the people who heard the words. They cannot speak for themselves."

"Then how does Naru know I have brought him the right words?"

"You can repeat what I say. You can ask someone else who was here. The woman who brought her paddle from the far bank heard it. The owner who wanted the charge heard it too."

"Will he say the same?"

"He may still think the charge is needed. He cannot say we agreed to take it."

The keeper had not raised her voice. A man standing outside the doorway stepped in when he heard her. I knew him. He was the owner who had supplied cord for Dala's frame and had argued for the watchers.

"I still think it is needed," he said. "When a marker moves and a boat strikes the mud, whose time is lost? Mine, if I am carrying cargo. The keeper's, if she must hear the complaint. Yours, if you are called from the bank."

"Then who has agreed to pay for watchers?" Salin asked him.

"No one yet. That is the trouble."

"Have you been taking payment?"

"No."

"Will you?"

The owner looked at me.

"If Ache wishes to keep this passage useful, he will have to find a way to pay men to watch it. You cannot ask men to stand in the rain for nothing."

"I have not asked anyone to do that," I said.

"You have asked them to trust the marker."

"We asked people to witness where it stood. That is not the same as hiring them to guard it."

He pressed his lips together. He had brought useful cord when the landing needed it. He also wanted his cargo moved safely. I had no reason to call him a cheat. I could see how a man hearing only the argument might carry away the charge and leave the refusal behind.

Salin turned to him.

"Would you tell Naru's people that a passing boat must pay you?"

"I would tell them the watch is worth paying for."

"That is a different answer."

"Then ask them whether they will help fund it."

"I will tell them you asked. I cannot promise their money."

The keeper laid the strip on the table where both could see it.

"The Maynila owner supplied cord for the frame and a worker for the marker. That was marked. It did not buy him a right to charge the river."

He looked at her sharply.

"I did not say it did."

"Then let Salin hear that from you."

The owner shifted his weight. Outside, a boat knocked against a post farther down the bank.

"I have not collected a new passing charge," he said. "I still want the owners who use the passage to pay for a watch. No one has agreed to it."

Salin repeated the sentence slowly. The keeper nodded. The owner did not seem pleased to hear his own position made so small, but he did not correct it.

I wanted to end the meeting there. Salin put the knotted cord back into his case and asked one more question.

"The salt man also said your men would protect his load if he used this water. Who are your men?"

I thought of the boy holding his canoe, of the pole-handler who had taken Ranu home, of Sulad working for someone else, of the workers who had helped Raya when they had been paid. None belonged to me.

"I have no crew assigned to protect his boat," I said.

"And if another boat blocks the turn?"

"The people using it must find a safe way through. They can bring a claim if the marker or landing work causes a loss. I cannot promise that no boat will block them."

"Does your keeper hear a claim from Naru's household?"

"If they come, she can ask those involved to speak. She cannot make the other household accept her answer."

Salin waited. I could have said more. I could have promised to send a man, to keep the passage clear, to give the salt boats first place. I had no man to send and no right to give first place over another owner's cargo.

"Then we must decide which way to send our pots," he said.

"Yes."

"And if we use the far channel, you will not call that a refusal of your protection?"

"No."

The owner by the doorway made a small sound. He was watching another load leave the marked turn. I had thought he would be pleased that no one was charged for passing. He was counting what the passage could have earned if people trusted it.

Salin looked toward the water.

"I want you to say this where the salt boat's men can hear. Some of them are waiting at the upper bank. They will not come into your hall."

The keeper took up the strip.

"I can bring this, if you want me to say what was marked."

"I do," Salin said.

We went down to the higher landing. Raya had left the loosened step on the bank and set a long piece of wood across the approach so no one would mistake the repair for an open way. Dala had returned her net to the safer side of the channel. The owner followed us, carrying nothing. He stopped before the wet ground.

Salin called to the boy. The boy pushed the canoe into the water and brought it near enough for two men farther upstream to hear. They were waiting with three pots covered in cloth, a small part of the load Salin had described. The rest was already on its way by another channel.

"These are the men Naru sent," Salin said. "They can hear you."

The keeper held the strip up.

"No new charge has been agreed for passing the marker. The share at Maynila is for a landing used by agreement. We have not hired watchers. We have not promised to protect boats beyond the work and witnesses named here."

The two men looked at me. I could feel the owner beside me waiting for the part that would make our passage seem worth their risk.

"She has said what we agreed," I told them. "The step is closed today because Raya has found the support loose. If you pass the turn, you choose your own safe water. We cannot give you leave to use another household's bank."

One of the men asked, "Then what does Ache give us?"

It was a fair question. I looked at the old marker standing in the current. The men who had witnessed its placement were not here. The water had not promised to keep the same depth.

"If you have a loss caused by our marker or our landing work, you may ask us to hear it with witnesses," I said. "You can also decide not to use this way."

He nodded to the other man. They turned their canoe toward the farther channel. The pots moved beneath their covering. They did not wait for a blessing or a charge.

The owner watched them go.

"You have told them why they need not come," he said.

"I have told them what we can answer for."

"That will not mend the step."

"No."

He looked at Raya's barrier and then at the keeper's strip. He did not offer another worker. I did not ask for one while he was angry.

Salin retied the case beneath his arm.

"I will tell Naru's household that no new charge was agreed, that the step is closed, and that the owner still wants a paid watch. I will not tell the other houses that they have accepted your hearing."

"That is what I want you to say," I answered.

"You may want more when they have heard it."

"Perhaps."

He went to the canoe. The boy steadied it while he stepped aboard. Neither man asked to use our steps.

Dala called from her net.

"Tell them also that my path is mine to open."

Salin turned his head.

"I heard you."

"And that it is closed to a load today."

"I heard that too."

He pushed away. The canoe crossed toward the upper bank without touching the marker. I stayed beside the owner, watching the three pots travel in the other direction. The hall had kept its word. The landing had earned nothing that morning, and the loose brace still lay above the water where Raya had put it.

---

The next morning the old stones showed their tops.

I could see three of them from the hall, a dark line below the place where Raya had barred the upper step. The water had gone down in the night. A strip of mud lay between the stones and Dala's path, narrow enough that a man with a basket would have put his foot in her drainage groove. I went down before anyone came to tell me that the landing was open.

Raya was already there. She had brought the short staff and the girl with her basket. The loose brace lay where she had left it, above the water, and the upper timber rested on its sound end. Raya had covered the hollow with leaves so the rain would not carry more earth into it.

"Those stones were used before you built the step," I said.

"They were used when people could reach them."

"Can someone reach them now?"

She put the staff against the lowest stone and pressed. The tip slipped on a skin of mud.

"Ask me after I have looked."

I stayed on the higher path. She went down on her hands, testing each place before moving a knee. The girl held her basket out of the way. Raya scraped the mud from the middle stone with a shell, then put one bare foot on it and shifted her weight slowly. The stone did not move. She stepped back and tested the next.

Dala came with her net folded over one arm.

"I was told a woman would bring cane again," she said.

"She has not agreed to come."

"Then why are you standing in my path?"

I moved onto the dry ground above it.

"I wanted to see whether the stones were sound."

"You have seen Raya seeing it."

Dala put her net down where she could keep it out of the run of water. The channel I had mended yesterday still held, though the outer edge had sunk a little. She pressed a pinch of earth into the gap and went back to her knots.

A canoe came from the bend. Iya was in the bow. Beside her sat an older woman holding a bundle of broken rack slats, tied with a cord that had once been white. The man at the stern brought the canoe against the higher bank without touching our stones. He stepped out and held it there while the women climbed onto dry ground.

"This is my sister Nali," Iya said. "She owns the ground where the rack stood."

Nali looked at Dala's net and at the wood across the upper approach.

"Which is the path you have been telling people to use?"

"I have not told anyone to use yours," I said.

"Then perhaps the people who came through my yard did not hear you."

"I do not know who they heard."

She laid the slats on the ground. One had a deep scrape across its end, as though something heavy had been dragged over it. The split peg Iya had carried yesterday fitted the broken corner, but it did not tell us who had struck it.

"I did not come for you to name a man you have not seen," Nali said. "I came because my sister says you have begun asking people where they may put their feet."

"That is what we have been trying to do."

"Then ask me."

I waited.

"There is a narrow path from my yard to the water," she said. "It passes behind the drying rack. The rack is broken, and the fish must be laid farther uphill. A load carried down that path now would knock against the children working there. I will allow one woman to walk from the yard to the water with a small thing in her hands. No carrying poles. No bundles laid on my ground. The men who broke the rack have not been found, and this permission does not settle what they owe."

I looked at Iya. She had not brought Nali to surrender the path on her behalf.

"Does Ranu know?" I asked.

"She has heard what I am willing to say," Nali answered. "She has not agreed to it."

The man who had brought them pulled the canoe farther up. Iya paid him part of his hire from a small wrapped parcel. He counted the pieces of dried fish before putting them away. He had agreed to carry the sisters home as well, but would not wait below the busy stones. He took the canoe upstream to the quieter landing and said he would be there until the sun stood above the trees.

We walked to the lower stones. Raya was sitting on her heels beside the lowest one. She did not rise when Nali came.

"Are you the maker?" Nali asked.

"I am the one who says whether this step is safe. The step is closed. The stones are older than my work."

"Can a woman stand on them?"

"One at a time, when the water is low enough, if someone keeps her from stepping into the soft edge. I have not tested the mud above the last stone."

Raya stood, went to the top of the old line, and pushed her staff into the ground. The point sank almost to her fist. She drew it out and walked two paces upstream. There the staff stopped sooner. She made me try it myself. My weight on the staff left a round hole but did not make the bank sag.

"That is the way," she said. "No one jumps from the boat. The boat must hold still, and someone on land must take a hand. Do not put a load down there. If the water covers the second stone, the way closes again. I am not leaving the upper step open because this is possible."

Dala came to see the line Raya had marked with three small shells. It passed above her net, though close enough that a careless foot could catch a drying cord.

"One person," Dala said. "With a small thing in her hands. Not a line of people following her because she was allowed."

"Will you stand here?" I asked.

"I have work. If she comes while I am at my net, I will tell her where to walk. If I am not here, she must wait. You cannot borrow my answer for another day."

A voice from the water called my name. It was Ranu, seated in Ilan's boat, with no cane bundles this time. She held a short roll of fine strips in her lap. Ilan himself stood at the stern, and the pole-handler was beside him, his wrist bare now. A red mark remained above the joint.

They did not come against the bank. Ilan kept the bow in the slower water.

"I was told you had a way," Ranu said.

"We are looking at one."

"I did not hire a boat to watch you look again."

Ilan lifted his hand before I could answer.

"I have brought her to hear what is possible. I have not hired my boat for another journey yet."

Ranu turned toward him.

"You said we would settle the price when we saw the bank."

"That is what I am doing."

The pole-handler touched his wrist.

"And I will not hold it for half a morning while you settle."

"Then we should settle before you come closer," Nali said.

Iya stepped to the edge of the higher path and called her sister's terms across the water. She named the yard, the rack and the small thing carried in one woman's hands. Nali repeated the terms herself, louder. Ranu listened without interrupting.

"I can come down with this," Ranu said, lifting the cane. "I will not carry the rest of the load. I will not set it on her ground."

"Nor will you bring a man to carry it for you," Nali said.

"I heard."

Raya told them what she had found at the stones. She named the second stone, the soft edge, and the point at which she would close the path. Ilan looked at the water rather than at me.

"I will not put my hull against the upper step or tie to the sounding stake," he said. "If the water rises or the boat will not hold, I turn back. No one calls that a debt."

Ranu nodded.

"And the journey?" she asked.

"From Nali's water path to these stones, and back to the same place after you have spoken to the basket maker. No cargo beyond the sample. No waiting after the light reaches that leaning tree."

The pole-handler looked at the tree.

"That is still too long if the current begins to run hard. I want my food before we leave Nali's bank, and I will decide whether the boat can hold when we get here. Yesterday I held it for nothing beyond my first journey."

Ranu pressed her lips together. She had paid for yesterday's wasted morning. I saw her looking at the cane as if its weight had grown.

"What food?" she asked.

"A bowl of cooked rice and fish for this journey. Not a promise to feed me when the maker has finished speaking."

"That is more than I expected."

"Then find someone else to hold the pole."

Ilan said nothing. He owned the boat. He had not offered the man's arms with it.

Ranu asked whether a smaller share of fish would do. The man shook his head. At last she agreed to the bowl and fish, to be given at Nali's bank before the boat set out. Ilan named his own hire, a measured portion of rice from Ranu's household, payable on return. She agreed to that separately. I did not offer her rice from Amaya's stores.

The keeper arrived from the hall with two empty strips and no knife. She had heard the voices from above. She stood beside Nali, looking first at the boat and then at the stones.

"Do you want me to hear this?" she asked.

"I do," Ranu said. "But I want to know whose words you will keep."

"Only the words spoken here. I cannot mark an answer for someone who has not heard them."

She asked each person to repeat what they had accepted. Nali gave the one-person path on her bank. Dala gave the one-person path above her net, while she was present and the stones were exposed. Raya gave a conditional judgment about the stones, and kept the upper step closed. Ilan gave one return journey under his own command. The pole-handler gave his work for the food he had named, retaining the right to turn away from unsafe water. Ranu gave her payment and limited her cargo to the sample.

When the keeper asked me what I had promised, I found that I had promised nothing that made the crossing possible.

"I will stand above Dala's path," I said. "If the boat comes, I will help one person from the stones when Raya says the way is safe. I will not ask another worker to leave her work for it."

Dala looked at me.

"And if someone arrives with a bundle?"

"I will tell him this was not leave for a bundle."

"And if he says you promised both banks?"

"I will send him to the people who own the paths."

Nali bent to pick up her rack slats.

"Do not send him through my yard to find me," she said. "He can ask from the water."

The keeper made one mark for the proposed journey, with the witnesses' names spoken aloud. She kept the strip apart from the older ones. Nothing had crossed. No payment had been made. Ranu and Ilan still had to return to Nali's bank, and the pole-handler still had to decide whether the water would let him work.

I had wanted the keeper to carry the terms back to the hall, where people could see them and speak of the crossing as ours. Nali was already turning toward the canoe waiting upstream to carry her home. The agreement had begun on both shores, but it could fail on either one.

The basket maker's boy came down the higher path with an empty jar on his shoulder.

"My mother is at the potter's yard," he said to me. "She will look at a sample before she goes home. She says you can carry the two full jars you left yesterday if you still mean to help."

I looked at the boat. Ranu had heard him.

"Go," she said. "I have paid enough for men to wait for me. I will not pay for you as well."

I told the boy I would come for the jars. When I reached the potter's yard, I would miss the chance to watch the first boat approach. I could still carry what I had promised. The keeper could stand at the stones without pretending that she owned either bank.

Ranu told Ilan to turn the boat. The pole-handler planted his pole and brought the bow around. They went toward Nali's path to make the payment before beginning the journey we had only spoken about.

---

The first jar was heavier than the boy had said.

He walked ahead of me with the empty one, turning at every narrow place to see whether I was still behind him. I had promised his mother I would carry two full jars from the potter's yard. I could balance one against my hip, but when I tried to take the second by its neck the first slipped against my ribs. The potter gave me a length of fiber to bind them to a carrying pole.

"Put the knots below the shoulders," she said. "If you tie them at the neck, they will strike one another."

I did as she showed me. The pole bent under the weight. I had spent so much of the morning telling other people where to place their feet that I nearly broke a jar by placing mine badly on the dry path.

The basket maker was scraping the inside of a narrow strip when we reached her work shade. She did not stop for us. Her boy set down the empty jar, and I lowered the pole until the full jars rested on folded leaves.

"Yesterday you said you would come," she told me.

"I did."

"Then I found another carrier."

"I know."

"He wanted a share of the rice I had meant for my children. I paid him because the jars were ready and I could not leave them at the potter's."

I looked at the jars I had brought. I had made yesterday cost her more than a missed hour.

"Do you want me to pay him back?" I asked.

"He has been paid. I want you to stop offering work before you know whether you will do it."

She pulled a thin shaving from the strip and laid it beside the others.

"A woman is bringing cane for you to inspect," I said.

"So my boy tells me."

"She has hired a boat for one return journey. She may come by the old stones if the water stays low."

"Then bring her here if she lands. I will not leave my work to meet a boat that may turn away."

I told her I would not promise the woman a purchase. The maker lifted the strip she had been working.

"You do not own my hands either," she said.

I left the pole at her shade with her permission and went down toward the river. The keeper was standing beside the shells Raya had set along the safe part of the bank. Dala sat farther uphill with her net. The upper step remained barred, and the loosened brace was still on the dry ground.

No boat had come.

"Did they refuse?" I asked.

"They have not crossed," the keeper said. "That is all I know."

Raya had her staff in the water. She was watching a line of small bubbles run past the lowest stone.

"Look there," she said.

A current I had not noticed was drawing along the bank and then turning sharply into the channel. A boat approaching the stones would have to hold its bow upstream to keep its side clear. The water was low enough to show the stone tops, but the narrow turn had grown stronger.

"Will it be safe?" I asked.

"For whom?"

"For Ranu."

"If Ilan cannot hold his boat, it will not matter what I say about the stones."

A call came from the far bank. Iya stood beside Nali's path, one hand lifted. Behind her I could see Ranu holding the cane sample, and Ilan with his boat. The pole-handler was eating from a bowl. He did not hurry when Ranu called to him.

The keeper raised the new strip. Iya held up her empty hands.

"They have paid him," she called. "Nali has let Ranu walk to the water. Ilan says the current is stronger here than it was yesterday."

Raya told the keeper to answer that the upper step was still closed and the old stones could be used only while exposed. The keeper called the words across. Iya repeated them to Nali, though Nali stood close enough to hear. Then Ilan pushed his boat away from the far bank to test the water without a passenger.

The pole-handler put his bowl aside and took the pole. Ilan held the steering oar. They brought the boat halfway across, then stopped in the current. The bow began to turn. The pole-handler planted his pole, felt it slide, and lifted it clear before it caught beneath the hull.

"Back," Ilan said.

The two men worked the boat toward Nali's bank. I had opened my mouth to call them on. Raya caught my wrist.

"Do you see the water between them and the stones?"

I saw it then: a seam where the smooth surface broke into a rougher strip. The boat had turned just before reaching it.

"They may try again," I said.

"They may decide not to."

Ranu was standing above the far bank now. I could see her speaking to Ilan, though I could not hear the words. Nali stayed on her own path, watching the men take the boat farther upstream. They did not bring it against the rack.

The keeper asked whether she should put a cut on the strip for the attempt. I shook my head.

"No one crossed."

"Then I will leave the strip as it is."

We waited. Dala went back to her net. Raya moved the shells a hand's breadth farther from the soft edge and told me not to stand between them and the water. I had been standing there to show the boat where to come. I was making a person on shore look more useful than the current allowed.

The boat appeared again above the turn. This time Ilan let it drift down with its bow held upstream, instead of forcing it straight toward the stones. The pole-handler used the pole only when the bottom rose beneath him. The boat came sideways into the slower water below us and stopped without striking the bank.

Ranu sat in the middle, the cane in both hands. She looked toward Raya.

"May I come?"

Raya watched the second stone. Water ran over its outer edge, leaving the middle dry.

"One foot at a time. Wait until the boat holds. Ache, stay above the soft place."

I moved to the point she had marked. Ilan held the steering oar. The pole-handler planted his pole well away from the stone. Ranu stood slowly, letting the boat's movement settle before she shifted her weight. She put one foot onto the lowest stone and then drew the other after it. The pole-handler kept his hands on the boat. I offered mine only when she reached the top stone.

She took it. Her fingers were cold from holding the wet cane.

I felt the urge to lift her the last distance. Raya had told me where the bank was sound. I waited while Ranu found her own footing and stepped onto it.

Dala called from above.

"Do not cross my channel."

Ranu looked at the line of shells, then at Dala. She followed the higher path without touching the net stakes. The sample remained in her hands. She had brought no other cargo.

The keeper stood aside to let her pass.

"Will you mark that I came?" Ranu asked.

"I can mark that we saw you land," the keeper said. "I cannot mark that the maker bought your cane."

"I did not ask you to."

I went with Ranu to the work shade. The basket maker was still scraping strips. She took the sample and bent one pale length around her thumb. It held. She bent another, and it split along a dark line.

"That part was cut too near the joint," she said.

Ranu leaned forward.

"Can you use the rest?"

"I can try it for the narrow edge of a basket. I will not buy a boatload because one strip bends."

"I brought only this because I was told to."

"Then you were told well."

The maker separated three pieces and laid the others back in Ranu's hands.

"I will take these for a trial. If they hold after drying, send someone to ask what I will pay for more. Do not bring a load until I have answered."

Ranu looked at the three pieces. She had paid for a boat and a pole-handler to carry a sample, and the maker had kept only a part of it.

"Will you pay for those now?"

"I will pay for what I have taken," the maker said. She brought out a small measure of rice, counted it into Ranu's cloth, and waited until Ranu accepted it. "That buys these pieces. It does not buy another journey."

Ranu tied the cloth. The rice would not cover the hire she had agreed to. She looked at me.

"You could have said the maker was interested," she said.

"I thought she was."

"That is why I asked her instead."

She thanked the maker and turned back toward the river. I followed her, carrying nothing. I had been eager for the first successful passage to bring a trade worth telling people about. It had brought three pieces of cane into one woman's hands and a small payment into another's. That was all either had agreed.

The water had begun to cover more of the lowest stone. Ilan saw it before I did.

"We go now," he called.

Ranu asked Dala whether she could use the path again. Dala looked up from her net and said yes, for the same one woman with the same small thing. Raya tested the top stone with her staff. The keeper held the strip but did not speak for either of them.

Ranu went down slowly. I kept my hand ready without pulling her. She stepped into Ilan's boat, sat where he directed, and put the rice parcel between her feet. The pole-handler waited until her hands were free before he pushed away.

"Is the journey finished?" the keeper called.

"Not until we reach Nali's bank," Ilan answered.

He turned the boat into the current. The pole-handler used the pole twice and then laid it across the stern. They went upriver a little before crossing, as they had done on the way over. Iya waited on the far bank, with Nali above her.

I could see Ranu rise when the boat came to their landing. Nali held back the children who had come down to watch. Ranu stepped onto the narrow path with her rice parcel. She did not go near the broken rack. Ilan and the pole-handler brought the boat clear of the bank and held it while she passed.

Only then did I look at the keeper.

"Now you can mark it."

"I can mark that I saw her return to Nali's ground," she said. "I did not hear what they said when she reached it."

Across the water Iya raised one hand. She called that Ranu had returned by the permitted path and had not laid down a bundle. Nali spoke after her. The words were lost in the wind, and Iya repeated them more loudly: the path was open for this journey alone; the rack remained broken.

The keeper repeated those words to me before she cut the strip.

"Do you trust Iya?" I asked.

"I heard her. Nali heard her too. Tomorrow Nali may ask whether I have marked the same thing. How will she know?"

The strip lay in her palm. The river was wider than the distance across the hall, but no one could make the cuts speak over it.

Amaya came down the path while we were still looking at the far bank. She had heard that the first passage had succeeded. She looked at the barred upper step, at Dala's net, and at the empty water between the stones and Nali's yard.

"Did you help them?" she asked me.

"I helped Ranu ashore. Raya said where."

"And the cloth?"

"I have not found it."

"I did not think the boat would bring it."

She took a folded piece of cloth from beneath her arm, the old measure with the darker square patch. She held it beside the keeper's new strip, making sure I saw the difference.

"Tomorrow I am going to the woman who has cloth above the market path. You will come and see what she will accept in exchange for one of this measure. If she will not give it to you, I will not pretend the debt has been paid. If you do not come, I will bring a witness to your house and ask you again."

"I will come," I said.

"You have promised to carry jars before."

"I carried them this morning."

"Then you know the way to finish a promise."

She did not ask the keeper to cut a mark for my answer. She had not accepted a payment or set a date for one. She had told me where she intended to go and what she would do if I failed to appear. I could not make that obligation smaller by saying I had helped a woman cross the river.

Amaya went back toward the hall. The keeper set the new crossing strip beside the older claims, keeping the cloth debt separate. Dala was still mending her net. Raya was still guarding the closed step. On the far bank Nali and Iya carried the broken slats uphill between them.

I watched them until the path turned behind the trees. The keeper could tell people on our bank what she had heard. Iya could tell Nali what we had said. Neither woman had promised to carry the other's words forever. If someone changed a phrase between the banks tomorrow, the cut strip would not go across the water to correct it.

"Who will take this to Nali?" I asked the keeper.

She held the strip against her palm.

"Ask who is willing to go," she said.

I looked at the river again. Ilan's boat was already moving toward other work.

<summary>Historical Notes &amp; Sources</summary>
<p><strong>Fictional crossing and witnesses.</strong> Ache's hearing, Amaya's cloth claim, Iya and Nali's broken rack, Dala's net path, Raya's closed step, Ranu's cane sample, Ilan's boat, and the keeper's marked strip are inventions for this novel. No known early-sixteenth-century source documents these named people, the single return crossing, the agreement between the two banks, or a Maynila system of landing permissions. The distinction between each household's consent and a general right to pass is a narrative choice, not an attested legal rule.</p>
<p><strong>Regional commerce and physical setting.</strong> Tomé Pires's <em>Suma Oriental</em> (composed c.1512–1515) supplies contemporary context for Luzon, Borneo and Malacca commerce, but not the individual goods, payments or dialogue here. See the <a href="https://asj.upd.edu.ph/mediabox/archive/Vol15%2CApr-Aug-Dec1977.pdf"><em>Asian Studies</em> discussion of Pires</a>. The Pasig River's connection between Manila Bay and Laguna de Bay makes changing water levels and river traffic plausible; the exact stones, current, tidal hour and early-1520s shoreline in these scenes are fictional. No modern hydrological measurement is used as a historical tide table.</p>
<p><strong>Records, testimony and later descriptions.</strong> The <a href="https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol70/iss2/2/">Laguna Copperplate Inscription</a> (900 CE; see Clavé and Griffiths, 2022) demonstrates written debt obligations in the wider region centuries before the story, but does not attest this keeper's bamboo marks. Juan de Plasencia's <a href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Philippine_Islands%2C_1493%E2%80%931898/Volume_7/Documents_of_1589"><em>Customs of the Tagalogs</em></a> (1589) is later, colonial-context testimony; it cannot determine the rights or wages of these fictional early-1520s workers. Original Portuguese and Spanish passages have not been collated for this chapter.</p>
