The trouble with being heir to Maynila was that everyone knew where to find me.

I had a solution. I intended to be on the river before they started looking.

Sulad had objections.

“You'll be late,” he said, pushing our little outrigger clear of the mud.

“You said that yesterday.”

“And yesterday you were late.”

“Only because you kept talking.”

He climbed aboard and settled his paddle across his knees. Sulad was my age, though his broad shoulders and the dark markings on his arms made him look older. My mother had entrusted him with keeping me safe. He took this duty seriously enough for both of us.

“Before the second call from the hall,” he said. “We must be back.”

“We will be.”

“You said that yesterday too.”

I raised the patched sail. The wind caught it, and our boat moved away from the landing.

Maynila was waking. Smoke drifted over the houses, and men called from boats clustered near the mouth of the Pasig. A woman on the bank was arguing with a porter over a basket of fish. Farther downstream, trading vessels waited to unload jars, cloth, and bundles whose owners seemed more anxious about the tide than the weather.

I preferred the river to the hall. In the hall, men bowed before I had done anything. The river never bowed. If I wanted a boat to go where I intended, I had to understand the current and the wind.

That seemed a fairer arrangement.

Sulad pointed upstream. “There's your post.”

A weathered timber stood beyond the busiest landings. We had made a wager: reach it, turn, and return before the second call. If I succeeded, Sulad owed me the rice cakes he had been saving. If I failed, I owed him three mornings of spear practice.

“We should turn now,” he said.

“We haven't reached it.”

“Exactly.”

I tightened the sheet. Our boat leapt forward.

At the post I eased the sail, brought the bow around, and caught the current downstream. Sulad steadied us with his paddle.

“Beautiful,” I said.

“It was a turn.”

“A beautiful turn.”

“I'll agree when we're home.”

Then someone shouted from the southern landing.

I ignored the first cry. Men shouted all morning on the river. The second made Sulad stand.

A cargo boat had broken loose above the quay. It rode low, carrying earthen jars and bundled cloth. Two men paddled desperately while a third struggled with a steering oar that hung broken against the hull. The current had caught them broadside.

Ahead stood a row of bamboo fishing stakes. Beyond them lay moored vessels too heavy to move quickly.

A child aboard the cargo boat clung to a bundle. She watched the stakes approach without making a sound.

“They'll strike,” I said.

“Yes.”

“The jars will shift. They could overturn.”

“I see it, Ache.”

Our boat was small enough to cross their path. We had a line. If we caught their bow and pulled it around, perhaps the current would carry them toward the bank.

I explained this to Sulad.

“Our line is too short,” he said.

“We can reach them.”

“And then? Their boat weighs several times ours.”

“With the wind.”

He glanced at our sail. “Lower it before the line tightens.”

“I know.”

“Then do it.”

I changed course.

The men on the cargo boat were shouting now. One demanded they throw the jars overboard. Another called for a pole. The child saw us coming and raised her head.

We came alongside. I threw our line. A man caught it on the second try and secured it to a crossbeam.

I hauled.

Nothing happened. Then the weight of the larger boat dragged our bow sideways, so sharply that the rope burned through my fingers.

“Let go!” Sulad shouted.

I did. The line ran overboard. The cargo boat drifted on.

My clever plan had lasted less time than it took to explain.

“We should leave it to the men ashore,” Sulad said.

I looked at the child. “Not yet.”

There was a stout mooring post on the quay. If we could bring the line to the men waiting there, they might turn the cargo boat without using our little craft as an anchor.

Sulad studied the distance. “That could work. But the sail stays down.”

I nodded. He did not look convinced.

We recovered the loose line, paddled toward the quay, and called for help. Men looked up from their work. Then one recognized me.

“The young raha!”

They ran. A porter abandoned a stack of bundles. Two fishermen dragged their gear aside to clear the landing.

I threw the rope. The coil opened badly and struck the edge of the quay. A man lunged, caught the wet end, and passed it around the post.

The line tightened. The cargo boat began to turn.

For a moment I thought we had done it perfectly.

Then the sail, which I had failed to lower completely, filled with a sudden gust. Our bow swung into the tightening rope. Sulad shoved with his paddle, but the outrigger struck a submerged stake.

Wood cracked beneath us.

Water splashed over my feet. A length of our outrigger floated away.

The cargo boat hit the quay. Several jars shattered. Men shouted and hauled at the rope.

A rower reached for the quay to steady himself. The hull struck before he could pull his hand free. He cried out and stumbled back, clutching his wrist against his chest.

But the hull stayed upright.

Someone lifted the child ashore. She began crying as soon as her feet touched the ground.

I had never been so pleased to hear someone cry.

The injured rower sat against a piling. Another man tried to examine his hand. He would not let anyone touch it. I looked at him, then at the girl, and told myself that the boat had been about to overturn.

Sulad sat in the water collecting inside our boat.

“Three mornings,” he said.

“I saved a boat.”

“You broke ours.”

“A smaller boat.”

“Still ours.”

We reached the landing with two men pulling us by rope and Sulad bailing with a bowl.

I stepped ashore expecting thanks. Instead, a fisherman was waiting beside a heap of torn netting.

“Who ordered my stakes pulled up?”

I looked past him. The men who had helped us had dragged part of his fishing gear out of the way.

“They were blocking the landing,” I said.

“They were there before that boat broke loose.”

A porter pushed forward. “My bundles are still on the quay. I'll miss my passage.”

The cargo boat's owner arrived, dripping water, and pointed at his broken jars.

“You saved my daughter,” he said. He looked toward the rower, still sitting by the piling. “I thank you for that. But who pays for these? And what happens if he cannot row?”

I had three men demanding answers, an injured rower who had said nothing, and only one answer: the child was alive.

It seemed enough to me. No one else seemed willing to call it enough.

I summoned the harbor steward. He arrived with a tally board and the expression of a man whose morning had been made unnecessarily expensive.

“Have the damages counted,” I told him. “I'll settle them.”

“From which store, my lord?”

“Mine.”

He looked at our broken boat. “And the repairs?”

“Mine too.”

“And the porter's missed passage?”

“I said I'll settle it.”

He lowered the tally board. “I will have to tell your mother.”

That was when I knew we would miss the second call.

* * *

My mother did not raise her voice.

Other people shouted when angry. She made them explain themselves until they wished she would shout.

We stood before her in the hall, Sulad still smelling of river mud. The steward's tally lay on the floor. Someone had brought the broken steering oar as evidence, although I had already described it twice.

“Tell me what happened,” she said.

“I stopped a cargo boat from overturning.”

“That is what the steward says. Tell me what you did.”

I told her about the line, the current, the post, and the collision. I left out the part where Sulad had warned me about the sail.

She turned to him. “Is that all?”

He looked at me.

“Mostly, my lady.”

She examined the tally. “You ordered men away from their work.”

“They were closest.”

“Did you know what they were carrying?”

“No.”

“Did you know who owned the fishing stakes?”

“No.”

“Did you know who would repair the boat you borrowed?”

I looked down.

“And the rower?”

“He would have been hurt if the boat overturned.”

“Perhaps. Was he hurt when you turned it into the quay?”

I stared at her. It was not a question I wanted her to ask in front of Sulad.

“I was trying to save them.”

“I know what you were trying to do.”

“Your father left you a claim to Maynila,” she said. “He did not leave you a separate river.”

I had never known my father. I knew him through the empty place beside her at ceremonies, and through older men who studied my face before they spoke.

“I didn't say the river was mine.”

“No. You gave orders as though everyone on it had been waiting for you.”

“I saved the child.”

“Yes,” she said. “You did.”

I waited for the rest.

“Tomorrow the fisherman, the porter, the boat owner, and someone who can speak for the injured man will come here. You will hear their claims and decide what can be settled.”

“I have training tomorrow.”

“Then your training will begin here.”

Beside me, Sulad lowered his head. I suspected he was smiling.

Outside the hall, the afternoon heat pressed against us. From the landing I could see our broken outrigger laid on the bank, surrounded by men discussing its repair.

“Three mornings,” Sulad said.

“We never finished the wager.”

“We didn't return before the second call.”

“Because we stopped to help.”

“Because you forgot the sail.”

I wanted to argue. I had several excellent arguments, beginning with the child and ending with the fact that we had, technically, rounded the post.

Instead I asked, “Would you have left them?”

He thought about it.

“No,” he said. “But I'd have listened when someone told me to lower the sail.”

At the landing the fisherman was mending his net. He began to rise when he saw me.

“Stay,” I said. “Show me where it tore.”

He spread the wet net across his knees. There were more broken knots than I had expected.

Sulad crouched beside us without a word.

I had won the wager around the post, if anyone still cared to count it.

Sulad never asked for his rice cakes.
