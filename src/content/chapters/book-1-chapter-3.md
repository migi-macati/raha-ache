My mother responded to my plan to investigate Tondo by sending me upriver with an old woman.

I considered this unfair.

“I said I wanted to find the men collecting in Lakandula’s name,” I told Sulad as we pushed away from the landing.

“And your mother said no.”

“She said not yet.”

“She said, ‘You will not go looking for a quarrel across the river.’”

“That means not yet.”

Sulad dipped his paddle. “It sounded like no.”

At the bow, Ula laughed.

She had heard everything.

Ula was small enough that I had once wondered how she managed to frighten adults. Then I watched a grown fisherman apologize to her because he had interrupted while she was speaking, and I stopped wondering.

Her hair had gone mostly silver. Around one wrist she wore cords, beads, and small pieces of shell. None of them looked valuable. People still noticed when she touched them.

“My mother sent me because she thinks you will keep me away from trouble,” I said.

“Your mother sent you because I asked for you.”

I nearly dropped my paddle.

“Why?”

“Because you are difficult.”

Sulad coughed into his shoulder.

I pointed at him. “He is enjoying this too much.”

“He usually does.”

Ula sat beneath the small woven shade we had rigged over the center of the boat, carrying a basket I had not been allowed to inspect. We were heading toward a settlement farther up the Pasig, where people from Namayan and nearby communities had gathered before several boats left for the lake and the routes beyond it.

My mother had called the journey an obligation.

I called it exile within exile.

For three days I had studied the landing tallies, and found more collections made by men who claimed authority from Tondo, Maynila, or both. Some names repeated. Some did not. Some payments had no clear collector at all.

I wanted to follow the money.

My mother wanted me to accompany Ula.

“The men will still be there tomorrow,” she had said.

“They may not.”

“Then if they can disappear in one day, they were not the whole problem.”

I hated it when she said things that became more annoying after I thought about them.

So I paddled.

The river narrowed as we moved inland. Houses stood close to the water in some places and disappeared behind trees in others. We passed fishermen checking traps, children swimming beside a landing, women rinsing vessels at the edge of the bank. A trader moving downstream called a greeting to Ula before he noticed me.

He bowed only after she had answered him.

I pretended not to notice.

Sulad noticed me pretending.

“You should wear shells,” he said. “Then people might greet you first.”

“I have silver.”

“You sold it.”

“I had more than one piece.”

“Then why are we repairing our own boat?”

“Because some people have no respect for rank.”

Ula turned. “The river has excellent manners. It drowns everyone equally.”

I decided not to speak for a while.

That was when I noticed the cord around Sulad’s neck.

I had seen it before, but usually tucked beneath his cloth. A small dark object hung from it, smooth from years of touching.

“What is that?” I asked.

He covered it with his hand.

“You know what it is.”

“I know you hide it whenever my mother’s Bruneian relatives visit.”

“I hide many things when important people visit.”

“That is not an answer.”

He looked toward Ula.

She said, “Ask him, not me.”

Sulad sighed. “My mother gave it to me.”

“For protection?”

“If you want to call it that.”

I looked at the object again.

“You believe it protects you?”

“I believe my mother wanted me to carry it.”

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

I waited.

He paddled.

“That is all?”

“That is all.”

It was unsatisfying. I had been taught that protection came from God, from judgment, from people, from a well-made hull, from knowing when not to sail. The little object seemed unnecessary.

Then I remembered that I still carried the strip of cloth from my father among my things.

I changed the subject.

* * *

The gathering had already begun when we arrived.

No one called it a ceremony.

That was the first thing I noticed.

People were repairing nets, loading baskets, checking lashings, arguing about weather, and carrying water to the boats. Children ran between adults until someone shouted at them. Near the largest house, Ula’s basket joined bowls, cloth, leaves, oil, and other things brought by different families.

Some people bowed their heads while Ula spoke.

Others did not.

A man from one of the boats touched his forehead, then the wood of his vessel.

A woman beside him whispered words I did not understand.

Sulad stood quietly.

I stayed near the edge.

Ula noticed.

Of course she did.

“You may come closer without agreeing with everyone,” she said.

Several people looked at me.

“I did not say I disagreed.”

“You walked far enough away to say it with your feet.”

Sulad turned his face so I would not see him smile.

I stepped forward.

Ula did not ask me to repeat words. She did not ask me to touch anything. She spoke to the families about the journey, about people waiting for them to return, about promises made before leaving.

It sounded less like magic than I had expected.

When she finished, one of the older men asked me to say something.

“Why me?”

He blinked.

Because I was Ache. That was the answer.

I suddenly understood how ridiculous the question had sounded.

I looked at the boats. Most were smaller than the trading vessels at Maynila, but they were well kept. Every crew had already checked what mattered. Ropes. Water. Food. Paddles. Hull.

I thought about the cargo boat I had nearly overturned while saving it.

“Do not trust a line until you know where both ends are tied,” I said.

Sulad closed his eyes.

The older man waited.

“That is all?” Ula asked.

“It is good advice.”

“It is advice about rope.”

“They are going on boats.”

A few people laughed.

I could feel my face grow hot.

So I tried again.

“Come back,” I said. “Whatever you believe is waiting on the water, come back to the people waiting here.”

No one laughed that time.

Ula nodded once.

It bothered me how much I wanted her approval.

* * *

After the boats left, we ate beneath the house with the families who remained.

There was rice, fish, fruit, and an argument between two old women about whose grandson had loaded the wrong basket. I understood this world. It sounded like Maynila.

Then a boy came running from the river.

“Boat from the west!”

Everyone moved.

A larger vessel was making its way upstream, broader than the local craft and carrying men whose clothing marked them as foreigners before I heard them speak.

Malay.

Not the language of home, though I had heard it often enough from traders and from my mother’s Bruneian kin. I knew greetings. Titles. Numbers. Useful phrases.

Enough, I believed, to impress Sulad.

The captain stepped ashore and greeted the headman.

I answered before anyone asked me to.

His eyes moved to me.

Then he said something much faster.

I caught perhaps one word in three.

Sulad’s expression became unbearable.

“Slowly,” I said in Malay.

The captain smiled.

This time he spoke as if addressing a child.

I understood almost everything.

That was worse.

He knew my mother’s family.

Not closely, he explained. He had carried goods through Brunei and had served men who served others connected to the court. The relationships became complicated enough that I stopped pretending I followed every name.

But when he heard who I was, his face changed.

“Ache?”

I straightened.

He repeated my name, then asked about my mother.

I answered carefully.

He asked about Siripada.

“My grandfather,” I said.

The captain nodded.

The word landed differently when spoken by someone who had seen Brunei.

Siripada had always existed in the same part of my mind as stories about far islands, great markets, and storms so large they swallowed fleets. Real, probably. Distant, certainly.

“You have seen him?” I asked.

The captain gave a cautious answer. He had seen the court. He had seen men of the ruler’s household. He did not claim intimacy he did not possess.

That caution made me believe him more.

“What is Brunei like?”

Ula sat beside me.

The captain looked at the river before answering.

“Busy.”

“That is not enough.”

He laughed.

He spoke of water crowded with boats. Of traders arriving from places I knew only by names. Of audiences where a man might wait for someone important enough to speak to someone else important enough to carry his words farther. Of houses and storehouses raised where water shaped the rhythm of movement.

He did not describe a paradise.

He complained about prices.

That made it real.

“Could I go there?” I asked.

Sulad looked at me sharply.

The captain’s smile faded into something more careful.

“You could travel there.”

“I mean to the court.”

“If your mother’s kin receive you.”

“They would.”

He tilted his head.

I heard my own certainty.

“My grandfather is Siripada.”

“Yes.”

“So I would be received.”

“As grandson.”

I waited.

He waited too.

“And?”

“And a grandson is not a fleet.”

Ula laughed so loudly that two women turned around.

I felt betrayed.

The captain went on.

“You speak some Malay.”

“I speak enough.”

“You speak enough to ask me to slow down.”

Sulad coughed again.

I was beginning to hate his cough.

“I can learn.”

“Yes.”

The captain said it without mockery.

That was somehow more serious.

“You can learn.”

I looked downriver, though Brunei was not downriver in any simple way. It lay beyond routes I had never sailed, beyond waters that existed in my head only because other men had returned from them.

For the first time, I did not imagine Brunei as the place where my grandfather would solve Maynila for me.

I imagined arriving there and being the one who did not know where to stand.

* * *

On the return journey, Sulad paddled for a long time before he spoke.

“You want to go.”

“I asked a question.”

“You ask questions with your whole body.”

“That means nothing.”

“You leaned so far toward him I thought you would fall into his lap.”

Ula made an approving sound from beneath the shade.

“I hate traveling with both of you.”

“No,” she said. “You hate being observed accurately.”

We passed a small landing where a family had placed food beside a post. I looked away before Ula could see me looking.

She saw.

“Say it,” she said.

“What?”

“The argument inside your head.”

“There is no argument.”

“Then it must be very crowded in there for another reason.”

Sulad laughed.

I stopped paddling.

“My mother’s family follows Islam.”

Ula waited.

“So do I.”

“I know.”

“Some of what people did today…”

“Was not what your mother’s Bruneian relatives would teach you.”

“Yes.”

“Did I ask you to do it?”

“No.”

“Did anyone?”

“No.”

“Then what is the problem?”

I looked at Sulad’s cord.

He saw me looking and tucked it under his cloth.

“That,” I said.

Sulad’s face hardened.

Ula’s did not.

“You think if you become ruler,” she said, “you will decide what everyone carries around their neck?”

“I did not say that.”

“You are thinking very loudly today.”

“I am thinking that people cannot believe opposite things and both be right.”

“Perhaps.”

I had expected resistance. The answer stopped me.

Ula looked toward the bank.

“But a ruler who waits for everyone to agree before he protects them will rule an empty shore.”

“That is politics, not belief.”

“Do you think people separate themselves so neatly?”

I did not answer.

She touched the cords around her wrist.

“I laughed when the captain corrected your Malay,” she said. “I enjoyed it more than I should have.”

“I noticed,” I said.

“You do not have to ask these things for the spirits’ sake, Ache. Ask them for the people’s.”

I wanted to tell her that faith mattered because truth mattered, not because it was convenient for government.

I still believed that.

I also remembered the merchant at our hall who wanted one record for both shores because arguments over authority were costing him money.

People crossed boundaries more easily than rulers drew them.

Sulad resumed paddling.

After a while he said, “My mother told me never to let anyone throw this away.”

He touched the cord beneath his cloth.

“Do you think it has power?” I asked.

“I think she does.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No.”

“Would you stop carrying it?”

He looked at me.

“For you?”

The question stung more than I expected.

“No,” I said.

He nodded once. I waited for him to make a joke.

He didn't.

That was the end of it.

For now.

* * *

We reached Maynila near sunset.

A trading boat was unloading at the landing. Two clerks sat beneath the shade with tally boards between them.

One was ours.

The other wore Tondo’s mark.

I stopped on the path.

My mother’s one-season agreement had already begun.

The Tondo clerk looked up, saw me, and bowed.

Respectful.

Perfectly respectful.

I disliked him immediately.

Our steward came forward.

“My lord.”

“How many boats today?”

“Seven since midday.”

“Any double collections?”

“None.”

I should have been pleased.

Instead I looked at the two boards lying side by side.

The arrangement worked.

That was the dangerous part.

Behind me, Ula stepped onto the bank.

“Still crowded?” she asked.

I knew what she meant.

Brunei. Tondo. Maynila. My mother’s Islam. Ula’s rites. Sulad’s cord. My father’s cloth. Two clerks recording the same river from two shores.

“Yes,” I said.

She smiled.

I walked toward the clerks.

The Tondo man rose again.

This time I returned the greeting.

Then I sat beside him.

“Show me what you counted,” I said.

He turned his board toward me. Seven vessels. The cargoes agreed with our steward's marks. Beside each boat he had cut a smaller row of strokes.

“The crews,” I said.

He nodded.

“Why?”

“A woman came to Tondo last month looking for her husband. He had gone aboard a trading boat. No one could tell her whether he had sailed, or crossed the river, or left the vessel before it departed.”

“Did you find him?”

“No.”

He touched the marks with one finger. “If it happens again, at least we'll know where to begin looking.”

I had come ready to catch him taking something from Maynila. Instead he had told me about a woman who wanted her husband home.

“And if a man comes looking because he claims a rower owes him service?” I asked.

The clerk looked down at the board.

“Then he will know where to begin looking too.”

Across the river a boat was coming in on the evening tide. I watched its crew prepare the lines. Every one of them was a person someone might be searching for.

“Who is allowed to look at your board?” I asked.

The clerk did not answer quickly.
