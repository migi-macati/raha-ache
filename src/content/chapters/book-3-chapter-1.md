I knew the shore by the color of the water.

The steersman told me that was a poor way to know a place. The water had been brown since dawn, and the wind could carry river mud a long way from the mouth. He showed me the line where the surface changed, a narrow run of smooth water between two broken fields of small waves.

"That is the river," he said. "Or another river. We will learn which when the tide turns."

I had watched for the first sight of land through three mornings. When it appeared, I had been below, helping Sulad shift a jar that had rubbed against the frame. By the time I climbed out, the shore was already there, low and green, as if it had been waiting without any interest in being found.

Harun was on the after platform. He had one hand on the steering rail and the other closed around a strip of cord. He was testing the wind against the current, not looking at the land.

"Maynila," I said.

He looked where I pointed. "Is that what you call all of it?"

"No. The river is there. The houses are farther in."

"Then show me the river."

I pointed again. I could see a darker break between the trees. It lay where I remembered the mouth, beyond a low spit that should have carried three fishing shelters. I saw only one roof. I could not tell whether the other two had fallen or whether I was looking at the wrong spit.

Harun called Isan up from the hold. Isan had been sleeping beside the cloth bundles after a night spent helping the men keep them dry. His cheek bore the impression of the woven mat. He listened to the steersman and then to me.

"You both see a river," he said. "One of you knows where it was. The other wants to know where it is."

Sulad laughed from the forward deck. He was repairing the loop on a basket, pulling the wet fiber tight with his teeth.

"You could ask the fish," he said. "They may remember us."

I wanted to tell him I could remember the path from the old landing to my mother's house. I could remember the post where the boys tied their smallest boats and the shallow water beyond it, where a man could stand with his feet on mud and still hold the gunwale. None of that told Harun how deep the channel had become.

A flying fish struck the side and fell back. The boy at the mast shouted as if it had been thrown at him. Harun did not turn.

"Keep the sail as it is," he told the crew. "We wait for someone who works this water."

He had already decided not to enter. I had spent the voyage preparing words for the first people who would meet us. I had imagined being carried up the river, standing before a house I knew, speaking my name and hearing it repeated. I had not prepared for a morning in which my first act was to wait.

We had parted from Tamin's boat at the last trading stop. He had gone toward the buyers who had agreed to take his salt. He had promised neither to follow us nor to return if we sent for him. We were alone with Harun's cloth, the resin, the water we had bought, and the letter I kept in a wrapped tube beneath my sleeping mat.

Harun had asked to see the tube once, when we took on fresh water. He had weighed it in his hand.

"It is lighter than the cloth," he said.

"It may open a door."

"A door can be opened from the other side."

I had thought him deliberately unkind. Now I watched him give an order to shorten sail, and I was glad he had not allowed my letter to steer the boat.

The first local craft came out of the glare after the sun had climbed above the trees. It was narrow, with two men working the paddles and a third standing aft. They had a basket covered with leaves between them. The man at the stern raised an arm. Harun answered with an open hand and ordered the crew to show the side where a visitor could approach.

The craft did not come alongside. It circled us at a distance that would have let its men turn away before we could reach them.

The man at the stern called a question.

I understood most of it. He wanted to know where we had come from and what we carried. I answered that we had come from Burné and brought cloth and resin to trade. He looked at Harun rather than me and called again.

Isan listened. He had worked with traders from this coast, but his speech did not sound like theirs. He turned to Harun.

"He asks whether you are the owner."

"I am," Harun said.

Isan answered. The man asked a longer question. Isan waited until he had finished, then asked him to repeat the middle part. The two paddlers exchanged a look.

"What did he say?" I asked.

"He asked whose permission we have to bring those goods upriver."

I took the letter tube from my mat and held it up. The man watched without moving closer.

"Tell him I am Ache," I said. "Tell him whose son I am."

Isan did not speak at once. "He asked about the goods."

"He will know the family."

"Perhaps. But he asked about the goods."

Harun laid a hand on the tube. He did not take it.

"Ask him where I can anchor without being accused of taking someone's place."

Isan called the question. The man pointed toward the shore, well outside the opening I had called the river. He named a stretch of water where traders waited for a guide. He said the bottom was firm enough for a small anchor, though the tide ran hard on the turn.

I knew the place. Or I knew a place that had been near it. My father had once stopped there to wait for a boat from the upper river. I remembered a stand of trees with roots rising out of the mud. The trees were gone. I could see only a low bank and a dark line of poles.

The man in the small craft said something else. I heard a word that could mean a landing or a share. Isan asked him to repeat it.

"He says the people who receive cargo there take a portion for keeping the peace," Isan told Harun. "I do not know whether he means a payment before landing or after sale."

"Ask."

The man answered with impatience. He lifted the leaf cover from the basket. There were fish beneath it, still moving. He had work to finish before the heat spoiled them. He would tell us the way to the anchorage, but he would not sit beside a foreign boat and settle a merchant's bargain for us.

Harun bought two fish. He paid the price the man named. The man counted the payment in his palm, then told us which side of the bank to keep clear of. Only then did he bring his craft close enough for me to see his face.

He was older than I had first thought. The skin at the corners of his eyes was deeply folded, and there was a pale scar above one ear. I searched his face for someone I might have known. I could not place him.

"Do you remember the house by the upriver bend?" I asked. "The one with the red-painted post?"

He looked at me as if I had asked about a dead fish.

"There are many houses by bends," he said.

"It belonged to my mother."

"Then ask her where it stands."

I opened my mouth. He had spoken without cruelty. He did not know what answer he had taken from me.

"She may no longer be there," I said.

He drew the leaf cover over the fish. "Then ask the people who are."

He turned his craft toward the bank. The paddlers leaned into their work. Within moments they were smaller than the basket they had carried.

Sulad had stopped mending. He watched them go.

"He did not know you," he said.

"He was a fisherman. He may never have gone upriver."

"Then you have learned one thing."

I could have struck him for how little he made of it. He had been away as long as I had. His old work had been on the water, among men who moved from landing to landing. If anyone should have been afraid that the names he knew were gone, it was Sulad.

"Do you remember the red post?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Then I was not mistaken."

"I did not say you were."

He drew the loop tight and set the basket aside.

"I remember the post," he said. "I do not know who owns the house."

Harun told the crew to make for the anchorage. The steersman put the boat across the current. For a time the land moved sideways rather than nearer. We passed a line of poles standing in water too shallow for our hull. Nets hung between some of them, and a boy waded along a narrow platform, lifting each net with a forked stick. The boy watched us pass. He did not wave.

There had been nets here when I was young. I remembered the smell of the catch drying on mats and the arguments over who had taken fish from whose stakes. The poles looked new. The boy could have been the son of a man I knew, or a stranger whose family had come while I was gone. I could not tell by watching him work.

A gust struck the sail as we crossed the smoother water. The boat leaned hard. One of the cloth bundles shifted against its lashings. Sulad went down on one knee and caught the loose rope before the bundle struck the water jar.

Harun ordered the sail eased. He waited until the steersman had the boat under him again, then sent two men to retie the cargo. He inspected the knot himself.

"Your shore is no easier than any other," he said.

"I did not tell you it would be."

"You told me you knew it."

I looked at the boy among the nets. "I knew it."

Harun took the line from the man at the bow and measured its length against his arm. He gave it back with instructions to sound before dropping the anchor.

I had wanted to offer my hands to the sounding line. I could have done it. Instead I went to the hold and brought up the two fish we had bought. The cook had asked for them before the sun rose much higher. I gutted them over a bowl and washed the knife in a little salt water. Their flesh was firm. The smell made me hungry, and I realized I had eaten nothing since the night watch.

Isan came down to help with the cooking fire. He carried the small clay vessel carefully, protecting its coals from the wind.

"You understood the man," he said.

"Most of him."

"You heard what he said about the landing?"

"I heard the word. I do not know whose share he meant."

"Neither do I."

He fed a little dry fiber to the coals and waited for the flame.

"Then when we go ashore," he said, "we ask the person who takes it."

I thought of Siripada's letter beneath my mat. I had imagined bringing it out before the first bargain, as if the seal could stand in place of all the words I might fail to find. The fisherman had asked about goods. The landing would ask about payment. My name had not yet answered either question.

We ate while the anchor was set. The first cast of the sounding line had found soft ground. Harun made the men try farther along. At the second place the weight came up with coarse sand, and the steersman judged the depth sufficient at low water. Harun ordered the anchor lowered there. The rope ran out, stopped, and tightened against the pull of the tide.

The boat swung until its bow faced the current. The river mouth lay beyond the nets and the dark line of trees. From where we sat, I could see smoke rising over roofs that had been hidden by the bank. I tried to count the distances between them. The old landing should have been somewhere behind the tallest roof.

A larger boat came out of the river toward us. It carried no cargo that I could see. Three people sat beneath a shade of woven leaves, and a fourth stood forward with a pole. They were not fishing. The standing man watched the water beneath our hull while the others watched us.

Harun set down his bowl.

"That will be the people who ask what we have brought," he said.

I wiped my hands. The wrapped letter lay where I had left it. I could fetch it before the visitors arrived.

Instead I went to the side and waited for their first question.

The larger boat did not come close enough to touch ours. It held against the current while a man in the bow measured the distance between the hulls with his eyes. The pole in his hands had a flat end worn smooth from pushing against mud. He lowered it into the water once, felt the bottom, and said something to the people beneath the shade.

A woman came forward. She wore a dark cloth folded at the shoulder and carried a small wooden box beneath one arm. The man with the pole shifted aside without being told.

"Who has come to sell?" she asked.

Harun stood where she could see him. "I have."

"From where?"

"Burné. We have cloth and resin. We are willing to trade at a place where the goods can be weighed and kept dry."

She looked at the sail, the lashings, the jars and the men who had come to the rail. Her eyes rested on me longer than on the others.

"How many will go ashore?"

"We have not agreed to go ashore," Harun said.

The woman opened her box. Inside lay several narrow sticks with cuts along their sides, a cord, and a small lump of dark wax. She lifted one of the sticks and turned it so that the cuts caught the light.

"Then you can remain here," she said. "But you cannot send your goods into the river without someone answerable for them."

"Answerable to whom?"

"To the people whose landing you use. And to the men who will have to pull your boat free if you ground it."

Harun smiled at that. He had spent the morning sounding the bottom. He knew what it cost to free a loaded hull.

"How much do they take?" he asked.

"That depends on what you bring, where you put it, and how long it stays."

"Then tell me what they take for a man who wishes only to ask questions."

She glanced at me. "Does the man have anything to sell?"

I had the letter. I had a little coin, less than I had intended after buying the replacement water jar in Burné. I had a knife, two changes of cloth and a small bundle of gifts that had once seemed suitable for a homecoming. I could have named them all. None would have answered her.

"He has a name," Harun said.

"Names do not keep goods from being stolen."

She spoke without raising her voice. The men behind her had not moved. One of them was looking at the rope between our anchor and the bow, judging whether the tide might bring us closer.

I stepped forward.

"My name is Ache. My mother had a house upriver. I left this shore as a boy."

The woman looked toward the trees. "Many boys leave."

"I have returned to speak with my kin."

"Then your kin can receive you. Have they sent a boat?"

"No."

"Have they been told you are coming?"

"No."

She shut the box. "Then you are a stranger asking us to guess who will answer for him."

I felt Sulad shift beside me. He had listened without offering a word. I wanted him to speak, to name the house and the river and the people who had once known us. Instead he asked the woman where the men who worked the lower nets sold their catch.

She looked at him with new attention.

"At the lower landing when the tide is high. Why?"

"I worked there before I went south."

"With whom?"

He named a man I remembered as a youth. I had not thought of him in years. The woman shook her head.

"He has not worked those nets for a long time."

"Is he dead?"

"He lives beyond the second bend. His sons work the lower landing."

Sulad's mouth tightened. He asked how many sons. She named two. He knew the older one's name. The woman looked at him again, this time as if she were trying to place a sound heard in another room.

"You can ask them," she said. "If they wish to answer."

I had spoken of my mother and received nothing. Sulad had asked about a net worker and learned where to look. I told myself this was because he had chosen an easier question.

Harun called for the bundle of cloth nearest the hatch. The steersman brought up a folded length and set it on a clean mat. Harun did not unfold it.

"This is a sample," he told the woman. "You may look at it here. If there is a lawful place to sell, I will take the rest there under terms we agree upon."

She asked him whether the cloth had already been promised to a buyer. He said no. She asked whether anyone aboard had a claim against it. He said that the crew's wages and the owner's obligations had been settled for the passage, but the sale would still have to meet his terms. She listened as if she had heard merchants say these things before and sometimes found them untrue.

"Who will keep it while you go upriver?" she asked.

"I will," Harun said. "I have not agreed to go upriver."

She smiled for the first time. "Then we understand each other."

The man with the pole brought their boat a little closer. The woman leaned across the gap, lifted a corner of the sample cloth, and felt its weave between finger and thumb. She looked for damp along the fold. Harun let her inspect it but did not pass the bundle over.

She named a place on the bank where visiting merchants could wait for a buyer. The shelter had a roof and a raised floor. Goods stored there would be recorded against a custodian's marks. A charge was due for each night. Another share would be owed if a sale took place through the landing.

I knew where she meant. There had been a shelter there when I was young, but its roof had been thatched with palm leaves and its floor was low enough for boys to crawl beneath. The new one rose above a line of posts. Two boats were already moored beside it.

"Who holds that landing?" I asked.

"The people who work it."

"Who speaks for them?"

"I do, for goods brought to this water. Someone else will hear a claim to a house."

I was about to ask whose order gave her that right. I could hear how the question would sound: a man who had not come home for years demanding the name of the person who allowed other people to work.

Harun asked about the charge. She gave a number. He countered. They settled on a fee for one night, payable only if his cargo was actually put ashore, and a smaller sum for the use of a landing boat. She would send someone to show him the channel after the tide turned. He would decide then whether to move the goods.

I had been standing beside him, thinking of the first words I would say to the people of Maynila. He had secured the possibility of a roof.

"May I go ashore before the cargo?" I asked.

The woman turned to me. "With what purpose?"

"To find the people who knew my mother."

"Will you carry goods?"

"No."

"Arms?"

I had a knife. I touched its handle before answering. "Only this."

"Leave it aboard."

I looked at Harun. He gave no sign that he would speak for me.

"How many people?" she asked.

"Three. Myself, Sulad and Isan."

Sulad turned his head. "You have not asked me."

The woman waited. Her two companions looked at the river. Neither seemed surprised.

I felt heat rise in my face.

"Will you come?" I asked him.

"To the lower landing, perhaps. I have someone to find. I will not tell strangers I speak for your family."

"I did not ask you to."

"You said three."

I had said it because three sounded safer than one. I had counted Sulad among my people as easily as I counted my own hands.

"Then I ask you to come as yourself," I said.

He looked at the shore. "I will come to the lower landing. After that I may go another way."

Isan was leaning against the mast. I turned to him.

"And you?"

"To help you ask for a place to sleep," he said. "If I can understand the people who answer. If they speak among themselves and do not wish me to hear, I cannot make them."

The woman watched this exchange with an expression I could not read.

"Three people," she said, "with separate reasons for coming. That is easier to understand."

She told us that a small boat would take us to the lower landing at the next safe turn of the tide. We would pay its paddlers. We would not enter any storehouse or dwelling without an invitation. If we wanted a hearing about a household, we would find the person responsible for that household and ask.

"Who is responsible for my mother's house?" I asked.

She looked at me a long moment. "You have not told me where it is."

I described the bend, the old red post, the path behind the house and the trees that had once shaded the yard. I gave the names of two neighboring households. She knew one name. The other had moved, or its people had changed what they called themselves. I could not tell from her answer.

"There is a house there," she said. "It is not the house you describe."

"Who lives in it?"

"I will not give you a household's names from the deck of a foreign boat."

I wanted to say I was not foreign. I had been born on this river. I knew where the fish gathered when the tide fell and how the mud smelled before rain. I could still picture the shadows of the roof poles on my mother's floor.

But the boat beneath my feet had come from Burné, and I had no one on shore who had agreed to speak for me.

The woman put the sample cloth down. Harun took it back without folding it.

"At the next tide," she said. "If you still wish to come."

Her boat turned away. The man with the pole pushed hard, and the hull slid clear of ours. She remained standing until the gap widened. Then she sat beneath the shade and opened her box again, checking the sticks one by one as they went toward the landing.

Harun ordered the cloth returned to the hold. He told the steersman to keep the anchor where it was until the promised guide arrived.

"You can go," he said to me. "But you will not use my cargo as a pledge for your family."

"I know."

"Then leave the letter here unless they ask for it. A letter from a ruler across the sea will not tell them whose floor you may sleep on."

I wanted to argue that Siripada's name might make someone listen. It might. It could also make the people on shore wonder what kind of man needed a distant ruler's seal before he could ask for his mother's house.

I took the knife from my belt and laid it beside my sleeping mat. The letter stayed wrapped. I counted the coin I had left, then put away half for the passage back to the boat. The other half would pay the paddlers and perhaps a meal. I had imagined arriving with gifts. I would begin by paying a fare.

Sulad sat near the bow, looking toward the lower nets. He had not spoken since the woman's boat departed.

"Did you know the man she named?" I asked.

"I knew his father. I thought he would still be working."

"She said he was alive."

"She said he lived beyond the bend."

"That is good news."

"Maybe. I owe him something."

"What?"

Sulad rubbed his thumb along a scar on the side of his hand.

"A net weight. I took it when I left. I meant to give it back."

"After all these years?"

"After all these years."

He looked at me then. "You have come to ask for a house. I have come to return a stone. We will see which one of us is welcome."

I tried to laugh. I could not.

The tide had begun to turn. The water along the bank drew a new line of foam around the posts. A boat pushed off from the landing shelter and headed toward us, its paddlers working slowly against the current.

I watched it come without reaching for the letter.

The boat that came for us had room for four passengers if nobody brought a bundle. Its paddlers made me sit low between the thwarts. Isan sat behind me, holding his knees together so the older paddler could work. Sulad had taken his place in the bow.

The landing woman had sent a younger man with us. He was there to show the way, and perhaps to make certain we went no farther than we had promised.

He watched me look upriver.

"Have you been here before?" he asked.

"I was born here."

"Then why are you looking as though you expect the bank to move?"

I could not tell whether he meant to be insolent. A tree stood where I remembered open water. A landing had been pulled farther inland. The river had moved, or the people had moved their work to suit it. I had spent the crossing imagining that the first familiar thing would steady me. Instead every familiar thing made the next one harder to find.

We came ashore at a narrow set of steps beside the new shelter. The paddlers held their boat against the current while we climbed out. I paid the agreed fare. The older man checked the coins and passed one to his companion.

A row of baskets stood beneath the shelter roof. They held dry fish, split bamboo, cord and pieces of pottery wrapped in straw. Beyond them a woman was washing a clay jar with sand. She looked at our wet feet, then at the landing guide.

"No goods?" she asked.

"Only questions," he answered.

"Those are harder to store."

The guide pointed toward the path behind the houses. "The bend he described is beyond the cooking sheds. Do not enter anyone's yard until you are asked."

Sulad looked downstream, where the net workers were lifting their lines out of the mud.

"I will go to the sons," he said.

"You will find us at the house?"

"I will find you if I need you."

He took the little wrapped stone from his belt and went toward the nets. I watched him until he disappeared behind a roof. He had carried that weight all the way from Burné. I had asked him to return with me, and only now understood that we had come back for different things.

Isan followed me along the path. The guide came behind us, stepping aside whenever a porter approached with a load. Nobody bowed. Twice we had to wait while a pair of men carried long poles between them. Their work was more urgent than my arrival.

At the bend I stopped.

The house was there. The red post was not.

A longer building stood on the old raised floor, with a new roof extending over the path. The walls were woven panels I had never seen. Beneath the house, a man was shaving a plank with a small blade. Two girls carried bundles of drying reeds past him. At the far end a cooking fire smoked beneath an open lean-to.

The old yard had held a tamarind tree. I remembered the rough bark against my back when my mother had sent me outside to wait for a visitor. Its trunk was gone. The stump, cut low and pale at the center, had become a place for setting jars.

I stepped toward it.

A girl carrying reeds moved into my path. "Where are you going?"

"To the house."

"Who sent you?"

I looked past her. I could see the hall opening where my mother had received petitioners. A new screen hung across it. Someone had stretched cord from the screen to the eaves and tied bunches of herbs to dry.

"I lived here," I said.

The girl looked at Isan. He looked at me.

A woman came out from beneath the roof. She was carrying a shallow basket filled with cut reeds. She set it down before speaking.

"Who lived here?"

"I did. With my mother."

"That was not my question."

I told her my name.

She studied my face. Her hair was threaded with gray, but her back was straight and her hands were stained where she had handled the reeds. I remembered her only when she moved the basket with her foot to keep the path clear.

"Amaya?" I said.

She had once brought baskets of river greens to the kitchen. I had called her by name as a boy and been scolded for doing so without greeting her first.

She did not answer at once.

"Your voice has changed," she said.

I felt relief so quickly that I almost laughed. "You remember."

"I remember a boy who took fruit from the kitchen and blamed the dogs."

"It was only once."

"It was often enough."

She looked toward the guide, who had stopped beside the path. He nodded to her. Then she turned back to me.

"You have come from the sea."

"From Burné."

"And you have come without word."

"I did not know who to send it to."

"Then you have found one of your difficulties."

I looked at the raised floor. "Who lives here now?"

"People who work here."

"I can see that."

"Then you can also see why you cannot walk through them as if they were shadows."

I had not meant to do that. I had imagined her calling into the house, someone coming to the steps, the old screen drawn aside. The girls with the reeds were waiting for us to finish. One had lowered her load to the ground.

"Is my mother here?" I asked.

Amaya's expression changed. Not into grief. I had feared grief and been ready to demand the truth of it. What I saw was caution.

"I will not answer for her from a path full of strangers."

"Is she alive?"

"I have not said she is dead."

"Then where is she?"

"I said I will not answer for her here."

The guide shifted his weight. Isan glanced at the girls, who were pretending not to listen.

Amaya picked up the basket. "Come to the shade. You can ask what you came to ask. But do not stand in the path."

We moved to a low bench beside the stump. She told the girls to carry their reeds inside and sent the man with the plank to fetch water. He did not leave his work immediately. He finished the cut he had begun, brushed the shavings from the blade and then went.

I looked at the building. The floor had been extended toward the river. Where my father's old storage space had stood, three smaller rooms now opened onto the yard.

"You changed everything."

"The roof leaked. The floor fell at the river end. We put it back."

"Who paid?"

"The people who needed a roof."

"My mother owned this place."

Amaya took the water from the man and set it between us.

"Your mother kept a household here. There were people who worked for it, and people who depended on what came through it. After you left, those people still needed to eat."

"I know that."

"You have just asked me who paid for a roof."

I looked at the reed bundles. "What do you make here?"

"Matting. Some rope. We dry what the fishers bring when the weather holds. The upper room is used by a family whose boat was lost. They keep the fire and watch the yard at night."

"And the old hall?"

"Used for work when it rains."

I tried to see the old hall beneath the screen. I remembered my mother's voice from the far end of it, telling a visitor that a promise made at low tide must still hold when the water rose. I had once thought every order she gave remained in the walls after she spoke.

"Who gave you leave to use it?" I asked.

Amaya looked toward the man who had been shaving the plank. "Ask him who put his hands to the floor. Ask the girls who carried the reeds when their father could no longer work. Ask the boat owners who bring fish here because the lower sheds flood."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the part of the answer you have not asked for."

She sat opposite me. The bench creaked beneath her.

"We did not take your mother's sleeping place and call it ours," she said. "We kept the rooms dry. We let the people who needed work use them. There is an agreement with the people who receive goods upriver. It has been renewed more than once. I can show you who witnessed it."

"An agreement made without me."

"Yes."

"I was the heir."

"You were gone."

I could have told her why. I could have described the crossings, the work in Burné, the men who had held me captive and the ship whose guns had shaken the river. None of those things would have changed who had repaired the floor.

"Do you still owe a share to the household?" I asked.

"We give a portion of what passes through here to keep the landing open. Some is taken for the river work. Some goes to people who can send men when there is trouble."

"Who are those people?"

"That is a question for the men who take it."

"Do you trust them?"

"I trust them to remember when a payment is due."

She said it with the weariness of someone who had answered the same question too often. I had meant to discover whether the house had been stolen. I was learning that the people inside had found a way to survive under terms they did not wholly choose.

A child appeared at the doorway with a torn strip of matting. She asked Amaya whether to cut it again or use the short pieces. Amaya took it, pulled at the damaged edge and told her to save the sound fibers. The child returned inside. Amaya had not asked my permission to interrupt our talk. It had not occurred to her that she should.

I drank the water. It tasted faintly of the jar.

"Will you tell me where my mother is?"

"I will tell her that you have come, if I can reach the person who carries her messages."

"Then she is alive."

"I told you what I can promise. Do not make a promise larger by repeating it to yourself."

I put the cup down.

"May I wait here until someone comes?"

"Not inside."

I looked at her.

"There are people sleeping here," she said. "One is ill. They do not know you. I know you as a boy, but I do not know what you have brought back."

"Two men. No weapons. A letter."

"From whom?"

"A ruler in Burné."

Her gaze moved to Isan. He had stayed quiet through most of the talk. When Amaya asked him where he came from, he told her that he had worked in Burné and traveled with traders, and that he could help with words he knew. He did not claim to be a messenger from a ruler.

Amaya looked back at me.

"Then keep the letter for the people who ask to see it. It does not make room for you in a house where others are sleeping."

I wanted to be angry with her. She had recognized me, remembered the fruit, spoken of my mother without the coldness of a stranger. Yet she had refused the one thing I had imagined even a stranger might grant: a place to stand in my own house.

Behind her, the man returned to his plank. He placed it across two supports and began to shave the edge. The sound was soft and regular. He had not looked up again.

"Who could hear my claim?" I asked.

"Someone who will hear theirs as well."

"Can you bring that person?"

"I can send word. I cannot order him to come."

"Will you?"

"Yes. But I will not send a message saying the heir has returned to take the house. I will say that a man called Ache has come from Burné, and that I knew him when he was young. If he wishes to be heard, he must listen to the people who live here."

I could have demanded she use my title. Instead I asked when the messenger might return.

"Not before the tide changes again. Perhaps later. You may wait at the shelter. There is shade there."

I stood. Isan rose with me.

At the edge of the yard I turned back. The stump of the tamarind tree stood in the sun, its cut surface cracked. I had played beneath its branches, and I had been certain that the tree would outlive me.

"Why did you cut it?" I asked.

"The roots lifted the floor. When the water came high, the posts shifted."

"It was a good tree."

"It was. We used the wood to mend the roof."

I looked up. Above the doorway, one beam was darker than the rest. Its grain curved where a branch had grown.

The house had kept part of the tree. It had needed the rest of it gone.

Amaya picked up her basket and called the girls back to their work. I went down the path with Isan. The guide was waiting where we had left him. He did not ask whether I had found my family.

At the first bend I stopped and looked back. The red post was nowhere in sight. I could still hear the blade working against the plank.

"She knew you," Isan said.

"Yes."

"She did not send you away."

"She would not let me enter."

"Those are two things she has done."

I had expected him to comfort me. He had given me the same sort of answer he gave when a trader spoke too quickly for him to be certain of the price.

We walked on toward the shelter. I had come to ask who had taken my mother's house. I would have to learn the names of the people who had kept it standing.

At the shelter the keeper had laid a strip of matting in the shade for people waiting to be taken upriver. She asked whether we had found the house.

"We found it," Isan said.

"That is more than some do."

She gave us a place away from the stored fish and told us not to put our feet on the bundles. I sat where I could see the path from Amaya's yard. Every time a porter came around the corner I lifted my head. After the fourth, the keeper stopped watching me.

"You are waiting for someone," she said.

"Perhaps."

"Then you should know the people who come by this path are mostly carrying things. They are not bringing answers."

I asked whether she knew Amaya.

"Everyone who works this landing knows her. She keeps a dry roof. That is a useful thing."

"Who does she answer to?"

The keeper took a knife to a length of cord, cutting away the part that had frayed.

"She answers when someone has work for her. You will have to ask her about the rest."

A boat arrived from upriver with jars sealed under leaves. The men bringing it in called for room at the steps. Two other boats moved aside, reluctantly. A man stepped ashore before the jars were unloaded. He wore a short cloth around his waist and carried a narrow parcel wrapped in oil-darkened leaves. The landing keeper greeted him by name.

"From Tondo?" she asked.

"From the far bank. The crossing was crowded."

He set down the parcel and asked whether a boat had come from Burné. The keeper pointed toward me.

I rose before he reached us.

"Are you looking for me?"

"I was told a man from Burné had asked for the house at the bend."

"Amaya sent you?"

"No. I heard it from the people who brought their fish downriver. The story has traveled faster than I have."

He looked at Isan and then at the guide who had brought us ashore.

"My name is Banu," he said. "I carry goods for men who have dealings on both banks. I am not here to judge whose son you are."

"I have not asked you to."

"Then perhaps we will speak plainly."

He sat on the edge of the shelter, leaving his parcel beside him. The keeper brought him water without asking. He drank and returned the cup.

"Your mother held a household here," he said.

"She did."

"People remember that. They also remember the years after you went away."

"I have heard about the roof."

"I am speaking about the river."

He pointed toward the boats waiting to unload. "The people who work here need a place to bring their goods when the water is high. They need someone to settle a quarrel over a broken jar or a boat left across the steps. They have found ways to do those things."

"With Tondo's help?"

"Sometimes with Tondo's boats. Sometimes with men from this side. The water is wide enough for both banks, and narrow enough for a man to be struck by the other man's paddle."

"Who now takes the share that once came to my mother's hall?"

Banu glanced at the keeper. She had returned to her cord, but I could see that she was listening.

"There is no single share," he said. "A man who stores fish owes one thing. A man who uses the landing boat owes another. A boat that has to be hauled free costs its owner something. There are quarrels about all of them."

"Who settles the quarrels?"

"People who can make their decisions hold. You will meet some of them if you stay."

I had asked the same question of Amaya and the landing woman. Each had answered with work and obligations. I wanted one name I could challenge, one person who had taken my mother's place.

"Are you afraid to tell me?" I asked.

Banu laughed once, without pleasure.

"I came here with goods that belong to other men. If I speak a name, you may decide I have spoken for him. Then he will ask why I have made his quarrel mine. That is not fear. That is knowing whose jars I carry."

Isan leaned toward me.

"He says he has no authority to offer terms," he said quietly.

"I understood him."

"Then do not ask him to give them."

Banu had heard enough of our exchange to understand that we were speaking about him. He did not object. He untied his parcel. Inside were two lengths of dyed cord, a small bundle of fine thread and a piece of polished shell.

"These are for a woman who makes sail repairs near the upper crossing," he said. "I was asked to bring them today. I had business here whether you returned or not."

"Then why speak to me?"

"Because a man who arrives with a letter from a distant ruler can make people nervous. They may stop bringing their goods until they learn what he intends."

"I have no armed men."

"Then tell them so."

"I have said it."

"You said it to a woman who receives visiting merchants. Have you said it to the people whose work you wish to enter?"

I thought of Amaya's girls carrying reeds. I had asked who allowed them to use the house. I had not told them what I meant to do with it.

"I want to speak with my family," I said.

"That is a reason to come ashore. It is not a reason for everyone else to stop working."

He wrapped the parcel again.

"Some people will remember you. Some will say you are an heir. Some will say a boy who left has returned with strangers and a seal. Those are different stories. You will have to decide which one you want them to hear from you."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe Harun has a boat full of goods and that the tide will not wait for us."

He had named Harun without my telling him. I looked toward the outer anchorage, hidden by the bend.

"How do you know his name?"

"The woman who came to receive his cargo sent word about a trader who would not promise what he did not own. That is worth remembering."

Banu stood. He took his parcel and turned toward the steps.

"Will you carry a message?" I asked.

"To whom?"

"To the people across the river who claim a voice here."

"What message?"

I had imagined saying that Ache, son of the former ruler, had returned to take his rightful place. The words were ready. I could feel them in my mouth.

"Tell them I have come to ask what has changed," I said.

Banu looked at me.

"That will not tell them what you want."

"Tell them I want a hearing. With people from both banks, if they have a claim to the landing and the house."

"That is more useful. Who will stand for you?"

I thought of Harun, whose goods I had promised not to pledge; of Isan, who had already warned me that he could not understand every word; of Sulad, who had gone to find a man over a borrowed stone.

"I do not know yet."

"Then find out before you ask for a hearing."

He took two steps, then turned.

"And do not say I have promised one. I have only agreed to carry your question."

I nodded. He left with the men carrying jars. I watched him take the upper path, his parcel held above the mud.

The landing keeper put down her knife.

"You asked him to do something difficult," she said.

"He carries messages."

"He carries things people have paid him to carry. A question like yours can cost him more than a broken jar."

"I offered to pay."

"You did not."

She was right. I had been so pleased to find someone who knew the far bank that I had forgotten to ask his price.

I stood and went to the steps. Banu's boat had not yet pushed off. I called his name. He looked up.

"What would you take to carry my question?" I asked.

He glanced at the men loading the jars. "I am going there already. I will take it. But if someone asks who sent me, I will say you did. I will not say I believe you."

"That is fair."

"Fairness will be tested when they answer."

He climbed into the boat. The men pushed it clear. For a moment the current held them almost still. Then the paddles found their rhythm and the boat began to cross.

When I turned back, Sulad was standing beside the shelter.

His hands were wet. The little wrapped stone was gone from his belt.

"Did you find him?" I asked.

"I found his sons."

"Did they take it?"

"The older one did. He said his father would remember why it was missing."

"Was he angry?"

"He said the old man had complained about it for years. Then he asked whether I had brought any news of the men who went south."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I knew some of their names and did not know the rest."

Sulad sat on the matting without asking. The keeper moved a basket to give him room.

"Will you come to the hearing?" I asked.

"What hearing?"

"The one I hope someone will agree to."

He rubbed the wet mud from his wrist.

"Will they hear the people in the house?"

"They must."

"Then ask them first."

"I have."

"You asked who gave them leave to stay."

I looked at him. He had not been there. Amaya must already have spoken to someone, or the story had spread along the bank.

"I asked who had the right to decide," I said.

"That is not the same question."

He looked toward the path that led to the old house.

"I came here to give back something I took. I did not come to take back something other people have been using."

"I did not come to drive them out."

"Then say that before someone has to ask."

The sun had moved across the shelter roof. Beyond the steps, a boat carried the last of its jars toward the upper landing. Men shouted for a line. The keeper rose to help them.

I had spent the morning asking who spoke for the shore. Now I needed to learn who would agree to speak beside me, and what I would be willing to promise them.

Harun sent for me when the afternoon tide began to fall.

His messenger was a boy from the landing boat. The boy had been paid to carry one sentence and repeated it three times while I walked beside him: the owner wanted to know whether I meant to return before dark.

At the outer anchorage, Harun was inspecting a wet bundle of cloth. One corner had taken water during the crossing. He had opened it in the shade and laid the damaged lengths across the deck.

"You have been ashore all day," he said.

"I found the house."

"Good. Will you be staying there?"

"No."

He looked at the cloth. "Then where?"

"I am arranging a place."

"You told me you knew this shore."

"I knew the people who lived here."

"Did they know you?"

"One did."

He picked up the wet edge of the cloth. The dye had run where the fold had been pressed tight. He rubbed the mark between his fingers and let it fall.

"I have been offered a landing place for the cargo," he said. "The fee is reasonable if we can sell before the weather changes. I cannot wait here while you decide whether a house is yours."

"I have not asked you to."

"You asked me to bring you home. I agreed because the voyage had buyers at its end. I will sell my goods. After that I will go where I can trade again."

He did not say when. I understood that he wanted me to ask.

"How long?"

"Until the next good wind, if I find buyers. Less, if this river becomes troublesome."

"I may need more time."

"Then you will need a place that does not depend on my anchor."

I had come back to the boat expecting shelter. Harun had given me a passage, and I was already treating it as a house I could return to whenever the shore refused me.

I went to the place where my sleeping mat had been rolled. I took out the small bundle of gifts I had kept for my family. There were beads, a length of fine thread, a little carved box and two pieces of cloth I had bought with my own money. I had imagined placing them before my mother, each one an answer to the years I had been away.

I set the bundle on the deck.

"These are mine," I said.

Harun looked at them without touching them.

"Will you sell them?"

"I may have to."

"Then do not sell them to me. I know what you paid. I would offer too little and feel ashamed, or too much and resent it."

He went back to the damaged cloth.

"Ask the woman at the landing. She knows who needs what."

I took the gifts ashore. Isan came with me, carrying his own bag. Sulad chose to remain at the lower nets for the evening. He said he had found a man who would pay him to help lift a boat at first light.

"Will you stay on this shore?" I asked.

"For tomorrow's work. After that I will see."

I wanted to tell him we had returned together. I wanted him beside me when I went to ask for a hearing. He had a day's wage in his hand already. I could offer him only the possibility of trouble.

"Then I will find you after the work," I said.

The landing woman was beneath the shelter, speaking with the keeper about a jar that had cracked in storage. She listened when I asked for a place to sleep.

"For how many?" she said.

"Two. Isan and me."

"For how long?"

"Two nights, perhaps three."

"Perhaps is a word that costs other people money."

"Three nights."

She named a price. It was more than the coin I had kept from the crossing.

"I can pay with these," I said, opening the bundle.

She examined the beads. She did not praise them. She asked who had made the thread and whether the carved box would hold a lid tight enough to keep salt out. I told her what I knew. She set aside the box and a length of thread.

"I have buyers for those. I do not have buyers for your memories."

"I did not ask you to buy them."

"You brought them out as if I should know their worth."

She called the keeper over. They spoke about the small room behind the shelter where tools were stored during the rains. It had a roof and a raised floor, but its door did not close properly. A man had been sleeping there while he repaired the steps. He was leaving that evening to work upriver.

"Three nights," the woman said. "You pay the keeper for the room. You bring your own food. If the workmen need their tools, they may enter in daylight."

"And after three nights?"

"You ask again. If you have brought trouble to the landing, the answer will be no."

"What counts as trouble?"

"If men arrive with weapons because you have told them this is your shore. If you stop a boat from unloading because its owner will not call you prince. If you take goods on credit in someone else's name."

I felt the guide watching us. The keeper was standing with her arms folded. They were waiting to learn whether I would bargain over the conditions.

"I agree," I said.

The woman looked at Isan.

"And you?"

"I am here to help him speak. I am not his property."

"Will you keep the peace in the room?"

"If he makes trouble, I will leave before the men with weapons come."

The keeper smiled. The landing woman did not.

She asked me to repeat the conditions aloud. I did, slowly. The guide and keeper listened. No written contract was produced. The woman took the carved box and thread as payment for the room, then returned the beads and cloth to me.

"You have paid for three nights," she said. "You have not bought the landing."

I carried my bundle to the room. Its floor smelled of damp wood and old rope. Two poles stood against the wall, and a coil of line hung from a peg. The man who had slept there was taking away a clay lamp. He left us a shallow bowl for water and told us which part of the roof leaked in a hard rain.

I placed my mat on the dry side. Isan set his bag beside the door.

"Will you tell Harun?" he asked.

"That we have a place?"

"That you have given up your gifts for it."

"I have not given them all up."

"You chose the ones you could bear to lose."

I looked at the little carved box in the keeper's hands as she carried it toward the main shelter. It had been meant for my mother. I had pictured her opening it and asking who had taught me to choose such things. Now it would hold salt or thread for someone who did not know her.

Outside, a loaded boat had come in too close to the steps. Its bow line caught beneath a projecting beam. The crew tried to free it with a pole, but the tide had already begun to pull the hull away. The keeper shouted for someone to hold the stern.

I went down to the water. Two men were pulling at the line. I could see that the knot would tighten if they hauled harder.

"Slack it," I said. "Let the bow come back."

The man nearest me gave the line a little room. The hull swung toward the steps. I took the loose end and passed it beneath the beam, keeping my fingers clear as the boat moved. When the steersman put his pole down, the line came free.

The boat's owner thanked the men who had held the stern. He nodded to me and went back to his cargo. He did not ask my name.

I stood in the water to my knees, holding the wet line. My hands shook from the strain. I had done no more than a boatman might do on any afternoon. The work had been useful, and nobody had needed to know who my mother was.

The landing woman came to the steps.

"Can you work a line?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then if you want another night after the third, ask the keeper whether she needs help. She will tell you what she pays."

"I have come for a hearing."

"I know. You can wait for it and still earn your supper."

I handed the line back to the boat's crew. At the top of the steps, Isan was waiting with the rest of my bundle. The shelter roof cast a long shadow over the path toward the house.

For three nights I had a place on the river. It belonged to someone else, and they had agreed to let me use it. That was the first promise I had received on my own shore.

On the first morning I woke in the tool room to the sound of a man trying to open the door.

The wood caught against the floor. He pushed once, stopped, then called through the gap.

"I need the long pole."

I moved my mat aside and lifted the door clear. The man stepped past me, took the pole from the wall and went without asking how I had slept. He was the same worker who had shown me the leak in the roof. He had not expected me to be there after sunrise.

I went outside. The river was low, and the boats nearest the landing leaned toward the mud. The keeper was lighting a fire beneath a pot. She told me to carry water if I wanted breakfast. I took the jar and followed her directions to the well.

When I returned, three men were waiting beside the shelter. They had come from the upper crossing. One held a length of cloth dyed dark at the edge. Another wore a knife with a wooden handle. The third had brought no weapon I could see.

They were speaking with the landing woman. I heard Burné, then my name.

The man with the cloth turned toward me.

"Are you the one who came from across the sea?"

"I am."

"Have you brought soldiers?"

"No."

"Then why do people say you have?"

I looked at the landing woman. She was watching me, waiting to see how I would answer.

"I came on a trader's boat," I said. "It is still at anchor. The owner will sell his goods and leave when he chooses."

The man with the knife pointed toward the river. "We were told a ruler from Burné sent you with orders."

"He gave me a letter."

"Then you have brought orders."

"Not for this landing."

"Let us see it."

I had left the letter wrapped in the tool room. I could have fetched it. I could have held the seal before them and hoped they would be impressed by a name from another shore.

"What would it prove to you?" I asked.

"That you have been sent."

"It would prove that a man across the sea knows I am here. It does not give me your boats."

The landing woman looked at me sharply. The man with the cloth did not seem satisfied.

"Why did you go to the house at the bend?" he asked.

"My mother kept it."

"That house has workers in it."

"I know. I spoke to them."

"Then leave them to their work."

I could hear the keeper moving her pot away from the fire. The people carrying baskets had stopped at the edge of the shelter. I had been ashore only one day, and already my arrival had made the place less useful to them.

"I have asked for a hearing," I said. "I will not take the house while people are living there."

The man with the knife stepped forward.

"Will you say that where the people can hear?"

"Yes."

"Then say it."

He had not called me prince. He had not asked who my father was. He wanted a promise that would keep his work from being disturbed.

I turned toward the baskets and the people waiting beside them.

"I am Ache," I said. "I was born upriver. I have come to ask what became of my family and the place they kept. I have no soldiers. I will not put people out of their house or stop their boats while my claim is heard."

The words sounded smaller than the speech I had practiced at sea. They were also words I could keep.

The landing woman raised a hand. "He has paid for three nights in the tool room. He has promised no more than he has said. If he breaks that promise, tell me before you come here with knives."

The man with the knife looked down at his belt. He moved his hand away from the handle.

"We were told to ask," he said.

"Then you have asked," she answered.

The three men left toward the upper crossing. The people with baskets began moving again. One woman passed so close to me that the edge of her load brushed my arm. She did not apologize. I stepped out of her way.

Isan came from the tool room, rubbing sleep from his eyes.

"What happened?" he asked.

"I was asked whether I had brought soldiers."

"And what did you say?"

"The truth."

"That is sometimes enough for a morning."

He took the water jar from me and went to help the keeper.

By midday Banu returned from the far bank. He found me beside the steps, helping the men lift a boat clear of the mud. He waited until we had set the supports beneath its hull.

"I carried your question," he said.

"What did they answer?"

"First they asked who had sent me. I told them you had. Then they asked whether you had brought men to make your claim. I said I had seen none."

"Will they hear me?"

"They will send someone to listen if the people at this landing also agree. They will not come because a foreign ruler has written your name on a letter."

"When?"

"After the next market day, perhaps. I was given no promise of an hour."

"Who will come?"

"A man who speaks for a house that keeps boats on the far bank. I did not ask him to give you his whole family."

I had hoped for a name I could place among the old rivalries. I knew the river had carried more families and more debts than I could remember.

"Do they still speak of my mother?" I asked.

"Yes."

"What do they say?"

"That she knew how to make men listen. Some liked it. Some did not."

He let the words stand. Then he took a small piece of dried fish from his bag and began to eat. He had carried my question without payment and had come back with the answer. I offered him part of my remaining coin.

He looked at it.

"Keep enough to stay until they come. If you have no food, you will be asking the wrong people for help."

He took one small piece and put it away.

Amaya came down the path while we were speaking. She was carrying a folded cloth wrapped around something hard. The girls from her house had not come with her. She had walked alone.

"I heard you spoke before the landing," she said.

"I said I would not put anyone out."

"I know. Someone came to tell me."

"Will you speak for me at the hearing?"

"I will speak about what I know. You will have to decide whether that helps you."

She unfolded the cloth. Inside lay a piece of old red-painted wood, no longer than my hand. Its edges were worn where it had been cut from a larger post. A hole had been bored near one end.

I knew the color before I touched it.

"The post," I said.

"Part of it. The rest was rotten. Your mother told us to take it down before it fell on someone."

I took the piece. Red paint remained in the grain where the sun had not reached.

"She told you?"

"Before she left the house."

"Where did she go?"

Amaya looked toward the people moving through the shelter.

"I have told you I will not answer for her here. I can tell you what she said to us."

I waited.

"She said the hall must remain open for the work that fed the household. If the roof failed, we were to mend it. If people needed the floor, we were to let them use it. She knew that you were gone. She did not tell us to leave the house empty until you returned."

I looked at the wood in my hand. I had imagined that the people at the bend had taken advantage of my mother's absence. Amaya was telling me that my mother had given them work to do.

"Did she say it was no longer ours?"

"She did not speak about ownership the way you have been speaking. She told us to keep the place alive. There were people who could not take their families away when she went."

"Who heard her?"

"Three people who worked there. One is dead. One has gone inland. I am here."

"Will you say that before the people from Tondo?"

"Yes. And I will tell them who repaired the floor and who paid for the new roof. If you want me to hide that, ask someone else to speak for you."

"I do not."

"Then we can speak."

She held out her hand for the wood. I gave it back. I had wanted to keep it, but she had brought it as evidence of a house that other people had maintained. It belonged with the rest of the things they had saved.

"Can I see it again?" I asked.

"When you come to hear what we have to say."

She wrapped it and turned toward the path. Then she stopped.

"Your mother would not have liked you standing here waiting for strangers to tell you whether you have a place to sleep."

"I have paid for one."

"Then she would have liked that better."

She left before I could ask another question.

Banu watched her go.

"That woman will speak clearly," he said.

"She always did."

"Then listen when she does."

He picked up his bag and went toward the boats bound upriver.

Sulad came back at dusk. He had earned his wage helping lift a hull from the mud. He carried a small piece of dried fish and a handful of rice wrapped in a leaf. He offered me half without ceremony.

I told him about the men who had asked whether I had soldiers, and about Banu's answer from Tondo.

"Will you speak for me?" I asked.

He looked at the river.

"I will say what I saw when we left. I will say what I have seen since we came back. I will not say your mother gave you a house that she told others to keep working."

"She was trying to protect it."

"Then that is what I will say."

I ate the rice. It had gone cold, but I was hungry enough not to care.

"Harun may leave soon," I said.

"He will leave when his trade is done."

"I have three nights here."

"Then we have three nights to learn whether you can stay longer."

"We?"

He looked at me.

"I have work at the nets tomorrow. I will hear what the men there say about the far bank. That does not mean I will fight for you."

"I know."

He nodded once. "Good."

The last light lay across the river. Boats were returning to the shelter, each one finding its place among the others. Beyond them, farther out, Harun's vessel was a dark shape against the bay. I could not see the people on deck. I knew he would be checking his goods and preparing for the next tide.

I had thought of home as a place that would wait for me. It had been a place where people worked, argued, repaired roofs and made promises in my absence. My mother had known that before I did.

I went back to the tool room and put my remaining gifts beside the mat. Tomorrow I would ask the keeper what work she had. I would ask Amaya who else had heard my mother's instructions. I would wait for the man from the far bank without sending for Harun's letter.

I still wanted Maynila. I had not stopped wanting it. But the river had given me three nights and the chance to be heard. I could begin there.
