By the time I could tie the receiving cord without looking at my fingers, Daran had stopped watching my hands. He watched where I stood instead.

“Move.”

I stepped back. Two men came through with a basket between them, their arms stiff under the handles. They set it beside the platform and went for the next. Daran pulled the cord across his knee and waited until the woman at the far end had seen the load.

There were mornings when she spoke more than anyone on the landing. This was not one of them. She lifted the covering from a stack of empty baskets, counted what was there, and told a boy to take three back to the boats. He brought two.

“Where is the third?” she asked.

“The man at the steps wanted one.”

“Did you give it to him?”

“He said he would bring it back.”

She held out her hand for the cord. Daran passed it to her. She untied the last knot, looked at the boy, and waited until he went down to fetch the basket.

I had learned that she would not spend breath chasing a promise she had not made.

A boat was coming alongside the outer steps. Its hull rubbed against the post, and a man at the stern pushed it away with his pole. The owner had already come ashore. He wore a clean cloth over one shoulder, folded carefully enough that its edge had not yet been darkened by the water. He was talking to the older man who kept our receiving cords.

I knew his voice before I knew his face. He had come the day before to ask when his goods could be brought up. The older man had told him to come when the men and the water allowed it.

Now he was here, and the men were busy.

“Those first,” the owner said, pointing to three covered baskets in the boat.

The woman looked toward them. “What is inside?”

“Bowls.”

“How many?”

He named a number. She asked whether he had counted them before they were covered.

“My people did.”

“Then your people can tell us what we are taking.”

He frowned. “I have already told you.”

She looked at the older man. He said nothing. The owner called to someone in the boat, and a younger carrier came up the steps with the first basket balanced against his side.

I moved to clear his path. The basket was wider than the space between the rail and the post. He turned it, shifted his feet, and brought the near handle past the wood. The covering was tied down with a length of dark cord. I saw the knot as it passed my shoulder.

The boat moved against the steps. The young man bent his knees. For a moment the basket tipped toward me. I reached out, but Daran caught my wrist.

“Let him find his feet.”

The carrier steadied himself. The basket came level again. He set it down on the planks with a sound that made the owner turn.

“What did you strike?” the owner demanded.

“Nothing.”

“I heard it.”

“So did I,” the carrier said. “It was the bottom against the wood.”

The owner came across the platform. “Lift it.”

The young man lifted one handle. The owner pulled the covering back himself. Beneath it were bowls nested with dry leaves, their rims separated by narrow twists of fiber. The first bowl looked whole. He took it out and set it beside the basket. The second had a pale line running from its rim toward the base.

He pressed his thumb against the line. A piece of the rim moved.

“Look,” he said.

The carrier leaned forward. The owner held the bowl away from him.

“You dropped it.”

“I set the basket down.”

“You struck it on the step.”

“I didn't.”

The owner turned to the woman. “You heard it.”

“I heard a basket set down.”

“You saw him lose his footing.”

“I saw the boat move.”

He looked past her and found me.

“You were standing here.”

“Yes.”

“You saw it.”

I looked at the bowl. The broken piece was still held in place by the owner's thumb. He could have removed it with almost no effort.

“I saw the basket tip,” I said.

“And strike the step.”

“I didn't see it strike.”

The owner stared at me as though I had answered in a language he did not know. Then he looked at the older man.

“This one is the grandson,” he said.

The older man gave a small nod.

“Then he knows the cost of a careless hand.”

I had heard that tone in Maynila. It was the voice men used when they wished to remind me who I was while asking me to agree with them. I was ashamed of how quickly I wanted to give him an answer he would like.

The owner set the cracked bowl on the platform. “The boy's payment can cover it.”

The carrier's face changed. Until then he had been looking at the basket and the man's hands. Now he looked at the woman.

“I carried what they gave me,” he said.

“Did you inspect it?” she asked.

“No. It was tied.”

“Did anyone tell you to open it?”

“No.”

The owner spread his hands. “If every carrier opens my goods, who will answer when something disappears?”

“Then you cannot ask him what was inside before he carried it,” she said.

“I can ask him not to break it.”

Daran had not moved from the cord. I expected him to speak. He had once stood before me while I insisted he had lost a bundle that was lying where I had failed to look. I had apologized, but I still felt the shape of the accusation in my mouth.

The younger carrier stood with both hands at his sides. There was a wet mark across his shoulder where the basket had rested. He did not try to touch the bowl again.

“Who tied the covering?” the woman asked.

“One of mine,” the owner said.

“Which one?”

“He is still aboard.”

“Then ask him to come up.”

The owner did not move.

“We are wasting the morning,” he said.

“We will waste more of it if the next basket is opened after the men have gone.”

He called sharply to the boat. A man looked up from the stern but did not come at once. The pole had caught between the hull and the post, and he was helping to free it.

The owner watched him struggle. Then he turned back to me.

“Your grandfather receives men who bring goods here,” he said.

“He receives many people.”

“And you are learning how their goods are handled.”

“I am learning where to stand.”

He smiled at that, though I had not meant it to amuse him.

“I have men who know the markets across the water,” he said. “Some have bought in Maynila.”

I thought of the words waiting in Siripada's house. My mother's name had been spoken before witnesses, and no one had yet said who might carry it. The owner had not offered to take a message. He had not even said that one of his men would go there again. I heard the place and began making a promise out of it.

“Do they go there now?” I asked.

“When there is reason.”

“Could I speak to one of them?”

He glanced at the cracked bowl.

“We can speak of many things when this is settled.”

I felt Daran look at me. He had been silent long enough that I had almost forgotten he was beside me.

The woman set the sound bowl back into the basket. She left the cracked one on the plank.

“Nothing else comes out of that covering yet,” she said. “I want the man who tied it, and I want the other two baskets kept aboard until we know what is being handed to us.”

The owner opened his mouth. She went on before he could speak.

“I can tell you what came onto these planks. I cannot tell you what happened inside your boat. If you want to keep back the carrier's payment, tell him that yourself. Do not say this house decided it.”

The older man lifted his cord and drew the newest knot toward him. He had counted one basket brought to the platform. He had not counted a broken bowl.

The owner looked at the cord, then at the cracked rim. I could see him deciding which of those two things would matter when he spoke of this morning elsewhere.

“Ask the grandson,” he said.

The woman turned toward me.

“What did you see before the basket reached the steps?”

I could see the water beyond the post. I had been watching the boat come in, but I had looked away to move out of the carrier's path. I remembered the basket turning against the rail, the sudden bend of the young man's knees, Daran's hand on my wrist. I had not seen the covering opened aboard. I had not seen the bowl before it came out broken.

“Not enough,” I said.

The owner made a small sound of disgust.

Daran loosened his hold on the cord. The younger carrier looked at me, not gratefully, but with the same impatience he had shown the owner.

“Did you see me drop it?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then say that.”

“I didn't see you drop it.”

He waited.

“I didn't see what happened before you brought it up,” I added.

The owner turned away. He called for the man who had tied the basket, this time by name. A head appeared above the boat's gunwale.

The woman told Daran to keep the first basket where it was. She asked me to move the empty ones out of the path before another carrier came up.

I took them one at a time. I could hear the owner questioning the man from the boat behind me. The young carrier was answering too. Their words rose over the water and crossed one another until I could no longer tell which man had spoken first.

I had kept my hands off the broken bowl. I had given no one a reason to thank me.

When I returned for the last empty basket, the younger carrier was still standing beside the first. The owner had not paid him, and the woman had not sent him away. The knot around the covering was whole. I had seen it pass my shoulder before the bowl was found.

That was all I knew about the knot.

* * *

The man who had tied the covering came up from the boat with a piece of leaf caught against his wet forearm. He was older than the carrier, with a narrow scar beside his mouth. The owner pointed to the cracked bowl before the man had both feet on the platform.

“Tell them how you packed it.”

“I packed the baskets,” he said.

“Were the bowls sound?”

“The ones I saw.”

The owner looked at the woman as if the answer had settled everything.

“How many did you see?” she asked.

The man looked back toward the boat. “I took them from the mat and put them in.”

“All of them?”

“There were others helping.”

“Who?”

He named two men. One was still aboard. The other had gone ashore before the unloading began. He spoke without lowering his eyes, but each answer made the owner's face harder.

The young carrier had moved into the narrow shade behind the post. He kept his hands under his arms. He was waiting for payment, and the waiting had begun to look like another kind of work.

The owner picked up the bowl and turned it until the crack faced us. The broken part of the rim lifted slightly beneath his thumb.

“This was worth something when it came to my boat.”

“Was it?” the woman asked.

“I paid for it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He put the bowl down too sharply. I expected the loose piece to fall, but it stayed in place.

The man who had tied the basket knelt and drew a handful of dry leaves from between the bowls. Some were crushed almost to dust. He held one twist up to the light.

“We put these between the rims,” he said. “If one struck another, it would be quieter.”

“Quieter?” the owner said.

“So they would not strike hard.”

“Then why is it broken?”

The man looked at the bowl. “I cannot tell you.”

The owner turned toward me again. He had done so often enough that I had begun to stand straighter whenever his head moved. I disliked knowing that he could still make me do it.

“You saw the carrier stumble,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And you heard the basket strike.”

“I heard it set down.”

He opened his hands. “You can choose the words. The sound will be the same.”

Daran, beside the cord keeper, gave a short cough. He was not ill. He was warning me that the owner had made a space for an answer.

I thought of the men the owner said knew Maynila. If I pleased him, I might speak to one. I could ask about the river, the markets, the house my mother had been left to govern. I might hear something true. He had offered none of this, yet I was already measuring how much of my answer I could give away.

“I did not see the bowl before the basket came ashore,” I said.

“You saw how it was handled.”

“Part of it.”

The owner glanced at the older man. “Is this how Siripada's grandson speaks for your house?”

The older man tightened a knot and laid the cord across his knee. “He has not spoken for the house.”

The owner stared at him.

“One basket came onto the platform,” the older man continued. “The covering was opened here. One cracked bowl was found. I can put those things into the count.”

“And the man who broke it?”

“I did not see that.”

The owner took a step toward him, then stopped. His cloth had slipped from one shoulder. He drew it back into place with slow care.

“I cannot stand here until every man remembers where he put his hands.”

“Then leave the other two baskets aboard,” the woman said. “Or bring them up and let us open them while your men are here.”

“I have another landing to reach.”

She looked at the sunlit water. “Your boat has not moved.”

He called for the two baskets to be brought up. No one obeyed. The man who had tied the first covering was still kneeling among the leaves. The younger carrier stayed in the shade.

“Get up,” the owner told him.

The carrier came forward, but stopped beyond the cracked bowl.

“My payment first.”

“You have damaged my goods.”

“You have not shown that.”

The owner turned to the woman. “Now he will not work.”

“He has already carried one basket,” she said.

“Badly.”

“Then pay him for the work you say he did, or tell him plainly that you will not.”

The carrier's jaw moved. I could see that he wanted to say more, but he was looking at the other men in the boat. Whatever happened to him would be repeated there.

The owner took a small piece of silver from his fold and held it out. The carrier did not move.

“That is not what you said I would have.”

“You cost me a bowl.”

“Then keep the bowl.”

The owner drew his hand back. For a moment I thought he would throw the silver into the water. Instead he put it away.

The man with the scar stood and brushed leaves from his knees. “The first basket was tied before the boat came alongside,” he said.

“Of course it was,” the owner snapped.

“We had to shift it once. A pole came in over the side when another boat passed us.”

The owner rounded on him. “Why did you not say that?”

“You did not ask when we shifted it.”

“Did the basket fall?”

“No.”

“Did it strike the side?”

“I was holding the other handle.”

The young carrier laughed once, without amusement.

The owner looked from one man to the next. There were now more people who had touched the basket than he had wanted us to know about, and none would give him the one answer he needed.

I looked at the dark cord still holding the covering in place. I had seen its knot before the basket was opened. It had seemed important then. Now I understood that a sound knot could hold a broken bowl as securely as a whole one.

Daran came to collect the empty baskets I had left by the wall. He put one in my hands.

“Take it around,” he said.

I carried it past the steps. From there I could see the boats at the lower landing. Sulad was on the far side of a narrow hull, helping two men pull a wet mat clear of the gunwale. He had his feet braced against the bank. He did not look up.

I wanted to call him. He might know the boatman, or the carrier, or someone who had seen the loading. I had already begun to make him useful in my mind.

I set the basket where Daran had pointed.

“Do you know that boat?” I asked him.

“Which?”

“The one with the bowls.”

“I know it is holding up the steps.”

“Could someone have seen it loaded?”

“Someone did.” He nodded toward the man with the scar. “He is standing there.”

I looked back. The owner had drawn the older cord keeper aside. Their heads were close. The carrier remained unpaid. The man who tied the covering was turning the cracked bowl in his hands, no longer certain he ought to touch it.

Daran took the empty basket from me.

“You have a question,” he said. “Ask it of the person who was there.”

“I did.”

“You asked what you wanted to know. That is different.”

He carried the basket away before I could answer.

When I returned to the platform, the owner had moved away from the others. He called me toward the steps.

“I was sharp with you,” he said.

I had not expected an apology, and did not know whether he meant to give one.

“I have goods waiting in other places,” he continued. “I have men who must be paid. When one thing breaks, every man says he saw nothing.”

I looked toward the carrier. “He says you have not paid him.”

“I know what he says.”

The owner lowered his voice.

“There is a man who comes through here from the western waters. He has bought goods near Maynila. If he comes again, I can tell you where to find him.”

“When?”

“I do not know. Men come when the wind and their business allow.”

“Would he carry a message?”

“You would have to ask him.”

It was less than I had hoped for, and more than anyone had offered me since I arrived. I hated how grateful I felt.

The owner glanced toward the platform. “I only need you to tell the old man what you heard.”

“I have.”

“You heard the basket hit.”

“I heard it put down.”

“Those are the same words to anyone who was not here.”

“They are not the same to the carrier.”

The owner watched me. The kindness left his face without any change in his voice.

“Then perhaps the man from Maynila will have no reason to speak to you.”

He went back to the boat. I stayed beside the steps until the woman called me to move the last empty basket. The owner had not promised to introduce me to anyone. He had only shown me how easily a hope could be taken back.

Behind me the carrier asked again for his payment. This time the man who had tied the basket stood beside him. The owner told them both to wait.

The other two covered baskets were still aboard. No one had yet brought them up.

* * *

The two baskets were still in the boat when the owner told his men to take them away.

The man at the stern looked toward the platform. He had his pole in the water, holding the hull clear of the post. The young carrier stood above him, empty-handed and unpaid.

“Where?” the man at the stern asked.

“Down to the other landing.”

“There are boats there.”

“Then wait your turn.”

The woman who watched our receiving platform put one foot against the first basket. Its covering lay open. The cracked bowl rested on a fold of cloth beside it, where the owner had left it.

“What of this one?” she asked.

“Put it back.”

“Whose hands will put it back?”

The owner looked at the young carrier. The carrier did not move.

“I have done my carrying,” he said.

“You have done damage.”

“You have not paid me.”

The man at the stern pushed against the post. The boat shifted a little and came back. Behind it another hull was waiting to reach the steps, its bow held off by a boy with a paddle. He called to ask whether we were finished. No one answered him.

The older cord keeper rose from his place beside Daran. He had made a knot when the first basket reached the platform. He had not made another.

“If you take it back,” he told the owner, “I will say that you took it back.”

“And the bowl?”

“We found it cracked when the covering was opened here.”

“Because he struck the basket.”

The old man looked at me. I wished he had not.

“I heard the basket put down,” I said.

“You have said that,” the owner answered.

He was right. I had said it so often that I could hear myself becoming tiresome. The carrier's wages had not moved any nearer to his hand.

Daran brought the empty baskets away from the steps. He had to turn sideways to pass the owner. The owner did not make room for him until Daran stopped and waited.

“Open the next basket here,” the woman said.

The owner shook his head. “You have already found a reason to keep one.”

“We have found a broken bowl. I would rather find the others whole while the men who packed them can see.”

The man with the scar, who had tied the covering, called from the boat. “I can bring it.”

The young carrier turned toward him.

“You can carry that one,” he said. “I will not.”

The man with the scar put a hand on the second basket's handle. Another man joined him. They brought it up together, slowly enough that the boy with the waiting boat began to complain again. They set it beside the first. The owner watched their hands until the weight was on the planks.

The woman did not touch the covering. She told the packer to untie it himself.

He loosened the cord and lifted the mat. Dry leaves lay between the bowls, curled into small brown pockets. The top two were whole. So were the next three. The packer took them out one at a time and laid them in a row.

The owner folded his arms.

“Now you have seen how they were packed.”

The woman took a leaf from the bottom of the basket. It had darkened along one edge. She rubbed it between her fingers, then let it fall.

“Was this wet when you packed it?”

The man with the scar leaned close. “I don't remember.”

“Did water come into the boat?”

“Water comes into every boat.”

The owner gave a short laugh. “Now we are to blame the water for a broken bowl.”

I looked from the damp leaf to the cracked rim on the cloth. I wanted the leaf to mean something. Perhaps the first basket had been soaked. Perhaps its padding had slipped before the young man lifted it. I had not seen the leaves in that basket before the owner pulled them aside. A wet leaf would not tell me when a bowl had broken.

Daran came to stand beside me.

“You are looking for a mark,” he said.

“There might be one.”

“There are many marks on things that have traveled.”

He went back to his cord.

The man at the stern called that he could no longer keep the boat in place without blocking the one behind. The owner told him to move farther out. The second basket stood open now, its bowls spread across the platform. The man with the scar looked at his hands, as if he had only just understood how long he might be needed.

The owner turned to the young carrier.

“You see what your refusal has done? We are still here.”

The carrier looked toward the bowls. “I did not refuse to carry the first.”

“Then finish your work.”

“My payment.”

The owner drew the same small piece of silver from his fold. He held it between two fingers.

The young man glanced at it and looked away.

“You told me more before we came ashore.”

“You had not broken anything before we came ashore.”

The man with the scar stopped lifting bowls. I saw the carrier notice. It mattered to him that someone else had heard the words.

I moved toward the owner, though I had no idea what I could ask him to do. I could have offered my own food. It would not have paid the promised wage, and the man might have taken it as payment for my silence. I could have called on Siripada's name. I had no permission to make him answer for a stranger's basket.

A wet mat brushed my ankle.

“Move your foot,” Sulad said.

He and another man were carrying the mat between them. They had to cross the platform to reach the space behind the receiving house. I stepped aside. The other man went ahead, pulling more of the weight than Sulad could comfortably hold.

Sulad set his end down once they were clear.

“That boat has taken our place at the steps,” he said, nodding toward the hull. “We have another mat to bring in.”

“There is a dispute.”

“I can see the dispute.”

“The carrier has not been paid.”

Sulad looked at the young man. “Will you pay him?”

“I cannot.”

“Then don't make him wait to hear you say it.”

I felt my face grow warm. “I was trying to understand what happened.”

“He knows what happened to his payment.”

The man who worked with Sulad called for him. Sulad picked up his end of the mat and went. He did not stay to tell me what I ought to do. I watched him carry the weight through the shade, keeping his side level with the other man's.

The woman told the packer to return the sound bowls to their places. He began to nest them among the leaves. No one asked the young carrier to help.

The owner stood close enough to hear the waiting boatman's complaints. He could take his goods elsewhere, but the cracked bowl would travel with him. The carrier could leave, but his payment would remain in the owner's fold. The receiving house could refuse to name the cause, but our steps would still be blocked.

The owner turned to the older cord keeper.

“I want the count to show that one of my bowls was broken here.”

“It was found broken here.”

“Are you playing with words?”

“No.”

“Then put it in the count.”

The old man held up the cord. “One basket brought up. One cracked bowl found when it was opened. That is what the knot holds.”

“And who pays for it?”

“I cannot put a man into a knot.”

The owner looked as if he might strike the cord from his hand. Instead he reached for the cracked bowl. The loose piece of rim fell away. It hit the platform with a sound smaller than I expected.

Everyone looked down.

The owner bent to retrieve it. His clean shoulder cloth touched the planks.

“Now it is in two pieces,” he said.

No one answered. The bowl had been cracked before he lifted it. I had watched him lift it. I could not tell whether that made the loss greater or merely easier to see.

The woman crouched and gathered the small piece in her palm. She set it inside the bowl.

“If you want it back in your basket,” she said, “put it there yourself. If you want us to hold it until you decide, say so while these men are here.”

The owner looked at the two baskets. His boat had drifted away from the steps. The man at the stern was holding it with difficulty. The waiting boy had brought his own boat nearer.

“Hold the first,” the owner said at last. “I will take the second.”

“For how long?”

“Until I return.”

“That is no answer.”

“Until the next tide,” he said.

The woman looked at the older cord keeper. He nodded once. She turned to the owner.

“Then let everyone hear it. The first basket stays here until the next tide. Its cracked bowl stays with it. You take the second. Nothing has been decided about the cause.”

The owner did not repeat her words. He called for the second basket to be covered and carried back. The man with the scar tied it. The young carrier stood where he had stood before.

As the men lifted the basket, the owner spoke to him without turning.

“You will have your payment when I know what this has cost me.”

The young man answered so quietly that I almost missed it.

“Then I will be here when you know.”

The owner went down to the boat. He passed close enough to me that I could have stopped him with a hand. I did not. He looked at me once, and I understood that the man who might know Maynila had become less likely to speak to me. I could not even say that my refusal had bought the carrier anything.

When the boat finally cleared the steps, the waiting boy came in at once. His hull struck the post. He swore, pushed away, and tried again.

Daran handed me a length of cord.

“Stay with that basket,” he said. “If the owner returns, bring the woman before anything is moved.”

“Is that all?”

“It is a basket he has left in our keeping. Do you want to lose it?”

“No.”

“Then keep it.”

I sat beside the open covering. The young carrier sat a little farther away, where he could see the water. Neither of us had been given what he wanted. I knew less about the broken bowl than I had hoped to know, but I knew exactly where the basket was.

The next boat came alongside. Men began to lift its goods past us. I moved my knees out of their way and kept the cord in my hand.

* * *

The boy from the waiting boat brought us a basket of his own before the owner of the bowls had gone far enough to be out of sight. His was filled with lengths of split wood, each piece dark where it had rested in the bilge. He put it down where the cracked bowl had been.

“Not there,” the woman said.

He lifted it again. The bottom dripped onto the planks.

“Where, then?”

“Where someone can pass.”

He looked along the platform. There was scarcely a place left that did not belong to somebody's goods. Daran came and showed him a patch of dry boards beside the wall. The boy took the basket there and began laying the wood out to be counted.

The young carrier who had been refused his wages watched him work. He had stopped asking the owner for payment once the boat moved away. He had not stopped watching the water.

I sat with the first basket of bowls and the cord Daran had given me. The cracked bowl lay inside it, with the loose piece of rim nested against its side. I could see the place where the owner had pressed his thumb. I could not tell whether he had made the crack worse.

A line of wetness crept across the board beneath the basket. At first I thought it came from the boy's wood. Then I saw water lifting between the planks.

“Daran.”

He was helping the boy arrange his pieces by length. He glanced over.

“The water is coming up.”

“It does that.”

“It will reach the basket.”

He came closer, bent, and touched the board with two fingers. When he straightened, he looked toward the woman.

“Move the first basket?”

She had been watching a pair of men bring sacks through the rear passage. She came at once. The water had not reached the covering, but the bottom of the basket was damp.

“Into the room,” she said.

I put my hands on the handles.

“Wait,” Daran said.

I let go. He did not explain. He looked at the older keeper of the cords, who was sitting beyond the water jar.

“The owner left one basket until the next tide,” Daran told him. “We are taking it inside before the water reaches it.”

The old man came over slowly. He looked into the basket and counted the bowls that could be seen without lifting them.

“You did not open it again?” he asked.

“No,” I said.

“The covering was left open,” the woman said. “The owner saw that.”

The keeper looked at the carrier. “You were here when he left?”

“Yes.”

“Did you see anyone touch the broken bowl after that?”

“The grandson sat beside it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

The carrier looked at me. He had been watching the water as often as I had.

“I saw no one take it out,” he said.

The keeper nodded, but he did not reach for a cord.

“Now move it.”

Daran took one handle. I took the other. The basket was lighter than the loads I had carried in the mornings before this dispute, but it was awkward because we had to keep it level. The cracked rim knocked softly against another bowl when we lifted.

“Slow,” the woman said.

We stopped. Daran looked at the broken piece. It had shifted but had not fallen.

“Is it worse?” I asked.

“I do not know.”

We carried it through the narrow passage. The room beyond smelled of damp matting and the sweet dust of old packing leaves. Baskets were stacked along the wall, some empty and some covered. The woman pointed to a low stand.

“Here. Nothing above it.”

We set the basket down. I had thought the room dry. A dark line ran along the base of the wall, where water had reached it on other days. The stand was higher than the line.

The woman looked at the broken bowl, then at the passage.

“Tell the owner where it is when he returns.”

“I will.”

“Tell him we moved it.”

“I will.”

“And tell him why.”

I nodded.

“Do not tell him we kept it untouched,” she said. “We did not.”

She went back to the platform before I could answer.

Daran knelt to check the basket's bottom. A few wet fibers clung to his fingers.

“You were right about the water,” he said.

“I almost moved it alone.”

“You would have moved it to keep it safe.”

“And then?”

“And then someone would have asked whether you broke it while moving it.”

He rubbed the wet fibers from his hand. “Now they can ask both of us.”

“That is not much better.”

“No. But there are two of us.”

He rose and went to help the men in the rear passage. I stayed by the basket. Outside, the boy was calling out the lengths of his wood. I heard the older keeper repeat each count. Their work had not stopped because a merchant was angry.

The carrier appeared in the doorway. He did not enter.

“Did you move it?”

“Yes.”

“Did it break more?”

“I don't know.”

He looked past me at the bowl.

“I should have watched.”

“You were watching.”

“Not every moment.”

He leaned against the doorpost. His shoulder was bare where the carrying strap had rubbed it. There was a narrow patch of skin near his collarbone that looked raw.

“Will you work for him again?” I asked.

“For whom?”

“The owner.”

He looked at me as though I had asked whether he would work for the tide.

“If he pays what he said.”

“And if he doesn't?”

“Someone else may need hands.”

“Here?”

“Here, or somewhere else.” He glanced toward the platform. “You have a place already.”

“For now.”

“Then for now you have a place.”

I had been thinking of my mother's message again. It had been spoken and witnessed, but it still waited in Siripada's house for a man who might carry it. The merchant had offered the possibility of a contact and taken it away when I would not give him the words he wanted. I had lost a hope. This man had lost the use of his hands for the better part of a morning, and might lose the wage he had already earned.

“Do you have someone waiting for the payment?” I asked.

“Do you?”

The question startled me.

“Yes,” I said, before I thought. Then I shook my head. “No one waiting here.”

He nodded once. He did not ask whom I had meant.

A man came through the passage with a coil of rope over his shoulder. The carrier moved aside to let him pass. When he settled back against the post, he was farther from me.

“If the owner says you broke it,” I began.

“He has said it.”

“If he says we broke it moving it inside—”

“Then he will have two stories.”

I looked at the bowl. The little piece of rim lay where the woman had placed it, inside the curve.

“Which one will people believe?”

“Whichever one they hear from the man who pays them.”

He said it without anger. I wanted to contradict him. I had nothing with which to do so.

From the platform came a sharp call. The woman was asking who had left a wet sack across the passage. Daran answered. The sack was moved. The boy with the wood began counting again.

The carrier straightened.

“I will go to the other landing.”

“Will you come back?”

“When the owner does.”

“How will you know?”

“He said the next tide. Everyone here knows when that is.”

“I can tell him you waited.”

He studied me for a moment.

“You can tell him I waited until now,” he said. “You cannot tell him I waited when I wasn't here.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He went down the passage. I heard his steps on the planks and then lost them among the men carrying the sacks.

I remained with the basket until Daran returned. He brought a narrow strip of matting and put it beneath the wet side.

“Did the carrier leave?” he asked.

“He is going to the other landing.”

“He has work to find.”

“He said he would come back.”

“Perhaps he will.”

Daran turned the basket slightly so that the damp side faced the open air. He did not touch the bowls.

“You can go and eat,” he said.

“The woman told me to stay with it.”

“She told you to keep it. I can keep it while you eat.”

I hesitated. I had begun to treat sitting beside the basket as if it were a duty no one else could perform. The thought embarrassed me, but I still wanted him to ask me to stay.

“Did you eat?” I asked.

“Before the boy brought the wood.”

I stood. My knees were stiff from the boards.

At the outer steps the water had risen high enough to wash over the lowest plank. The boy's boat had gone. Another had taken its place. Men were moving without shouting, passing loads along a line that left the center of the platform clear. The woman stood where she could see both the landing and the rear passage.

She gave me a small portion of rice wrapped in leaf. I ate beside the wall and watched her settle a disagreement over which sacks belonged to which boat. She asked the men to identify their own loads, then sent each to a different place. No one invoked Siripada's name.

When I finished, she was still working.

“The basket is inside,” I told her.

“I know. I sent you there.”

“Daran is with it now.”

“I know that too.”

I held the empty leaf between my fingers.

“The carrier went to find work.”

“He is free to go.”

“He says he will return at the next tide.”

“Then we will see him if he does.”

“What if the owner comes first?”

“Then we tell him what happened here.”

“He may say we broke it.”

She looked at me. “Did we?”

“We moved it.”

“That is what we did. Do not make a second quarrel before the first has ended.”

She took the leaf from my hand and pointed me toward the place where the waste was kept. I went.

By the time I returned, a boat was approaching the outer steps against the current. Its stern moved sideways before the poleman brought it straight. I recognized the owner's folded shoulder cloth before I could see his face.

The woman saw him too. She called Daran's name, then looked at me.

“Bring the keeper,” she said. “And remember where the basket was when we found the water.”

I went to fetch the old man. The owner was already calling from the boat, asking why his goods were no longer on the platform.

* * *

The owner reached the platform before I returned with the keeper. He had stepped from his boat without waiting for the poleman to bring it close, and one hem of his cloth was wet. The woman stood between him and the passage to the receiving room.

“I left it here,” he said.

“And we kept it here,” she answered. “The water came through the boards.”

“I can see the water.”

“Then you can see why we moved it.”

He looked past her, toward the room. “Who opened my basket?”

“It was open when you left.”

“Who touched the bowls?”

She did not answer at once. She waited until the keeper and I had come close enough to hear.

“Daran and the grandson carried it. I told them to. We looked inside before it was lifted. The carrier was there.”

“Where is he?”

“He went to seek work,” I said.

The owner turned to me. “He left?”

“He said he would come back at the next tide.”

“And you believed him?”

“I heard him say it.”

The owner gave a short, tired laugh. He had been angry when he left; now he seemed more worn than angry, as though the trouble had traveled with him to the other landing and back.

“Take me to it,” he said.

The woman led us through the passage. Daran rose from beside the basket. He had placed a strip of matting beneath its damp side. The little piece of rim still lay inside the cracked bowl.

The owner crouched, then stopped before his fingers touched anything.

“This was not how I left it.”

“The mat is new,” Daran said.

“The bowl has moved.”

“It shifted when we carried the basket,” I said. “We stopped when we heard it touch another.”

The owner looked up. “So you admit it struck another bowl.”

“Yes.”

“Then how can you say you did not make the damage worse?”

“I cannot say that.”

For a moment he looked satisfied. I felt the old impulse to add words until I had made myself understood. Daran was watching me. I kept quiet.

The woman pointed to the broken piece inside the bowl.

“That fell away while you were holding it on the platform,” she said.

“I remember.”

“The crack was there before you lifted it.”

“Do you remember that too?”

“I was there.”

The owner looked at the keeper. The old man stood just inside the doorway, holding the cord with the single knot for the basket.

“What will you tell people?” the owner asked him.

“That you brought one basket here. We opened it and found a cracked bowl. You left it until the next tide. We moved it out of the water while you were gone.”

“And you will not say who broke it.”

“I did not see who broke it.”

“You are all very careful about what you did not see.”

The keeper rubbed the cord between his fingers. It had grown dark where the knot had been tightened.

“I have a knot for the basket,” he said. “It does not tell me how a bowl was cracked.”

The owner stood. “If my men are to bring goods here again, I need to know who will answer when something is spoiled.”

The woman looked toward the doorway, where a boy was trying to pass with a bundle of split wood. She moved aside to let him through before answering.

“When your men bring goods, we can tell you what comes into our hands. We cannot answer for what happened before.”

“That will be a comfort when I lose another bowl.”

“You can take this one now.”

He did not move.

I began to understand that he had returned for more than the basket. If he took it away, he would have the broken bowl and his own account of its breaking. He wanted an account from this house as well. The woman would not give him one that named the carrier.

“Did the carrier come back while I was gone?” the owner asked.

“No,” Daran said.

“Then perhaps he knows what he did.”

“He told us where he was going,” I said.

“You know where he went?”

“He said the other landing.”

“Did you see him there?”

“No.”

The owner spread his hands. “Then you do not know where he is.”

I wanted to say that a man might leave a place without fleeing it. I had left Maynila myself. That would have been a poor answer. The carrier had gone to find wages. I had left with a boat and the hope that my grandfather would receive me.

“No,” I said. “I do not know where he is now.”

The owner turned back to the woman. “Then why should I pay him before I know what he has cost me?”

“Because he carried your basket,” she said.

“And broke the bowl.”

“That has not been established.”

“Must everything be established before a man is paid?”

“Ask the man you owe.”

The owner looked at her for a long moment. She had not raised her voice. The boy with the wood returned through the passage and waited behind us. There was no space to pass until the owner stepped aside.

Outside, someone called that the current was turning. The poleman answered from the boat. The owner glanced toward the water.

“I have another load waiting,” he said. “My men will not stand here all day.”

“Neither will ours,” the woman said.

He bent over the basket again. This time he took the cracked bowl in both hands and held it where the light from the passage fell across the rim. I saw a narrow line running down its side. I had seen that line before. I could not tell whether it was longer now.

He placed the bowl back among the leaves.

“I will take the basket,” he said. “But I want the grandson to say, before these men, that it struck another bowl when he carried it.”

“I will say that,” I answered.

“And that the piece is loose now.”

“It was loose when you lifted it earlier. It fell onto the platform.”

He looked at me. “You have chosen which part of the morning to remember.”

“I remember both.”

“Then say both.”

I did. Daran told them he had held the other handle. The keeper said he had seen the basket before it was moved, though he had not watched every step through the passage. The woman said she had ordered it carried out of the water. Each of us spoke for a different part of the work. None of us could give the owner the one sentence he wanted.

The poleman called again.

The owner pulled the covering over the bowls. He did not tie it.

“Fetch someone to carry this.”

The woman looked toward the platform. “Our men are moving other goods. You may ask them when they are free.”

“He is standing here.” The owner pointed to me.

I felt my face grow hot. I could lift the basket. I wanted him to see that I could do something useful after spending so long saying what I could not know.

The woman did not look at me.

“He is keeping the basket until it leaves our hands,” she said. “If he helps you carry it, Daran will take the other handle. We will all see it go.”

The owner stepped away. “Very well.”

Daran and I lifted the basket. The wet matting clung to its bottom, and we had to set it down once to peel the strip free. The owner watched. We carried it through the passage and onto the platform, where the water had begun to fall below the lowest board.

The young carrier was standing at the far end.

He had returned without the strap he had worn across his shoulder. His hands were empty. He watched us set the basket down.

“You came back,” the owner said.

“You said the next tide.”

“I said you would be paid when I knew the cost.”

“Do you know?”

The owner glanced at the cracked bowl beneath the covering.

“I know more than I did.”

The carrier looked at the basket, then at the owner. “You know I carried it.”

“You were paid to carry it safely.”

“I was to be paid when I brought it up.”

The owner reached into his fold and brought out the same small piece of silver he had shown earlier. He held it toward the carrier.

“Take this, and we will call the matter finished.”

The carrier did not reach for it.

“That is less than you said.”

“It is more than you would have if I charged you for the bowl.”

The man looked at the silver. His shoulder was raw from the strap. I could see how much it cost him to leave the piece in the owner's hand.

“If I take it,” he asked, “will you say I agreed I broke your bowl?”

The owner closed his fingers.

“You want payment and an argument.”

“I want the payment you promised.”

“Then you will wait.”

The carrier looked toward the other landing. A boat was being drawn in there. Men were calling for hands. He could go now and perhaps earn something before dark. If he stayed, the owner might still refuse him.

I could have asked the woman to speak for him. She already had. I could have offered to carry another load in his place. That would have made his refusal easier for the owner to ignore.

The carrier made his own choice.

“I will not carry your next basket,” he said. “You can find another man.”

The owner put the silver away. He told the poleman to bring the boat close.

Daran handed me the cord. “Hold this until the basket is aboard.”

The carrier stepped aside, but did not leave. He watched Daran and another man take the basket down. I stood where I could see the handles, the gunwale, and the owner's face. When the basket was settled in the boat, Daran looked up. I held the cord for the keeper.

He loosened the knot while I kept the loose end.

The owner had his basket again. The carrier still had no wages. The woman had other goods coming up the steps, and Daran had gone to meet them. The merchant's boat began to turn with the current.

The young carrier watched it move away.

“Will you follow him?” I asked.

“Why?”

“For the money.”

“He knows where I work.”

“Will you work here tomorrow?”

“If there is work.”

He turned toward the other landing. I nearly asked his name. We had spent the morning speaking about his hands, his wages, and what he might have done to a bowl. I had never thought to ask what he was called.

“Wait,” I said.

He stopped.

“What do they call you?”

“Idris,” he said.

“Idris,” I repeated.

He nodded, then went.

I stood with the empty cord in my hand until the keeper reached for it. There was no knot left for the basket. There was still a man to whom the owner owed money.

* * *

The keeper's fingers closed around the cord. I was still holding one end.

“The basket is gone,” he said.

I let go. He laid the loose length beside his knee. There was a damp place on my palm where the knot had rested.

The owner’s boat was already past the farther post. Idris had gone toward the other landing, where men were calling for hands. I watched until I could no longer distinguish him from the people moving between the boats.

“Is there work?” Daran asked.

I turned. He had two empty baskets on his arm.

“For me?”

“For anyone standing in the passage.”

I took the upper basket. Its handle had been repaired with a strip of fiber that cut against my thumb. Daran went ahead of me through the narrow opening, and I followed without asking what he intended to do with the baskets. I had learned where the empties belonged.

The woman was clearing the platform for the next boat. The boy who had brought the split wood had left a short piece across the walking boards. She picked it up and placed it with the rest.

“Where is the carrier?” she asked.

“Gone to find work,” I said.

“Did he get his payment?”

“No.”

She nodded once and pointed to the baskets in our hands.

“Those belong against the wall. Leave the middle open.”

I put mine where she indicated. When I turned back, the keeper was winding the cord he had taken from me. He laid it with the other lengths, where I could no longer tell it from the rest.

I went to the steps.

The next boat came in slowly, its bow low under two heavy bundles. A man in a red-brown cloth stood above them with his feet apart. He called to the woman that his load must be taken first because he had another place to reach before the water fell.

“There are men before you,” she answered.

“My people were told to come here.”

“By whom?”

He named a man I did not know. The woman looked at the keeper. He shook his head.

“Wait until there is space,” she said.

The man stared at the cleared boards. “There is space.”

“There is room for a man to walk. That is not the same thing.”

A carrier on the boat laughed. The man turned and said something to him too low for me to hear. The carrier stopped laughing.

Daran came to stand beside me.

“Do you know whose bundles those are?” I asked.

“No.”

“He says they were expected.”

“Then someone may know.”

He stepped around me and went to help a different boat come alongside. He had given me an answer without giving me a task. I could hear the woman asking the newcomer to wait, and the man asking whether she knew who had sent him.

I had once been able to say my mother’s name and watch people move aside. Here I knew what that felt like from the other end of the boards.

The woman called for a boy to take a message through the rear passage. He had already gone to fetch drinking water. She looked around, saw me, and pointed.

“Find the man who sent these bundles. Ask what he expects us to receive. Come back with his answer.”

“Which man?”

“The one whose name the boatman gave us.”

“I don't know him.”

“Then ask where he is.”

She repeated the name. I repeated it until she was satisfied I had heard it correctly.

“Am I to tell him we will take the bundles?” I asked.

“No.”

“What if he says we must?”

“Come back and tell me.”

The boatman was watching us. He looked pleased when he saw me turn toward the rear passage, as though I had been sent to make the woman change her mind. I wanted to tell him that I had been sent only to ask a question. I went before he could stop me.

Beyond the platform, the passage opened between two houses. Someone had hung wet cloth over a pole at shoulder height. I bent beneath it and came out where three men were sorting lengths of timber. The first man I asked had heard the name but knew no one by it. The second told me to try the shaded yard behind the larger house. The third said the man had gone upriver yesterday.

“Yesterday?” I asked.

“That is when I saw him.”

“Did he come back?”

“I did not see him come back.”

I was about to turn away when a woman carrying a shallow tray passed us.

“If you mean the man with the bundles,” she said, “his brother was here this morning.”

“Where is he?”

“He went toward the boats.”

“Which boats?”

She shifted the tray against her hip. “I cannot walk the landing for you.”

I thanked her and went back.

The boatman was still waiting. He had pulled his bundles farther from the bow so that the men could work around them. The woman stood with her hands at her sides, listening to a carrier from another vessel explain which of his sacks belonged together.

When she had finished with him, I gave her the answers I had found.

“Did you speak to the man himself?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then we still do not know what he expects.”

“The boatman says he was sent.”

“I heard him.”

She looked toward the shaded yard, then at the water. The next load was already coming up the steps.

“Stand by the rear passage,” she said. “When someone comes to claim those bundles, bring him to me. Do not take them into the house.”

The boatman heard her.

“So I must wait for a boy to find my owner?”

“You must wait for someone who can tell us what we are receiving.”

He gave me a hard look. I wanted to defend her decision. It was not mine to defend, and I had no answer that would shorten his wait.

I went where she had pointed.

The place beside the passage was narrow. Men passed with sacks against their shoulders, and each time I stepped aside I found myself standing in someone else’s path. I watched Daran cross the platform twice before I understood where he put his feet when a load came through. There was a patch of board near the wall that stayed clear if I stood sideways. From there I could see the waiting boat and the passage without forcing anyone around me.

It was a smaller place than I had imagined when the woman told me to stand there.

Idris came up the outer steps after the sun had moved past the roof.

He carried no basket. His shoulder had been covered with a strip of cloth, and the edge of it was dark with sweat. He stopped when he saw me.

“Did you find work?” I asked.

“For one load. They had more hands than they needed.”

“Did they pay you?”

“For the one I carried.”

I wanted to ask how much. I could not think of a reason he should tell me.

“Has the owner come back?” he asked.

“No.”

“He may not.”

I looked toward the water. “Will you ask for your money tomorrow?”

“If I see him.”

“What if he refuses again?”

Idris watched a pair of men bring a heavy sack through the passage. One of them shifted the weight to the other before they reached the steps.

“Then he refuses,” he said. “You think I can spend all my days following one man?”

I had no answer. The one load he had found would buy him something, but I could not know what. The owner had taken his basket and gone, and Idris had to decide how much more of his time to give him.

The woman came over while we were speaking.

“Can you carry?” she asked Idris.

He glanced at his covered shoulder. “If the load is not against this side.”

“We have empty baskets to move, and two men who will take the heavier ones. Daran will show you.”

“For how much?”

She named a payment. Idris asked whether it was for the work or for the time until the next tide.

“For the baskets I show you,” she said.

“How many?”

She looked toward the wall, counted, and told him.

He considered it, then nodded. She sent him to Daran.

I had wanted to ask her to give Idris work. She had asked him what he could do, and he had made his own bargain before I spoke.

When he passed me with the first empty basket, he looked at the narrow board where I stood.

“Is that your work?”

“For now.”

“Then keep out of the way.”

He smiled only after he had gone past. I could not tell whether he was making a joke of Daran’s instruction or giving me the same one.

Farther along the landing, Sulad was helping a man replace a plank. The old one lay in the mud beside them. He had his knee against the new board while the other man drove a wooden peg through a hole. When the peg caught, Sulad shifted his weight and the board settled into place.

I waited until he came for water.

“You were here all morning?” he asked.

“Most of it.”

“I heard there was trouble with bowls.”

“There was.”

“Did you break one?”

“No.”

“Did you find out who did?”

“No.”

He drank from the jar and handed it back to the boy who had brought it. His hands were dark with wet wood.

“I am going farther along the bank tomorrow,” he said. “The man with the plank has another platform to mend.”

“For how long?”

“He doesn't know yet.”

“Will you come back here?”

“When the work is done.”

I almost asked him to find out whether the merchant’s boat would return. He had been working near the other landing. He might hear more than I could. The question rose in my mouth before I remembered what he had said the night I asked him to stay at Siripada’s house.

“Will you come when you can?” I asked instead.

“If I can. You know where I am going.”

“I don't know the place.”

He pointed along the bank and described the turn by a house with a high roof. I listened until I could have found it without him.

“Your message?” he asked.

“Still waiting.”

“And mine?”

“I have not gone back to the witnesses.”

“Then it is still yours alone.”

I looked at him. He had not raised his voice.

“I said I would ask them to add it.”

“I know what you said.”

The man with the plank called. Sulad took the jar back to the boy and went to work. He had told me where he would be. He had not promised to wait there for me.

The woman found me at the passage when the shadows had begun to reach the lower steps.

“Did anyone come for the two bundles?”

“No.”

“Did anyone try to bring them inside?”

“The boatman asked again. I told him you had not agreed.”

“Good. Tomorrow, if he returns, send him to me. If someone else comes, ask his name and what he has been sent to collect. Do not decide for me.”

“Am I to stand here again?”

“Until I give you something else to do.”

She looked down at the board where I had planted my feet.

“And keep that way clear. You found the right place.”

I wanted to ask whether she would tell Siripada. She had already turned toward the incoming boat.

Daran came to collect the last empty basket. Idris was beside him, carrying another. They set them down against the wall and counted what they had moved. The woman paid Idris the amount they had agreed. He looked at it before putting it away.

It was not the silver the merchant owed him. He did not say it was enough.

He nodded to Daran, passed me on the clear board, and went down the steps.

The waiting boat with the two bundles had turned away without unloading. The man aboard called that he would return when someone knew what to do with his goods. The woman did not answer. She was watching another vessel find the steps.

I looked once toward the bank where Sulad worked. He was kneeling over the plank with his back to me. The message to my mother was still in Siripada’s house, and I had not carried Sulad’s words to the witnesses. I had no word from Maynila and no way to know whether the merchant’s western contact would ever come.

At the passage, the boards were clear. I stood sideways while the next men brought their load through.

When the last man passed, I stayed where I was. Another boat was coming in.
