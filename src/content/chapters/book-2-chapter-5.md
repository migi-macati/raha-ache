I had been given a clean piece of wood for the names of the missing. By morning I had rubbed it so often with my thumb that the edge was smooth.

There was only one name on it.

Bakar had agreed to take his boat to the lower bank, where a man who tended fish traps said he might have heard shouting the day Hamzah's boat was taken. He had not agreed to search the inner passage. He had not agreed to take me.

I waited while he and Tuma loaded three jars. The man who owned them had come down twice to ask when they would reach the outer landing. He was there a third time when Bakar saw me.

"If you have another route for us," Bakar said, "you can tell the owner."

"I have no route."

"Then what do you want?"

"Jamil said I could ask whether you would take me."

"He said you could ask?"

"Yes."

Bakar turned to the jar owner. "You heard him. He has come to ask. I have not said yes."

The owner put a hand on the nearest jar. "Will he make us late?"

"He might."

"Then he can stay here."

I wanted to say that we were looking for a man who had been taken or drowned, and that three jars could wait. I knew Bakar would ask me whether I owned them. The owner was already watching my mouth.

"I will not ask you to stop," I said. "If you have to pass the bank to make your delivery, I can look from the boat."

"And if you see something?"

"I will tell you."

"You will not tell me to turn?"

"No."

Tuma lowered the last jar into its bed of folded matting. He had heard enough to look at me without smiling.

"He can hold the cover down," he said. "It keeps lifting at the corner."

Bakar inspected the cover, though he had tied it himself. Then he pointed to a place between the jars.

"Sit there. If I tell you to move, move."

I climbed aboard. The jar owner watched until we were clear of the post. I could still see him standing with his hands on his hips when the houses hid the landing.

The water was lower than it had been the morning we lost Hamzah's boat. Mud showed on the roots along the banks. Tuma took the forward pole. Bakar steered us past a line of stakes set out from a house, then held the boat away from a shallow place where two children were gathering shellfish. I had seen the stakes before, but I had never known how far they ran beneath the water.

I kept looking for a place where a man might have climbed ashore.

"Not every opening is a landing," Bakar said.

"I know."

"Then stop leaning across the jars to count them."

I sat back. The corner of the cover lifted. I caught it before the wind could turn it over.

At the outer landing the buyers were not ready. One had gone to find the man who would carry the jars uphill. The other argued that the clay had been left in the sun too long and should cost less. Bakar waited while the owner of the jars, who had come by another boat, argued back. Tuma went to find drinking water. No one asked me to help settle the price.

I took the clean wood from my belt and looked at the single name.

Nasir's sister had said that if we learned where he was, we were to tell her ourselves. She had not asked us to bring back a story. I wondered what I would say if Bakar returned without going to the place where the man at the fish traps had heard voices.

The jar owner came over while I was still looking at the wood.

"You wrote my three jars on that?"

"No."

"Then what?"

I showed him Nasir's name. He read nothing from the mark, but he recognized the space I had left beside it.

"The boy from Hamzah's boat?"

"Yes."

"I heard he had gone into the trees."

"Who told you?"

"Someone at the market."

"Who?"

He frowned. "Do you want my jars or my memory?"

"Your memory."

"Then it is worth no more than that. I did not see him."

He went back to the argument. I made no mark beside Nasir's name.

When the jars had been carried off, Bakar asked Tuma how much water remained. Tuma showed him the jar he had filled. They spoke about the tide, the time needed to reach the fish traps, and whether the buyers might have another load waiting. Only when they had finished did Bakar look at me.

"We can go as far as the outer bank," he said. "Not beyond it."

"Thank you."

"You have not heard what it costs me yet."

I had thought the trip was already being made. I looked toward the empty place where the jars had stood.

"There is another load I could have taken before the water fell," Bakar said. "I will miss it if we go."

"I can pay you for the time."

"With what?"

I named what I had. It was less than he would have earned.

"Keep it," he said. "I want you to know what I am leaving."

He pushed us off. I could not tell whether he meant to be kind.

The man at the traps was mending a torn basket beneath a tree. He had heard shouting on the afternoon of the attack, but he had not seen Hamzah's boat. He said there had been two voices, perhaps three. One had called from the water. He had gone to the bank and found nothing but a broken branch caught against the roots.

"Was it a man calling for help?" I asked.

"It was a man calling."

"Could you tell which way he went?"

"I could tell where the sound came from. That is not where a man went."

I asked whether he had heard a name. He shook his head.

"Did you see another boat?"

"I saw a boat after the shouting stopped. It was far off."

"Was it the one that took Hamzah?"

"How would I know?"

I felt Bakar watching me. I wanted to ask whether the boat had been large, whether men had stood in it with weapons, whether it had come from the cut. The fisherman had already told us what he saw. I had been about to give him the rest of the picture and ask him to agree.

Tuma moved along the bank, lifting leaves where the water had laid them against the roots. He found a strip of cloth caught beneath a branch. It was dark with mud, and one edge had been torn.

"Nasir wore a cloth at his waist," I said.

"So did the men who carried your jars," Tuma answered.

He washed the strip and held it out. There was no mark on it. Bakar told him to leave it on a dry branch where the people who used the bank could see it.

"Should we take it to his sister?" I asked.

"And tell her what?" Bakar said.

"That we found it near the place where someone heard shouting."

"You can tell her that without carrying it away. If it belongs to a man here, he may come looking for it."

The fisherman had gone back to his basket. He listened while I repeated his account: voices after midday, a boat later, no person seen, no direction known. He corrected me once. The boat had passed before the light began to fall, he said. He could not tell whether it was before or after the shouting.

I scratched a small mark on the wood for the account, leaving Nasir's name untouched.

On the way back I watched the strip of cloth until the bend hid it. The bank seemed crowded with places where a man might have stood and been unseen. I knew that was not the same as evidence that he had stood there.

At the landing Nasir's sister was waiting with a basket of fish. She had brought them to sell and had stayed after the buyers went. Her hands smelled of the water. I had expected her to be watching the river. She was watching us.

"Did you find him?" she asked.

"No."

"What did you find?"

I told her about the man at the traps. I said he heard voices and saw a boat, but he could not place them together. I told her where Tuma had left the cloth and why we had not brought it. I did not tell her that I had almost taken it.

"Did you ask him if he knew my brother's voice?"

I looked at Bakar. He did not answer for me.

"No," I said.

She tightened her grip on the basket.

"Then you will have to ask him another time. Nasir used to shout across the water when he came home. The men at the traps might have heard him before."

I had asked the fisherman so many questions that I had thought there were none left. Hers was the first that could have changed what we knew.

"I will go back," I said.

"You will ask whether someone can take you back," she corrected. "That is what you told me yesterday."

"Yes."

She looked at Bakar. "I can pay for a man to carry a question if your boat has work. You need not lose another load for it."

Bakar said he would ask the fisherman when next he passed. He did not promise a day. She accepted that, and asked him to send the answer to her stall rather than through Jamil's house.

After she had gone, I went to Hasan with the wood. He listened while I gave him the account and asked me to repeat which words were the fisherman's and which were mine. When I had finished, he laid the wood beside the other strips.

A merchant was waiting at the doorway. He had a folded list of loads and a dispute with a man who said two bundles belonged to him. He had come to ask Hasan for someone who could keep the names apart on a voyage beyond Burné. The men who owned the goods wanted a witness who would not become their debtor merely by writing for them.

Hasan said he could not spare a man from the work of hearing Hamzah's owners.

"What about the prince?" the merchant asked, looking at me.

I felt my face warm. It was the first time that day anyone had called me that.

"He is not carrying my instructions," Hasan said. "And he cannot promise anything in Jamil's name."

"Can he count?"

"He can make a count and tell you when it is uncertain."

The merchant looked at the strip of wood in my hand. "Will he tell me when it is uncertain?"

I thought of the torn cloth, of the boat no one could place in time, of Nasir's sister asking the one question I had missed.

"If I do not know," I said, "I will say so."

"That is easy here," he answered. "Ask me again when a man has put a knife beside the goods."

He left his list with Hasan. He would return the next day, after speaking to the owners and the crew. No place had been promised to me. I did not even know whether the men who worked his boat would want another passenger.

Hasan gave me a second piece of wood for the disputed bundles. I placed it beside the one with Nasir's name. On the first, there was a mark for a voice that might have been his. On the second, two men claimed the same goods.

I had once thought a name written down could make a thing certain.

I began by leaving space beside both.

---

The merchant came back with two men and no answer about the bundles.

One was older than he was, with a narrow face and a scar beside his ear. The other had a band of cloth around his wrist. They stood on opposite sides of Hasan's mat and would not look at one another.

"This is Yusuf," Hasan said, indicating the merchant. "He has asked for someone to keep an account on his voyage. I have told him what you are permitted to do."

Yusuf set down the list I had seen the day before. "And what he is not permitted to do."

"Good," Hasan said.

I had expected to be asked whether I could write the marks. Instead the older man wanted to know whether I had ever been to the place where they meant to land.

"No."

"Do you know the people who will receive the goods?"

"No."

"Then what use are you?"

"I can tell them what was put aboard."

"So can I."

"You can tell them what you say was put aboard," the man with the cloth on his wrist answered. "He can tell them what he saw."

I thought he was helping me until the older man turned on him.

"You want another witness because you have changed your claim."

"I have not changed it."

Hasan held up his hand. He was not there to decide whose cloth had been sold. He wanted the men to state what they had agreed before the bundles were tied, and to name anyone who had been present.

They began again. One said that the cloth had been given to Yusuf for sale. The other said it had been pledged against an older debt and could not leave until that debt was counted. Both said they had a witness. Neither witness was in the room.

"Then leave those two bundles," Hasan said.

Yusuf looked at him. "I have promised the space."

"You have not promised the ownership."

"The boat is waiting."

"It can wait for a true account, or sail with less cloth."

I remembered Hamzah's boat, carrying goods that belonged to other men. I had learned the owners' names only after it was gone.

"Could I mark the bundles as disputed?" I asked.

The older man turned toward me. "If you put a mark on my cloth, will that stop him selling it?"

"No."

"Then do not put a mark on it."

Hasan nodded. Yusuf looked away, but he left the bundles behind. I had thought my first service would be to make a better record. It had been to keep something off the boat.

We walked down to the landing together. Yusuf carried the list; I carried nothing. The two claimants followed as far as the steps and stood there still arguing about who should pay for the space now empty.

The vessel was larger than Bakar's jar boat but smaller than the ships that sometimes lay beyond the river mouth. Its deck was crowded with covered goods, water jars and lengths of timber set where men could reach them. The sail was lowered. A man on the stern was mending a split seam in a basket with a strip of rattan. He looked up as we came alongside.

"This is Samad," Yusuf said. "The boat is his."

Samad did not stop mending. "And who is that?"

"The recorder I spoke of."

"I asked for someone who could hear two men quarrel and keep their words apart."

"He can do that."

"He looks as though he might begin the quarrel."

I had been introduced to people in Brunei who bowed before they knew what I wanted. Samad made room for the basket between his knees and kept working.

"What have you been told about me?" I asked.

"That you sent a boat where it was told not to go."

"Yes."

"And that the boat did not come back."

"Yes."

"Then you will not tell my men where to go."

"I have no authority to do so."

"That is what Yusuf says. What will you do when you think we are wrong?"

"I will tell you what I think."

Samad pulled the rattan tight. "And if I do not agree?"

I looked at the stern, where the water ran dark against the planks. I had told Bakar I would not order him to turn. That promise had been easy while we were still tied to the landing.

"I will not give your crew another instruction," I said.

"That is better."

He set the basket aside and called to the man standing by the mast. The man came over with a wet rope in his hands. Samad told him who I was and what Yusuf wanted. He listened without expression.

"He eats with us?" the man asked.

"If he brings his share," Samad said.

"And if he takes sick?"

"We carry him if we have to."

"Then he is another weight."

"I can work," I said.

The man looked at my hands. They were still rough from carrying jars, though the cuts had begun to close.

"You can work when someone shows you what to do," he said. "That is not the same as knowing."

He went back to the mast. I waited for Samad to call him back and tell him that I was a prince. Samad did nothing of the sort.

Yusuf offered to pay for my food from his own share of the cargo. Samad refused. The agreement had to be clear before the boat moved, he said. A passenger who ate from the common jars took food that the crew had counted for themselves. Yusuf could bring more food or give up another passenger's place. He could not make the difference disappear by calling it generosity.

"I can bring rice," I said.

"How much?" Samad asked.

I gave him an amount. He looked at the man by the mast, who shook his head. It was not enough for the time they expected to be away.

"Then I will bring more," I said.

"From whom?"

I had been about to say from Jamil's stores. I could ask Hasan to arrange it, and Hasan might have agreed. But I had not been given permission to take food for a voyage, and the men in this boat had no reason to trust a promise made in someone else's name.

"I will ask what I can buy," I said. "If I cannot bring it, you need not take me."

Samad put the basket down. "Now we can speak about the voyage."

He showed me where the crew slept, where the water was kept, and which covered goods could be moved when the weather changed. Yusuf could say what he wanted delivered. Samad decided when the boat would leave and where it could safely stop. The men who handled the sail had to agree that a load was possible before Samad accepted it. If a landing was unsafe, Yusuf could not buy their obedience by offering more cloth.

I listened while they argued about a heavy jar that had been set too far forward. The man from the mast said it must be moved. Yusuf said the space behind it belonged to another owner's goods. Samad told both of them to move aside and let the men who would carry it decide.

The jar went aft. Yusuf had to untie two bundles and make room for it. The man at the mast did not look pleased to have won. He had work to do before the boat could sail.

I went back through the market with a smaller purse than I wanted to show anyone. I bought rice and dried fish. The seller made me count the pieces before he wrapped them, because I had asked him to make the parcel light enough to carry. He wanted no complaint afterward that I had been given too little.

At the lower steps I found Nasir's sister sorting fish into two baskets. One held the larger fish, which she said would bring enough to pay a boy to carry a question to the man at the traps. She had already found the boy. He would go when the water rose.

"Bakar said he might ask him himself," I told her.

"Bakar has work. So do I."

"I may be leaving with a merchant."

She did not stop sorting.

"Where?"

"Toward the islands east of here. The boat's owner has not agreed to take me yet."

"And you will look for my brother there?"

"I do not know that he was taken there."

"Then do not tell me you will look there."

I crouched beside the baskets. The larger fish were still wet. She moved one into the smaller basket and weighed it in her palm before changing her mind.

"If you leave," she said, "who will tell me what Hasan learns?"

"Hasan will hear the men."

"That is not what I asked."

I had promised her I would carry news directly. I had been so pleased by the possibility of passage that I had begun to treat the promise as something I could leave behind with the count-stick.

"I will ask Hasan to send the answers to your stall," I said. "If he agrees, I will tell you before I go. If he does not, I will not leave without telling you."

"You ask more questions now," she said.

I could not tell whether she meant it as praise. She returned to the fish.

Hasan was still hearing the owners of Hamzah's lost cargo when I came back. I waited outside until the last man had gone. He had brought another list of goods that did not agree with the first, and Hasan looked tired.

I told him about the food, Samad's conditions, and Nasir's sister.

"You may buy what you can afford," Hasan said. "You may not take from Jamil's stores."

"I know."

"You were about to ask."

"Yes."

He agreed to send Nasir's sister the answers he could verify, directly to her stall, whether or not I remained in Brunei. He would not promise that every question would have an answer.

"Jamil told me to remain and hear the owners," I said. "I have not asked him to release me."

Hasan put down his list. "Then you have not finished asking."

I found Jamil with Hamzah and one of the cloth owners. He heard what I wanted, then asked whether I had completed the accounts he had assigned me. I had copied the names and statements I knew, but the owners had not settled their claims.

"Their claims will remain here whether you do or not," Jamil said. "If you go, you go in your own name, at the owner's permission. You carry no message or order from me. When Hasan sends for your testimony, you will answer. If you cannot promise that, you will stay."

I promised. He did not ask where the voyage would make my reputation. He asked how Hasan would reach me if the witnesses' accounts changed. Yusuf had a carrier who came through the landing on his return voyages; I gave Hasan the man's name, but we could not promise when he would return. Jamil told me that uncertainty was mine to bear.

I went back to Nasir's sister. She listened to Hasan's arrangement, then asked who would carry the message. Hasan had named a boy who worked near the lower landing. She knew his mother. That was enough for her to know where to complain if no message came.

By the time I returned to Samad, the light had changed on the river. He inspected the food I brought, then added a little from Yusuf's share after Yusuf agreed to the amount in front of the crew. The man at the mast counted the water jars again. Samad asked whether anyone objected to my taking the space beside the covered goods. No one spoke. The man from the mast looked at me once, then went back to his rope.

"You may come," Samad said. "You may keep the account. You may help when told. If I put you ashore, you will go ashore."

"And if I think you are making a mistake?"

"Tell me. Then listen to the answer."

I climbed aboard with my food. Yusuf gave me the list. Two bundles had been crossed out. He had left a space beside them for the names of the men who had claimed them.

"I thought we were leaving those behind," I said.

"We are. But the men who own them will ask what happened to their space."

I looked at the empty place where the bundles would have lain. The boat had room for me because two men could not agree on whose cloth it was. I did not know whether that made my passage cheaper or more expensive.

Samad called to the crew. The men took up their places without looking at me. The boat moved from the landing so slowly that I could still hear the fish sellers calling their prices when we reached the bend.

I had no order to carry and no promise of a place at the next shore. Samad gave the next instruction to the man at the mast. I opened Yusuf's list and made the first mark while the landing slipped behind us.

---

By the time I learned which jar held the crew's drinking water, the man at the mast had stopped telling me to move my feet. He still moved them for me when I stood in the wrong place.

I learned his name when Samad shouted it while he was asleep and needed to take the sail. Tarin woke, cursed Samad, and came forward without looking at me.

The boat had been away from Burné long enough for my rice to taste of the sack. On the first evening I had counted the jars twice and written the same number both times. By the next morning one had been opened, and a man had drunk from it without telling me. I made a mark for what remained. Tarin took the wood from my hand and asked whether I meant to follow every man's mouth with a tally.

"Samad told me to keep an account."

"Of the goods. If we begin counting thirst, you can have my share."

He gave the wood back. I thought he was mocking me. Later, when a jar shifted and nearly crushed his foot, he made me hold it steady while he found another place for it. I did not ask how many jars there were after that.

We passed low land and then water in which I could not tell where one channel ended and another began. At night Yusuf named places I could not see. He had been this way before, he said, but he asked Samad where we would make our next stop, and Samad asked a man who had come aboard before we left the last shore. The man pointed with his chin, then took the steering place while Samad watched.

I had expected the voyage to make me useful. Mostly it showed me how much work the others did before there was anything for me to record.

One morning we came toward a shore where houses stood above a strip of wet ground. Small boats lay farther in, and two men were carrying baskets across a plank that bent under them. The smell of smoke reached us before their voices. Yusuf stood beside me and straightened his clothing.

"These people know me," he said.

"Do they know what you brought?"

"They know what I usually bring."

I looked at the list. Two of the spaces remained empty where the disputed bundles had been left in Burné.

"Should I tell them about the missing cloth?"

"Tell them what is aboard."

He went forward to speak to Samad. I could see he wanted to be the first man ashore.

The boat slowed before the shallows. A narrow craft came out to meet us. The woman at its stern wore a faded wrap tucked above her knees. She looked at our load, at the water under the bow, and then at Samad.

"You have come on the wrong side of the falling water," she called.

Samad answered that he would wait.

"You may wait there. You will not wait against my posts."

Yusuf leaned over the side. "Saliha. I have cloth for your household."

"I can see cloth. I cannot see who will carry it."

"Your people, as before."

"My people are carrying a roof today."

She pointed toward the houses. Two men had stopped with their basket and were watching us. Behind them a broad piece of thatch lay on the ground. I had thought they were waiting for cargo. They were working on a house.

Yusuf told her he would pay for the carrying. Saliha asked how much. He named an amount. She looked back at the shore, called to one of the men, and waited while he answered. Only then did she say they would take the first load when the water rose.

"Can we put the jars ashore?" I asked Samad.

"Not until she tells us where."

"The jars belong to Yusuf."

"The shore does not."

I had heard him say much the same thing about the boat. I was beginning to dislike the ease with which he found another owner whenever I wanted something done.

We waited while the sun moved above the roofs. Tarin made me help lift the smaller goods out of the way so the men could reach the water jars. Yusuf complained that the cloth would be late for its buyer. Tarin told him the buyer could come aboard and carry it himself. Yusuf did not repeat the complaint.

When the water returned, Saliha brought two men in her craft. They took a line and showed Samad where it could be made fast. I tried to help Tarin pass the line. It caught beneath a bundle and pulled it toward the side. Tarin seized the bundle with his foot and shouted for me to let go. I let go so quickly that the line struck my palm.

Saliha watched us from her boat.

"Is he your new sailor?" she asked.

"He is the man who writes down what I lose," Samad said.

I wanted to say I had not lost anything. The bundle was still aboard. Tarin showed me the mark the line had made across its covering. I wrote that down.

The first baskets went ashore under Saliha's eye. She named the carriers and told Yusuf who would receive their payment. He said he had an arrangement with a man named Karim.

"Karim does not carry baskets," she answered. "If he promised you my hands, ask him to lend you his."

She was not angry. She had work to finish, and she was telling him what it would cost to interrupt it.

I followed the third basket onto the planks with the list tucked beneath my arm. The wood shifted under my weight. I looked down and found that the gap between two boards showed the water running below. A carrier behind me waited without speaking. I stepped aside, and he passed with twice the load.

On shore I counted six covered rolls laid on a mat. Yusuf had listed six for this receiver. I put a mark beside them, then noticed that Saliha had set two apart.

"Those are for the house," she said.

"Which house?" I asked.

"Mine."

"Yusuf says the six are for Karim."

"Yusuf can say that when Karim comes."

She took a short cord from a peg beside the mat. Three knots had been pulled tight in it. She held it out for Yusuf to see.

"Your man brought this last season," she said. "He said the loss on the crossing would be paid when the next cloth came."

Yusuf took the cord and looked at it.

"That was another boat."

"That was my brother's boat."

"I did not own it."

"You sent the load."

Yusuf turned to me. "Write that the cloth is mine until the buyer receives it."

Saliha held out her hand for the cord. "Write that he will not let me have what his man promised."

I could hear the carriers moving behind us. One of them had set down his basket. The others were waiting to see whether they should bring the next load.

I had seen Yusuf put six rolls aboard. I had not seen the earlier crossing or the knotting of the cord. I knew which words I could write as facts, and which would only be the words of the people standing before me.

"I can mark the six rolls," I said. "I can mark that Saliha claims two for an earlier loss and that Yusuf disputes the promise. I cannot mark who owns them."

Yusuf looked at me as if I had turned my back on him.

"Then you have brought me a witness who cannot finish a count," he said.

"The count is six," Saliha said. "The argument is over two."

A young man who had been standing in the shade of a house came toward us. He wore no weapon. Two others followed him, though they did not walk as if they had been summoned. One carried a coil of rope; the other stopped to ask a carrier where to put an empty basket.

"Why is the cloth still on the mat?" the young man asked.

Yusuf's face changed. He greeted him warmly and gave his name as Jalal.

Jalal looked at Saliha before answering Yusuf. She told him about the cord and the missing payment. He listened, then asked whether the carriers had been paid for the loads already brought in. Yusuf said they would be. Jalal asked when.

"At the end of the receiving," Yusuf said.

"Then there is not yet an end."

Jalal smiled. It made Yusuf smile too, although neither man had agreed to anything.

"Karim will hear your claim," Jalal told Saliha. "I can let Yusuf keep the goods under cover until he comes."

"Under whose cover?" she asked.

Jalal glanced toward the house behind him. "Ours."

"And who will stand there when Karim says the rolls have gone missing?"

The smile left his face. He asked the man with the rope whether there was space in the raised store. The man said there was room if they moved the fishing gear. He would not move it until the owner returned.

Jalal turned back to Yusuf. "You may leave the goods on the mat until the shade reaches them. After that they go aboard unless someone agrees to watch them."

Yusuf protested that the receiving had already begun.

"It has," Jalal said. "That is why you owe the carriers."

I had expected someone like Jalal to settle the quarrel by speaking. Instead he had found another person whose permission was needed. I disliked that too, until I remembered the men carrying the rolls.

Jalal saw the list in my hand.

"Who writes for Yusuf?"

"I do."

"Then tell me what he brought."

I read the six rolls and the other goods we had counted. I left out the two bundles that had never left Burné. Jalal noticed the space in the list.

"Did you forget something?"

"Two men claimed the same cloth. It stayed behind."

"Then there are people waiting for it."

"Yes."

"And now there are people waiting for these."

He took the list, looked at the marks, and handed it back. "You have traveled a long way to learn how to keep people waiting."

He said it lightly. I almost laughed. Yusuf did not.

From the water Samad called my name. I went to the edge of the planks. He wanted to know whether he should send the remaining baskets ashore. I looked toward Jalal. Jalal looked toward Saliha.

"The carrying is hers," he said. "The boat is his. You are the one with the list."

I told Samad what had happened and asked him to hold the remaining goods. He asked whether Yusuf had agreed. Yusuf came to the edge and shouted that the goods were his. Samad said he would not unload another basket into an argument until he knew who would pay for its return.

The carriers began moving the six rolls out of the path of the other work. Saliha put the knotted cord beside them where all of us could see it. She would not surrender the claim, but she would not take the cloth by force while the count was open.

I went back to Jalal. He had stepped aside to speak with the man who carried the rope. I heard him promise to find another place for the fishing gear. The man shook his head. Jalal promised again, then stopped when the man turned away.

"Do you command this shore?" I asked.

"My mother's kin have a house here," he said. "That gives me somewhere to sleep."

"And the goods?"

"Karim receives them. Saliha's people carry them. Samad keeps his boat. If I could command all three, I would be sitting down."

He looked at my palm, where the line had raised a red mark.

"Samad has given you work."

"He has given me a place."

"That is not always the same thing."

I did not know whether he meant to insult me. He was already looking past my shoulder.

Three men had come along the wet edge of the shore from beyond the houses. Two carried spears. The third had nothing in his hands and walked ahead of them. People made room without running. Saliha put the cord beneath her mat, then stood beside the cloth.

Jalal said something to the man with the rope. The man went toward the houses, not toward the newcomers.

"Are they yours?" I asked.

Jalal looked at me sharply.

"No."

The unarmed man stopped at the end of the planks. He looked at the six rolls, then at Yusuf.

"Which of you speaks for the goods taken on the last crossing?" he asked.

Yusuf opened his mouth. Saliha spoke first.

"That is what we are trying to find out."

The man glanced at her cord, half hidden beneath the mat. He did not come closer. The two spear-bearers stood behind him with their points toward the ground.

I held the list against my chest. Six rolls lay between the water and the houses. I knew how many there were. I still did not know who could let them move.

---

Yusuf stepped between the man and the cloth.

"I sent six rolls. I have witnesses."

"So have I," the man said. "They are waiting on the other side of the houses."

"Then bring them."

"Will you wait for them?"

Yusuf did not answer.

The man's name was Rafiq. He gave it when Saliha asked who had sent him. He said no one had sent him. Two of his kinsmen had come because he did not want to stand alone before the men who had accused his family of taking a boat's cargo.

I looked at the spears. Their points remained down. I had seen men with weapons take Hamzah's vessel, and for a moment I saw the same thing about to happen here. Rafiq saw where I was looking.

"They are here to see that I go home," he said. "You can ask them."

"Why would you not go home?" I asked.

"Because a man with goods can call another man a thief, and then there are people who think he may keep the thief until the goods are found."

He spoke as though I should already know the story. Yusuf did. His eyes had gone to the path beyond the houses.

Saliha drew the knotted cord out from under her mat. "My brother's boat came back with less than it carried. Yusuf's man said the loss would be made good when the next cloth arrived."

Rafiq pointed to the cord. "The loss was laid on my uncle. Karim held back what he owed us for carrying that load."

"Your uncle was seen with the cloth," Saliha said.

"He was seen carrying it. He was hired to carry it."

"Then who took it?"

"Ask the man who received it."

Yusuf said Karim was away and would return before dark. Jalal, who had been listening beside me, said Karim had gone to another landing and might not return until the next day. Yusuf turned on him.

"You told me he would be here."

"I said he was expected."

"You let me unload."

"I let you come to shore."

It was the first time I had heard Jalal's voice lose its playfulness. He glanced at Saliha, then at the men who had stopped work on the house. They were watching the six rolls. One still held a coil of binding fiber he had been carrying when the argument began.

Rafiq said that if Karim would not return, the cloth could remain until he did.

"It is not yours to hold," Yusuf answered.

"Nor was my uncle's payment yours to keep."

"That was Karim's arrangement."

"Then bring Karim."

The words passed among them until I could hear the same demands returning with different names. The water was falling. Samad's boat had settled lower against the posts, and Tarin was shouting to the men aboard to keep the line clear of the plank.

I wanted someone to ask me what should be done. I had seen the goods leave Burné. I knew their number and the names Yusuf had given me. I thought I could speak and make one part of the quarrel certain.

Yusuf turned toward me.

"Tell them whose goods these are."

I held out the list. "I saw six rolls loaded under your account. I did not see you buy them."

"I paid for them."

"Who saw that?"

He stared at me. "You are my recorder."

"That is why I have to ask."

Rafiq gave a short laugh. I did not like the sound of it. It made me want to agree with Yusuf, if only to stop Rafiq thinking we were weak.

"Perhaps your recorder should count what Karim took from us," Rafiq said.

"I cannot count a payment I have not seen."

"Then you are of no use to either of us."

I felt my face warm. I had heard that before in Burné, when I had thought it would be easy to prove otherwise.

A carrier stepped toward the mat. He had been waiting with an empty basket and wanted to know whether he should take the rolls under the roof. Yusuf told him to do it. Rafiq told him to leave them. The carrier stopped and looked at Saliha.

"Who will answer if I am struck?" he asked.

Neither Yusuf nor Rafiq answered.

"I will not carry them with men disputing them," the carrier said. "I have a roof to finish."

He set the basket upside down and walked away. Another man followed him. The six rolls remained on the mat, close enough to the water that a careless step could have pushed one into it.

Jalal called after the carriers. He offered to pay them himself. The first man turned back.

"With what?"

Jalal touched the small pouch at his waist. "I have enough."

"For today?"

"For the carrying."

"And tomorrow, when my roof is still open?"

Jalal lowered his hand. The man went on toward the house.

I had liked Jalal when he made Yusuf laugh. Now I saw that he had offered money without asking what it would buy. He looked at me and lifted one shoulder, as if he knew what I had seen and would rather I said nothing.

A gust lifted the edge of the cloth covering. Saliha knelt to pull it down. Rafiq moved toward her, and one of the men with spears stepped after him. Samad called from the boat.

"Keep them away from my load."

"They are already ashore," Yusuf shouted.

"They were my load when they crossed the plank."

Rafiq stopped. "Will you carry them away?"

"If the people who put them aboard agree, and if I can take them back without losing a man."

"You will not pass them through this landing while my claim is unheard."

"Then I will use another landing," Samad said.

Saliha stood up. "You will not use another landing on this shore without someone showing you the bottom."

Samad said nothing. I knew he could choose to turn away, but I also knew he had asked her where to make fast for a reason. The water was already too low for the boat to move easily.

Yusuf came close to me.

"Tell them who you are," he said quietly.

"They know I keep your list."

"Tell them who your family is."

I looked at the men with spears. Their hands were loose on the shafts. I could have named Jamil and watched what happened. Perhaps they would have stepped back. Perhaps they would have asked what authority Jamil had given me. I had promised him I would carry none.

"I came in my own name," I said.

"You came because I gave you a place."

"Yes."

"And now you will let them take my goods?"

"No one has taken them yet."

Yusuf's expression hardened. "You speak as though that is a victory."

It was not. I could see the mat, the rolls, and the people who had stopped their work. Every moment we stood there was being paid for by someone.

I went to Saliha and asked where Karim's receiving house stood. She pointed to a raised building beyond the roof the men were repairing. She said the room beneath it was dry, but the goods stored there belonged to Karim's relatives. No one could move them aside without asking.

"Who can ask?" I said.

"I can. They know me."

"Would you?"

"For what?"

"To keep the cloth where everyone can see it until the people who made the earlier agreement come."

"Who will watch it?"

I looked at Rafiq. He was still beside the mat. I looked at Yusuf, who had begun talking urgently to Jalal. Neither man would accept the other as a watcher.

"I don't know," I said.

"Then find that out before you ask me for a room."

She went back to the house with the unfinished roof. I followed at a distance and waited while she spoke to an older woman seated near the doorway. The woman had been cutting lengths of binding fiber. She listened, looked at the rolls, and asked how long we expected to keep them.

"Until Karim returns," Saliha said.

"That is not a time."

"Until tomorrow's light, if he does not come today," I said.

The older woman asked who would pay for the space. I told her I would ask Yusuf. She shook her head.

"You may ask. That is not payment."

She named a measure of rice for the room and another for anyone who stayed to watch. If the cloth remained beyond the next day, the price would have to be agreed again. She would not take responsibility for what was inside the bundles.

"Can the men who claim them each choose a watcher?" I asked.

"They can choose," she said. "I can refuse anyone who enters my house."

I went back to the mat. Jalal was speaking to Rafiq now. He had promised to keep the goods safe. Rafiq wanted to know whether that meant safe from Yusuf or safe for Yusuf. Jalal had no answer.

"The woman at the house will rent us a dry place," I said. "She wants rice. She will accept watchers if she agrees to the men. The claims can be heard when Karim returns."

Yusuf asked whether I had offered his rice.

"No. She has named a price. You must agree to it."

"Why should I pay to hold my own goods?"

"Because they are in the way and the men will not carry them."

Rafiq said the cloth should stay where he could see it. Saliha answered that it would be wet before morning if the water rose high enough. He looked toward the shore, then asked whether his cousin could watch.

The older woman came out to inspect him. She said she knew his cousin's mother and would allow him to sit beneath the house. Yusuf chose a man from his own party. She refused him because he had once taken a basket from her doorway without asking. Yusuf chose another. She agreed.

For a moment I thought the matter was settled. Then Samad came up from the boat.

"Who pays my crew for waiting?"

Yusuf told him the boat would leave after the cloth was stored.

"The water has fallen," Samad said. "We will leave when there is enough of it and when my men have rested. That may not be until tomorrow."

"Then I will pay for one more day," Yusuf said.

"How much?"

They argued. Tarin remained aboard, shifting the load away from the side where the boat had settled. I could see him working while we stood on dry ground deciding how much his time was worth.

Yusuf finally agreed to a day's food and a payment for the delay. Samad made him repeat the amount before the crew. Then he looked at me.

"You were to keep the account. Put that down too."

I wrote the rice for the room, the watchers' food, the carriers' pay for the six rolls already brought ashore, and the extra day for Samad's crew. Yusuf watched each mark as if I were cutting a piece from his purse.

Rafiq had not agreed that the goods belonged to Yusuf. Saliha had not surrendered her claim. Karim was absent. The two watchers took their places while the carriers lifted the rolls beneath the raised house. They carried them together, one from each side, and stopped twice to make sure neither watcher had lost sight of a bundle.

The older woman inspected the six rolls herself. She made me read the number aloud, then told both watchers to repeat it. When they had done so, she tied the door covering where she could see whether it had been disturbed. It was a poor defense against a determined thief. It was enough to show whom to ask if the count changed.

I returned to the landing. The men repairing the roof had lost most of the afternoon. The new thatch still lay on the ground. One of them looked at the sky and began carrying it inside. Jalal stood beside the house, holding a basket of binding fiber he had offered to bring. The carrier took it from him without thanks.

"You have made us all wait," Jalal said when I approached.

"I asked them to wait."

"You are careful with words."

"I am trying to be."

He looked toward the raised store. "My aunt will ask me why I let strangers fill her room."

"She agreed."

"She agreed because Saliha asked. I was standing there looking important."

I almost laughed. He did not.

A shout came from the water. Samad wanted his crew aboard before the light failed. I went to help carry their remaining food. Tarin pointed to the right side of the plank and told me where to step. This time I looked before moving.

When I came back, Rafiq was still beneath the raised house. One of the spear-bearers sat beside him, his spear laid flat. He was eating from a small bowl. The other had gone to fetch the witnesses.

"Will they tell us who took the cloth?" I asked Rafiq.

"They will tell you what they saw."

"And if it is different from what you say?"

He finished his mouthful before answering.

"Then you will have another thing to write."

I had wanted the delay because it would keep us from taking something we could not yet name. The boat could not leave. Saliha's roof was unfinished. Yusuf had agreed to pay for food, space and work he had not planned to buy. Rafiq had spent the day defending a claim that might still fail.

I went back to the list. The six rolls were still six. Beneath them I made marks for everything their waiting had cost.

At the house, a man was carrying the unfinished roof covering inside before night. The other men were still watching our cloth.

---

In the morning the six rolls were still beneath the raised house. I knew because the two watchers had woken the older woman before the first light and made her count them.

She came down carrying a bowl of water and asked why men who had spent the night guarding cloth could not count it without her.

"Because one of them says the other moved a roll," Saliha told me.

"Did he?"

"He says he heard something. Neither says he saw it."

The older woman lifted the door covering. All six rolls lay where we had left them, but one had been turned. Its binding was wet on the side that had faced the floor. The watcher chosen by Yusuf said he had shifted it away from a drip. Rafiq's cousin said the man had moved it after everyone slept.

"Then you should have woken me," the older woman said.

"Would you have come?" Rafiq's cousin asked.

She gave him the bowl. "I am here now."

No one laughed. She sent both watchers outside while she inspected the mat and the knots. I had made six marks the evening before. They told me nothing about who had touched the sixth roll.

The men who had been repairing Saliha's roof were already at work. They had put the thatch over the frame before dark, but a corner remained open. The older woman looked up at it each time the wind moved. She said we could have her dry room for one more day if we paid again. After that she wanted her space back.

Yusuf was not at the house. He had gone to look for Karim.

Rafiq's kinsman returned with a witness before Yusuf came back. The man had carried goods on the earlier voyage, though not for Rafiq's uncle. He had a scar along one shin and stopped to rub it before stepping onto the raised floor.

His name was Danu. Saliha knew him. She asked him to sit and gave him water. Rafiq wanted him to speak at once.

"Tell them where the two rolls went."

Danu drank first.

"I saw four taken from the boat at Karim's landing," he said. "I saw two still under the cover when I went ashore."

"Who was with them?" I asked.

"Men of the boat."

"Did you see those two brought off later?"

"No."

Rafiq looked toward Yusuf's watcher. "You hear?"

Danu turned to him. "I saw four carried ashore. I did not say your uncle took the others."

Rafiq's mouth tightened. His uncle had been accused of taking cloth because he had been among the last to handle the cargo. Danu could not clear him, but neither could he say he had seen him steal.

Saliha brought out the cord with three knots. "My brother was told the loss would be made good."

"By whom?" Danu asked.

"Yusuf's man."

"Was Karim there?"

"No."

"Then Karim may say he never heard it."

"He has said so already," she answered.

I made a separate mark for each man's statement. The new witness had not given us the missing rolls. He had given us a place where they had last been seen.

Jalal came from the house with the unfinished roof, carrying a length of thatch on his shoulder. He set it down when he saw Danu.

"My aunt wants her room back," he said. "She has kept other people's goods all night while the roof was open."

"Then help her finish it," Saliha said.

"I was doing that."

"You put down the thatch to come here."

He picked it up again. He carried it to the men at the roof without looking at me.

I followed because I had begun to understand that the argument was not only happening beneath the raised house. The two roof workers had lost a day of work on another dwelling. One said he could no longer finish that work before the owner returned. He would have to give back the rice he had taken in advance, or find someone else to do it.

"Yusuf has paid you for carrying," I said.

"He paid us for six rolls. We carried six rolls. The roof did not finish itself."

I asked what it would cost him to make up the lost day. He named an amount of rice. I had less than that left in my food parcel.

"Could I work with you?" I asked.

He looked at my hands. "Can you bind thatch?"

"I can learn."

"Then you will be slow. Slow work is another cost."

He went back to the roof. I stayed long enough to hold the loose edge while he fastened it. He corrected my grip twice. I could feel the wet fibers slipping under my fingers.

When Karim arrived, he did not come alone. Two men carried a basket of fish behind him. He greeted the older woman first, and she told him to take his quarrel out of her house before noon.

Karim was older than Jalal, with gray in his beard and a careful way of stepping over the planks. He listened to Saliha's account, then to Rafiq's, then asked Yusuf whether he had brought the goods that were promised.

"Six rolls," Yusuf said. "All counted."

"Then I will receive six."

"Two are claimed against the old loss," Saliha said.

Karim looked at her cord. "That was between your brother and Yusuf's man."

"My brother carried for you."

"He carried to my landing."

"Then pay him for the carrying."

"I paid the men who brought the load."

"Not Rafiq's uncle," Rafiq said.

Karim turned to him. "Your uncle left before the account was settled."

"He left because you held his share."

"He was named when the goods went missing."

"Named by whom?"

Karim did not answer. He asked for Danu's testimony. Danu repeated what he had seen, no more. Karim said that four rolls had been received and that two were missing. Yusuf said the two could not be his responsibility because his man had put six aboard. Saliha asked why her brother had been promised replacement cloth if no one admitted a loss.

The words came faster. I had heard them all before, except that Karim now stood in the middle of them.

Jalal asked whether Karim would release the unpaid carrying share to Rafiq's uncle.

"When the missing goods are accounted for."

"His hands carried the four you received," Jalal said.

"Then let him come and say so."

Rafiq stepped forward. "He will not stand before you while you call him a thief."

"I have not called him that."

"You have kept his pay."

Karim's two men put down the basket of fish. One moved closer to him. Rafiq's kinsmen stood. Their spears remained against the house, but I saw where their hands went.

Samad had come up from the water. He took one look at them and called Tarin from the boat. Tarin did not come ashore. He stood where he could release the line.

"If this becomes a fight," Samad told Yusuf, "my crew will leave the goods. I will not wait for men to decide who was struck first."

Yusuf asked Karim to take all six rolls and settle the older quarrel afterward. Karim agreed at once. Rafiq said his kinsmen would stop the rolls before they reached the house. Saliha said she would not ask her carriers to cross between them.

I looked at the six rolls. Their bindings were the same color. I had made the count myself. I could have handed Yusuf the list and let him say I had seen all that mattered.

"Could one roll stay here?" I asked.

Karim stared at me. "For whom?"

"Until the earlier loss is heard with the people who were aboard. Not given to anyone. Held here under the same watchers."

"That makes me pay for a loss I deny."

"It keeps five moving."

Saliha shook her head. "I claimed two."

"I know."

"And you would leave one."

"I cannot make him give two."

"Nor can you make him give one," Karim said.

He was right. I looked at Yusuf. He had brought me to count his goods, and now I was asking him to surrender use of one of them.

"If you leave one under watch," I said, "the five can go to Karim today. If the witnesses cannot agree, the sixth stays until a new term is made. It is still yours to claim. I will write that."

Yusuf looked toward the boat. "I have already paid for waiting."

"You will pay again if no one carries the cloth."

Karim said he would accept five only if the remaining roll was counted against no debt until the hearing. Saliha said she would allow five to move only if the sixth stayed and her brother's claim was read aloud before the witnesses. Rafiq demanded the pay owed to his uncle for the four rolls received on the earlier voyage.

Karim refused.

"Then your men can carry today's cloth themselves," Saliha said.

The carriers put down their baskets. I saw Jalal look from them to Karim. He did not speak for them.

Karim turned to his two men. They looked at the six rolls, then at the planks. One had a sore hand wrapped in cloth. The other said he would not carry all five without help.

The bargaining took longer than the quarrel. Karim agreed to release part of the older carrying share from his own stores, with the rest still disputed. Rafiq accepted the part on behalf of his uncle only after Danu said aloud that he had seen the uncle work on that voyage. The payment did not clear the accusation. Rafiq insisted I mark both facts.

Saliha agreed that her carriers would move five rolls. She made Yusuf pay them before they lifted the first. The sixth would remain beneath the raised house until the people who had made the earlier promise were heard. Karim's receiver and Rafiq's cousin would watch it, subject to the older woman's permission. The older woman agreed to one more night and named a new price. She refused to guarantee the cloth itself.

Then she asked who would finish her roof.

Yusuf said he had paid for carrying, storage, watchers, and his crew's delay. He had no reason to pay for a roof.

"You took the workers from it," Saliha said.

"They chose to carry."

"Because you offered pay."

"And I paid it."

The roof worker who had spoken to me earlier stood beside the unfinished corner. He was waiting for an answer about the rice he owed elsewhere. No one offered one.

I took the remainder of my food from the boat. Tarin made me count it in front of him, because some had been set aside for the return passage. I told him I would leave it with the workers and stay to help finish the roof.

"You will have nothing to eat when we sail," he said.

"I know."

"Then do not come aboard and ask for ours."

"I won't."

Samad heard us. He said he would not carry a passenger who could not feed himself, and he would not keep his boat waiting for my generosity.

"I understand," I said.

I had imagined the sacrifice before I made it. In my imagination the workers had been grateful. The man at the roof took the food, counted it, and told me it did not replace the day he had lost. He would take my labor too, if I could follow instructions.

Jalal came to stand beside me.

"You could have asked me for rice," he said.

"You offered to pay the carriers yesterday."

"And they asked what I would pay them tomorrow."

"Yes."

He looked at the roof. "My aunt will not thank either of us for promising her other people's time."

He went to help the men bind the open corner. I followed.

By afternoon five rolls had crossed the planks under the eyes of people who did not agree on their ownership. The sixth stayed behind. Rafiq had received a part of his uncle's unpaid share. Saliha had a witness to the promise made to her brother, but no replacement for the missing two rolls. Karim had five rolls and a public account he disliked. Yusuf had less cloth to sell and another day of costs.

I had given away the food that would have taken me home. I had thought it would feel like settling something. My fingers were cut by the binding fiber, and the man on the roof kept telling me I had tied it too loose.

When Karim passed below us, he looked up.

"Your marks will be carried back to Burné," he said. "Do not make them say I agreed that anything was stolen."

"I won't."

"Then write that I did not."

He went on without waiting for an answer. Jalal watched him go.

"He will remember you," Jalal said.

"For keeping one roll here?"

"For making him say in front of his people that he could not settle it alone."

The worker beside me pulled at the binding I had made. It came loose in his hand.

"Again," he said.

I began again.

---

I slept beneath the roof I had helped bind. The older woman allowed it because I had carried the loose thatch inside before the rain, and because I was not one of the men guarding the cloth.

The rain came before dawn. It entered at the corner we had not finished. A drop struck my cheek, then another. I lay listening until the worker beside me sat up and told me to move the mat.

"We will have to lift that edge again," he said.

I had spent the afternoon tying it. He had spent the afternoon untying what I tied.

When the light came, the sixth roll was dry. The older woman inspected it before she looked at her roof. Rafiq's cousin and Karim's receiver had both stayed awake for part of the night. Each claimed the other had slept longer. She told them that their quarrel had not changed the count.

"Six yesterday, one here now," she said. "If you want to argue about sleep, do it after you carry the water."

She put a jar in each man's hands. Neither refused.

I had kept a copy of Yusuf's account on a thin board. The five rolls delivered to Karim had been marked separately from the one still held. Beneath them were the rice and work owed for carrying, the payment made toward Rafiq's uncle's share, and the cost of keeping the last roll under cover. I had not written that the old debt was settled.

The older woman looked at the marks, then asked where her payment for the second night appeared.

I showed her.

"That is the payment Yusuf agreed to give," she said. "Where is the payment I received?"

"He has not brought it yet."

"Then you have written a promise where there should be a payment."

I scraped the mark away. I had been proud of how neatly I had divided the account. The place where I rubbed it was rougher than the rest.

Yusuf arrived with Samad as the workers began repairing the leaking corner. Yusuf had brought rice for the room, but less than the older woman had named. He said the fifth roll had been received late, and Karim's people had delayed his own payment. The older woman asked whether her house had also arrived late.

Samad waited while they settled the difference. He had already told Tarin to make the boat ready at the next usable water. The crew had been paid for the delay, and he would not promise another one.

"Are you coming?" he asked me.

"I gave the food away."

"I know. Tarin told me."

"I can work on the return."

"You worked on the outward passage. You still ate."

I had no answer. I had thought he might let me return because I had been useful to Yusuf. He did not say I had been useless. He said the food had been counted before we left Burné, and that he would not take it from his men to correct my decision.

Yusuf stood beside us, listening. I asked whether he would pay for my place.

"You have cost me a roll of cloth," he said.

"It is still yours to claim."

"It is not in my hands."

"Nor is it in Rafiq's."

"Do you think that comforts me?"

I told him I had made two accounts, one for the people here and one to carry back. He asked to see the second. He read it slowly, moving his finger along the marks. When he reached the part about Karim's payment to Rafiq's uncle, he stopped.

"That is not a payment I made."

"I wrote that Karim released it."

"And the cloth?"

"Five received by Karim. One held under the older woman's roof, with both sides choosing watchers. Ownership not decided."

Yusuf handed the board back. "You will make me answer for that in Burné."

"You sent the goods."

"I sent you too."

I thought of saying that he had brought me because two disputed bundles had been left behind and there was room. It was true, but it would not make the return fare appear. He had given me a place when I needed one. I had helped make his voyage longer and less profitable. Both things could be true.

Samad asked whether I wanted him to take the account to Hasan. I told him Hasan had agreed to receive reports through Yusuf's returning carrier, but I did not know when that man would next pass the landing. Samad said he could carry a copy himself, if he reached Burné before the carrier.

"Who will know that it came from you?" he asked.

I asked Saliha and Rafiq to hear the account before it went. They came with the older woman. Karim sent his receiver instead of coming himself. Yusuf stood beside Samad. I read every line aloud, including the part where the witness Danu had seen four rolls brought ashore on the earlier voyage but had not seen what became of the other two.

Rafiq interrupted when I said his uncle had received part of the carrying share.

"He has not received it," Rafiq said. "I have. I must take it to him."

I changed the line.

Saliha interrupted when I said her brother had been promised replacement cloth.

"He says he was promised it," she told me. "You did not hear the promise."

I changed that too.

Karim's receiver would not confirm that Karim owed anything for the earlier loss. He confirmed only that Karim had accepted five of the new rolls and had agreed that the sixth could remain under watch until the earlier witnesses were heard. The older woman confirmed the room and its price. She would not say the goods were safe from theft. She had not promised that.

I read the account again. It took longer. No one thanked me.

When I had finished, Samad asked each person whether the words describing their own actions were accurate. The receiver said he would repeat them to Karim. Rafiq said his uncle might still reject the terms. Saliha said her brother might too. The older woman said she wanted the room cleared before the next market day.

I made two copies on separate pieces of wood. They were not identical. One stayed with Saliha and the older woman, because they needed the names of those who would return for the last roll. The other went with Samad for Hasan. I read the differences aloud so that no one would later say I had hidden them. Samad made me tie his copy inside the covered basket where the crew kept dry things. He would not carry it loose against his body.

I asked him to tell Hasan that I had heard nothing here about Nasir. The absence of news was not proof that Nasir had never passed this shore. It was all I could say. I asked him also to remind Hasan of the promise to send verified answers directly to Nasir's sister. Samad said he could carry my words; he could not answer for Hasan.

"Hasan may ask you questions," he said.

"I told Jamil I would answer."

"Then find a way to get back when he asks."

He turned toward the water.

I followed him as far as the planks. Tarin was moving the last water jar into place. He saw me and pointed to a rope lying across the boards.

"Do not stand on that."

I stepped aside. I had learned at least that much.

Yusuf came down after us. He told Samad that he expected the boat to return with the remaining goods. Samad told him which stops he would make if the water allowed it. Yusuf asked whether he would take a different route to save time. Samad said he would decide after speaking with the pilot.

I watched the exchange without offering a shorter passage.

When the boat moved away, Yusuf was aboard and I was on shore. The men at the mast and steering place looked toward the water, not toward me. Tarin raised a hand once, then returned to his work. I had come east to learn what I could do without Jamil's order. I had learned that I could lose my passage without anyone taking it from me.

Jalal found me carrying the empty baskets back to Saliha's house.

"You have stayed," he said.

"I could not pay for the return."

"That is one reason to stay."

"Do you have another?"

"My aunt says the roof still leaks. She has work for you if the men will let you help."

"She has already had my work."

"Then you know what it is worth."

He took two baskets from me and carried them to the house. I had expected him to tell me that I could travel with his people, or that he would speak for me before someone important. He did neither.

When we reached the shade, I asked whether his kin sent boats farther along the islands. He said they did when the owners had goods to carry and reasons to go.

"Would they take me?"

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then how could they agree?"

I told him I had wanted to see the coasts beyond this landing. I did not tell him that I had once imagined returning to Burné with men who would follow my orders. He had already seen me lose the place I had bought for myself.

"You have spoken to my aunt," Jalal said. "You have spoken to Saliha. You have spoken to Rafiq. You have not spoken to the men who own those boats."

"I could ask."

"You could. They might say no."

"Would you speak for me?"

"About what?"

"That I kept the account."

"Yes. I saw that."

"That I did not let the cloth be taken."

He stopped walking.

"One roll is still under my aunt's roof. Karim has five. Rafiq has part of his uncle's pay. Saliha's brother has no replacement. Which taking did you prevent?"

I wanted to answer that no one had been struck. It was the one thing I had feared most when the spear-bearers came. But the workers had lost time, Yusuf had lost the use of a roll, and Karim had made it plain he would remember me. I had prevented a fight that might never have happened. I could not count it as though it had.

"I don't know," I said.

Jalal waited. He did not help me finish.

"I kept them talking long enough to make a partial bargain."

"That is something you can tell the boat owners."

"And will you help?"

"I will tell them what I saw. I will not promise their boats."

He looked toward the raised house. "Karim is my kin through people who do not like being named in public. You made him answer in front of workers. He will say you brought an old quarrel into a new bargain."

"Was the old quarrel not already here?"

"It was. That will not make him like you."

"And you?"

He shifted the baskets in his arms. "I think you listen better when someone can put you ashore."

I was ready with a reply. I let it go. He smiled a little, then turned toward the roof.

The older woman had found another leak. She stood beneath it with her hand raised, feeling for water. The workers had gone to fetch fresh binding fiber. She asked me to climb up and hold the thatch until they returned.

I did. The edge was wet and heavy. From the roof I could see the river beyond the planks, Samad's boat already a small shape against the farther shore. I could also see the raised room where the sixth roll lay beneath two watchers' eyes.

Saliha came with a new piece of fiber. She asked for my account board, the one that would remain here. I handed it down. She checked the marks for the carrier's payment, then asked where I had recorded the labor still owed on the roof.

"I have not," I said.

"Then leave room."

She made a mark in the blank space herself, where she could show the workers what had been promised. I watched her do it. It was the last mark on the board, and it was not mine.

I held the thatch in place until she told me to let go.
