Nasir's sister brought me a board with three names on it. She had written none of them herself. She had made the clerk write them again after he read the first copy aloud.

"The first man was seen on the bank," she said. "The second was taken aboard the foreign ship. The third was last seen in a boat. You have put them together."

"The headings are different."

"Then read them to me without looking at the headings."

I did. I had learned the cuts well enough to say which name belonged beneath which mark. When I came to Nasir, I stopped. The space after his name was still empty.

"Good," she said. "Leave it that way until somebody brings me a reason to change it."

Below the steps the tide had uncovered the mud around the posts. Small crabs came out of the holes and withdrew whenever a foot struck the planks. Tahir was counting the boats that had returned from the night fishing. The one Hasan had paid to go toward the traps was not among them.

"Should we send another?" I asked.

"With whose boat?" she said.

"I could ask."

"Ask the first owner whether he has finished the journey I paid for. If he has not returned, another boat may only bring back the same empty water."

I had come to help her with the names. I was already trying to give her a voyage.

A loaded timber boat touched the lower landing while we were speaking. The steersman called for someone to catch his line. A boy reached for it, missed, and nearly slid between the hull and the post. A man in the bow caught him by the back of his tunic. The boy climbed out laughing until the man told him how close his head had come to the wood.

The timber was bound in short rafts along the boat's sides. Its cut ends were marked with charcoal. One bundle had slipped below the surface and was dragging against the mud. Two men climbed onto the landing to find the owner who had agreed to pay them.

The third man was Sulad.

I knew him by the way he stepped from a moving boat. He waited until the hull lifted toward the planks, put one foot down, and brought his weight over it without catching the rail. I had watched him do it in Maynila. I had forgotten that I knew.

He saw me before I called his name.

"You are still here," he said.

"So are you."

"I came in this morning."

"From the work below the bend?"

"From the work beyond it. We moved twice when the bank gave way."

He looked past me to the woman with the board. She held it against her chest. Sulad gave her room to pass and watched until she reached Tahir. He did not ask who she was.

"I went looking for you," I said.

"I heard. The boy brought your question."

"He said you would speak when the work was counted."

"It has not been counted yet."

He turned toward the timber boat. The owner had arrived with two assistants and a measuring cord. He was pointing at the bundle beneath the water.

"Seven," the owner said. "I paid for eight lengths."

"Eight were cut," the steersman answered. "One was taken off at the upper landing."

"By whom?"

"Your men."

"Then show me where they put it."

Sulad went down to the mud. He pulled the submerged bundle clear enough to see the charcoal mark and called for the boy to bring a pole. The owner said he would count only the wood standing above the water. Sulad looked up at him.

"Then you will count less each time the tide rises."

The owner's assistants laughed. The owner did not.

I moved closer. Sulad had a narrow strip of wood tied to his belt, with cuts for the bundles brought from the work site. I recognized the marks. Hasan had shown me how to keep a delivery separate from a promise. I wanted to take the strip and explain the account to the owner.

Sulad held it out to the steersman instead.

"Read it," he said.

The steersman named eight bundles. He pointed to the seventh mark and said that one load had been taken from them above the bend by a man in the owner's employ. The owner denied that his man had accepted it. The steersman named the man and the landing.

"He is not here," the owner said.

"Then send for him," Sulad answered.

"You expect me to wait while my wood lies in the water?"

"We waited while your bank was too soft to stand on."

The owner looked at the measuring cord. He told his assistants to lift the bundles before they swelled any further. They did not move. They were waiting to see whether the men from the boat would be paid first.

I could have called Hasan. I could have offered to settle the count. The owner knew me as Jamil's troublesome young kinsman, and a word from me might have made him pay simply to end the quarrel. I was already thinking how useful that would be. Sulad would have his share and I would have done something for him after so long apart.

Instead I asked the steersman whether he had kept the cord from the upper landing.

He looked at me. "What cord?"

"The one used to mark the bundle they took off. You said the man accepted it."

"He cut his own length from the binding."

"Then there may be a piece still on the boat."

The boy who had nearly fallen into the water crouched by the stern. He drew a short cord from beneath a mat. One end had been cut clean. The other still held the knot by which the bundle had been fastened.

"That proves a cord was cut," the owner said.

"Yes," I answered.

He looked disappointed. He had expected me to say it proved his man had taken the timber.

"Can you tell whether it was cut above the bend?" I asked the steersman.

"I can tell you who cut it. He can tell you why."

The owner sent one of his assistants to the upper landing. The man went reluctantly, looking back at the timber in the water. We waited. The tide began to lift the submerged bundle, and the steersman pulled it against the side so it would not strike the planks.

Sulad took a seat on the lower step. I sat two boards above him. We had crossed a sea together. Now I did not know whether I was welcome beside him.

"Did you hear what happened at Laoe?" I asked.

"Men coming downriver told us you had taken houses."

"I was there."

"I know."

"We went to settle a claim. There was fighting. Some of the houses burned."

"Who lived in them?"

I could name Mara and the child she had carried. I could name the man who had been killed in the lane. I did not know how many people had lost their roofs. I told him what I knew. I did not tell him the attack had been necessary.

"And after that?" he asked.

"The foreign ships came."

"I heard you were taken."

"I was."

He touched the end of the strip tied to his belt.

"One man said you were dead. Another said the ruler had paid to bring you back. They both spoke as though they had stood beside you."

"Neither had."

"Then you know how the words travel."

I looked at the empty stretch beyond the traps. The inquiry boat had not appeared.

"Hasan is keeping the accounts of those who came back," I said. "I have been carrying questions for him."

"That is work."

"It is."

"Do you like it?"

I almost told him that I had been trusted with larger work before the foreign ships. He knew that already. He had heard enough men explain what I had once been allowed to do.

"I am better at it than when we came," I said.

"Then you have learned something."

The assistant returned with a thin man who had mud on his calves. He was the owner's receiver at the upper landing. He said he had taken one bundle and put it beneath a shed because the bank was too low to hold the whole load.

"Did you tell them it was received?" the owner asked.

"I told the steersman I had it."

"Did you mark it?"

"I was going to when the rest came."

The owner stared at him. He had spent half the morning disputing a bundle his own man had put away.

"Then count it," Sulad said.

The owner took the strip from the steersman, called for his measuring cord, and counted the seven bundles at the lower landing. He sent his receiver back to measure the eighth. He would pay for eight if it matched the others. The steersman demanded payment for the time lost while they waited. The owner refused that part. They argued over the number of hands needed to lift the wet timber, and in the end agreed that the owner would supply two men from his yard.

Sulad did not thank me. I was glad he did not. I had asked a question that could be answered. He and the others had kept the bargain from being rewritten.

When the owner went to fetch the payment, Sulad stood.

"You wanted my words before the witnesses," he said.

"Yes."

"Which words?"

"What happened before we left Maynila. What you saw. What you agreed to."

"I will tell Hasan what I saw. I will not have you put my name beside a promise I did not make."

"I have not asked you to promise anything."

"You asked me to follow you once."

I remembered how easily I had spoken for him when we were younger. We had left home with different reasons and I had been able to pretend they were one.

"Will you come back to Luzon?" I asked.

"Is there a boat?"

"No."

"Is there a crew?"

"No."

"Then you are asking me to follow a question."

He looked toward the owner counting out the first payment. The steersman was watching every piece.

"I will go to Hasan after I have my share," Sulad said. "I will tell him what belongs to me. If you find a passage, tell me its terms. I will decide then."

"Do you want to know what happened at home?"

"Of course."

"And if I go?"

"Going is not the same as bringing back the place we left."

He went to receive his pay. I stayed on the steps until the owner had counted it twice and Sulad had taken it into his hand.

Nasir's sister returned from Tahir's board. She had seen Sulad speaking with me.

"Did the boat from the traps come in?" she asked.

"No."

"Then keep his name where it is."

She meant Nasir. I knew she meant Nasir. I had been thinking about Sulad, about whether he would sail with me, and for a moment I had forgotten which name she had come to ask about.

I took her board and read the three headings again. There was still an empty space after her brother's name. She listened while I left it empty.

The rain began while I was carrying a message from the returned junk's master to the receiving house. By the time I reached the yard, water was running off the roof in thick ropes and gathering in the hollow where the jars stood.

Laila had ordered the largest jars moved onto planks. Four women were working under the eaves. One held the mouth of a jar steady while the others lifted its base. They set it down, rolled their shoulders, and went back for the next. A boy stood beside the door with a tally. He was making marks faster than the women could move the jars.

"That one has already been counted," Laila said.

He looked up. "It has been moved twice."

"It has been moved once to the wall and once onto the planks. We still have one jar."

The boy stared at the marks and scraped one away with the edge of his knife.

I stepped beneath the roof and shook water from my sleeves. Laila saw the board I carried.

"Another question?"

"A message from the junk's master. The man who was hurt has eaten the rice you sent. His master says he cannot pay until his cargo is counted."

"I had expected that."

"He asked me to tell you he has not forgotten."

"Did he ask you to promise it for him?"

"No."

"Good. Put the board there. Keep it out of the water."

I set it beside a basket of bindings. The rain was falling hard enough that the yard beyond the eaves had become a pale moving sheet. I had meant to leave as soon as I delivered the message. The water was already over the top of my sandals.

A woman called from the storeroom. Two jars near the back wall had begun to leak. Laila went inside, and I followed without asking whether I was wanted.

The room smelled of rice, damp wood and the sharp leaf oil rubbed along the shelves. One jar stood on its side, its neck plugged with cloth. Water had found the floor beneath it and was creeping toward a basket of dried fish. Laila crouched, pressed the cloth deeper into the neck, and told the woman to lift the basket.

I took its other handle. The weight surprised me. I had lifted jars for boatmen before, but the fish had been packed so tightly that the basket did not give when we raised it. We carried it to a dry shelf. Laila checked the underside before letting us set it down.

"That shelf will hold one more," I said.

"Not with the fish already there."

I looked at the support beneath it. The peg was sound. The plank had bent in the middle.

"We can move the smaller jars to the other wall."

"The other wall is wet."

I had not noticed. A dark line ran down from a crack in the plaster.

Laila called for a second plank. The woman who brought it set one end on a low stone and the other on a stack of old boards. They tested it with an empty jar before moving the fish. The plank shifted. Laila told them to put another stone beneath the middle.

The boy with the tally came to the door.

"Shall I count the baskets on the new plank as moved?" he asked.

"Count what is on the plank," Laila said. "Write where it came from. If we put it back tomorrow, do not say we have gained another basket."

He looked at me. He had heard I kept harbor lists. I was tempted to show him how Hasan made the marks. Laila had already given him an answer he could use.

I went back into the yard to find the stone. The rain had washed mud across the threshold. I chose a flat one from beside the drain and brought it inside. When we lifted the plank, Laila put her hand under it to feel whether it rested evenly. Her fingers were cold from the water.

"Try it," she said.

I pressed down. It did not move.

"Now the basket."

We lifted the fish together. For a moment her shoulder was close to mine and I could feel her holding the weight steady while I changed my grip. I had remembered her standing in a dry passage with people moving aside for her. I had forgotten the way she checked a load herself before she let anyone call it safe.

The rain weakened toward afternoon. A man from the kitchens came to ask whether the evening meal could be made from the rice Laila had set aside for tomorrow's workers.

"No," she said.

"Then the cook will need another measure."

"How many people are eating?"

He gave a number.

"How many have already been fed?"

He hesitated.

"Go and ask," she said. "I will not take tomorrow's food because you have counted today's people twice."

He went back through the yard. She watched him until he was out of sight.

"Does everyone come to you for an answer?" I asked.

"Only when they hope I will give one without asking what happened."

I thought of my first day in her yard. I had come looking for a man who would carry my words to Maynila. She had been counting food for workers I did not know. I had thought her position meant that she could make a boat appear.

"You were right that day," I said.

"Which day?"

"When I asked you for a carrier. You said I had brought guesses."

"I remember."

"I still have no boat."

"Then you have learned how long a guess can last."

She sat on a low stool to wash the mud from her hands. The bowl had a crack near the rim. She poured the dirty water into the yard, filled it again from a jar, and offered it to me. I washed the grit from my fingers. She waited while I finished.

"Why did you come back from Laoe?" she asked.

"Jamil ordered me home."

"That is why the boat came. I asked why you came."

I had been relieved to leave the burned houses. I had wanted the ruler to hear that I had done something useful. I had not known what the people there would say about me when we were gone.

"I thought there would be another voyage," I answered. "One that went toward Luzon."

"And after the foreign ships?"

"I was glad to come back alive."

She nodded. It was not the answer she had been waiting for, but she let it stand.

"While the ships were in the harbor, the guards asked for more food," she said. "Some came with orders. Some came with nothing but a loud voice. I fed the men whose duty I could confirm. Others went to their own households. They were angry."

"Did the ruler hear?"

"He heard that men were hungry. He did not hear which stores they had already emptied. I sent the counts."

"Did he answer?"

"He sent someone to check them."

"Was that enough?"

"It kept the next man from taking food meant for the women who cooked it."

I looked toward the yard. The boy had put his tally beneath a dry tile. He was holding it down with a stone so the wind would not carry it away.

"I could do work here," I said.

"Yes."

The answer came too quickly. I had not expected her to agree.

"What work?"

"Someone must check the grain brought by the boatmen. Someone must hear the people who say they delivered it. Someone must go to the kitchens and ask whether it arrived. I can pay for that work when I have a measure left to sell. I cannot promise a court place."

"Would Jamil allow it?"

"Ask him. You have spent enough time asking other people to speak for him."

I laughed before I could stop myself. She did not laugh, though her mouth moved slightly.

"And if I go north?" I asked.

"Then I will find someone who stays."

I wanted her to ask me not to go. I wanted her to say that the days when I was missing had changed what she thought of me. She had asked after the ship that held me. That was all she had told me. I was already trying to make it more.

"Did you think I was dead?" I asked.

"I knew people had died. I did not know whether you were one of them."

"Would it have mattered?"

She put the bowl down.

"You are asking me to answer for something that did not happen."

"I am asking whether you were afraid."

"I was afraid for the people who had gone out to the ships. You were among them."

I waited. She did not add my name again.

The kitchen man returned. He had counted the evening mouths a second time and found two who had already eaten. The cook could make the meal from what remained. Laila sent him back with the corrected count and a small bundle of dried fish for the people who had carried the jars in the rain. She asked him to name them before he left.

When the yard had emptied, I took up the board from the junk's master.

"I should bring this to Hasan," I said.

"Then take it. He will want to know whether the man who sent it has agreed to the words."

"He has."

"Good."

I reached the edge of the eaves. The rain had stopped, but the path was still running with water.

"Ache," she said.

I turned.

"If you stay, come after the next market. I will have the receiving counts. Bring Jamil's permission if you want to work here."

"And if I leave?"

"Tell me before I have paid someone to wait for you."

I had been offered work I could do. There was food to be counted, a store that could be lost to a careless mark, and a woman who had told me the terms before I agreed. It was a life I could imagine more clearly than the voyage I kept asking about.

I walked back through the wet yard with the master's board under my arm. I had no answer for Laila. I had not promised to return after the market. For once I knew she would not count me until I did.

Jamil sent for me when the water was low enough to walk beneath the houses on the eastern bank.

The messenger said the ruler would be there. I had expected to be called into the hall where men stood according to their place and waited for a name to be spoken. Instead I found Siripada in the shade beneath a raised house inside the palace enclosure, watching two carpenters remove a rotten crosspiece from the platform above him.

The piece had been painted once. The color remained only where a beam had covered it. One carpenter was sawing through a peg while the other held the wood so it would not fall into the mud. Siripada stood with his hands folded behind his back. Jamil was beside him, holding a short board.

"Tell me whether they can use that timber again," Siripada said when he saw me.

I looked at the crosspiece. Its ends were black with rot. The middle still seemed hard.

"For a shorter span, perhaps."

The carpenter with the saw stopped.

"Would you put your foot on it?" Siripada asked.

"Not without testing it."

"Then why have you offered it to another man?"

The carpenter resumed cutting. I had wanted to give an answer that saved wood. I had not considered who would stand on the platform afterward.

Siripada waited until the peg came free. The crosspiece fell into the carpenter's arms, and he carried it into the light. Its underside was split along most of its length. He set it aside with the other waste.

"Jamil tells me you have been carrying questions for Hasan," the ruler said.

"Yes."

"Has he been able to use your answers?"

"He has sent me back when I brought only what I thought had happened."

"And now?"

"Now I ask who saw it."

Jamil looked at me. "Sometimes."

Siripada smiled at him, not at me.

A servant brought a mat and set it on the dry ground. We sat where the workers could still be heard. A bowl of water stood between us. Siripada did not ask for a full account of the foreign ships. He had heard men describe them until they disagreed over which ship had fired first. He asked what had happened to the people taken from the junk.

"Some came back," I said. "Others were still held when I left."

"Can you name them?"

"Some."

"Can you tell me what was paid for you?"

"I saw gold brought aboard. I did not hear the bargain."

"Can you tell me who gave it?"

"No."

He looked toward Jamil.

"The merchant says his chest was opened," Jamil said. "The people who carried the metal say they obeyed an order. No one has produced an account that both sides accept."

"Then the debt remains," Siripada said.

I had hoped he would tell me who had paid. I had hoped, too, that he would say the payment was a gift from the ruler and that I owed nothing more. He said neither.

"Men at the harbor are asking whether you will send boats toward Luzon," Jamil told me.

"I have asked owners about passage."

"One owner has agreed to speak to you," Jamil said. "That is all."

"Ruma knows him."

"Ruma has her own work."

"I know."

Siripada lifted the bowl and drank. The carpenters above us struck a new peg into place. Three blows sounded firm. The fourth made a different noise. The man with the mallet stopped, withdrew the peg, and looked into the hole before choosing another.

"You came to Burné with a story about the home you had lost," Siripada said. "You have told that story to my household, to merchants and to men who might carry your words. You have now been away long enough that the place may have changed again."

"That is why I must go."

"Must?"

I looked at Jamil. He did not rescue me from the word.

"I want to know who holds Maynila," I said. "I want to know what has happened to the people I left."

"Then a traveler might bring you an answer."

"A traveler might be refused."

"And a ship with armed men might be welcomed?"

I had imagined a vessel approaching the river mouth with my name carried before it. I had imagined the men who had once dismissed me standing aside when they saw that I had returned with friends. I had also seen Laoe after men came to settle a claim.

"I do not know," I said.

"Then you will have to learn before you decide how many men to take."

Siripada set the bowl down.

"There is work for you here. Hasan needs men who can carry a count from one bank to another without making it larger on the way. Jamil has a place for a clerk who knows the routes. You have learned the names of owners and the limits of their bargains. You could keep that work. There would be food and pay, and you would answer for what you did rather than for what people say your grandfather ought to give you."

I had expected him to tell me that I could stay if I gave up speaking of Maynila. He had offered something harder to dismiss.

"Would I serve Jamil?" I asked.

"You would work with his men. He would decide which duties you had earned."

"And my place in your household?"

"You would still be my kin."

Jamil shifted his board. "He would not command the harbor boats."

Siripada looked at him. "He would not need to."

I thought of Laila's storeroom and the jars we had moved above the water. I thought of the job she had offered after the next market. The ruler had given me a way to remain without pretending that my losses had made me a captain. I wanted to ask whether I could keep the work and still be treated as someone who might one day lead men. I knew Jamil would hear the question I was really asking.

"What if I choose to go north?" I said.

"Then I will hear what the owners offer you."

"Will you give me ships?"

"I will not give away boats that belong to other people."

"Men?"

"Not men who have not agreed."

"Then what can you give?"

Siripada's face changed. He had been patient with me, but I had begun to ask as though his first answer were an insult.

"A name under which you may ask," he said. "A letter for the people you hope to meet. Permission for any owner who wishes to trade on that route to hear your proposal without fearing that I have forbidden it. If there is a public reason to send goods, I may approve a limited share from my own stores. I will not pay for a quarrel whose end I cannot see."

"Would you give me authority over the voyage?"

"Over what voyage?"

I had no answer. The carpenter above us drove the replacement peg home. This time the sound did not change.

Jamil opened the board he carried. It held the names of boats with work already agreed. Beside each were cuts for the owner, the cargo and the hands.

"Choose a master who will go," he said. "Find out where he intends to sail. If he has room for your purpose, tell him what you will pay. If you need men to accompany you, let them hear the terms from you and answer for themselves. Then bring the agreement here. I can judge work that has been offered. I cannot judge a fleet you have imagined."

I looked at the board. I had spent months learning how to make marks like those. I wanted to throw it aside and ask for something grander. I did not.

"And if the owners refuse?"

"Then you have no voyage," Jamil said.

Siripada turned toward the carpenters. They had raised the new crosspiece into place. One was testing it with his weight while the other watched the joints. The man stepped across it twice before he called to the workers above that they could continue.

"Your return could help the merchants who trade with Luzon," Siripada said. "Or it could make their passages dangerous. If men in Maynila take your arrival as a threat, they may blame the ships that brought you. Those owners have a right to know what you intend."

"I intend to go home."

"That tells them where you want to land. It does not tell them what you will do after."

I could have said I would restore what had been taken. I could have said I would make Maynila answer to me. The words rose easily. I had carried them in my head since the day I left.

"I will have to speak to the people there," I said.

"That would be a beginning."

Siripada stood. Jamil rose with him, and I followed. The ruler placed a hand against the new crosspiece and tested it. It held.

"You do not have to answer today," he said. "If you stay, there is work. If you go, bring me terms that other people have accepted. Do not ask me to call a wish a ship."

He went up the steps into the house. Jamil stayed until the carpenters had begun clearing the old timber.

"Hasan says Sulad has come back," he said.

"He has. He will give his own account."

"Has he agreed to go with you?"

"No."

"Then do not put him on a crew list."

"I have not."

"Good. There is a place in my yard tomorrow if you wish to work. I will not hold it open forever while you ask about a northbound passage."

He left me beside the discarded crosspiece. Its painted face looked sound where the sun touched it. I turned it with my foot and saw the split running almost to the middle.

I had been offered two places. One had a roof, work I knew how to do, and people who would count whether I arrived. The other had no hull beneath it yet. I had wanted the second to look like a command. Siripada had made me see how much of it I still had to ask for.

I went toward the landing to find the owner Ruma had mentioned. I did not know whether he had arrived. I knew I would have to let him answer before I counted his boat.

The owner came after the market, when the boats that had brought vegetables were already leaving with their baskets empty.

Ruma pointed him out from the landing. He was kneeling beside a low cargo vessel, drawing a cord beneath its bow while two men held the other end on the bank. The hull had been dragged high enough to show a pale scrape where the bottom had touched stone. A boy with a pot of resin stood behind him.

"That is Naim," she said. "He goes toward the mouth."

"Does he go farther?"

"Ask him. I have told you what he told me."

She carried her own basket past him and went to settle an account. I waited until Naim had finished measuring. He took the cord from the men and made a mark on a short piece of wood.

"Ruma said you might speak with me," I said.

"She said you wanted to travel."

"To Luzon."

He looked at my feet, then at the clean cloth Jamil had given me for the palace. The hem was already stained from the yard.

"My boat goes to the outer settlements," he said. "After that I return with fish and timber. It is too small to carry your quarrel."

"I have not told you my quarrel."

"You have told enough people that it has reached me."

I crouched beside the scraped plank. A seam had opened where the bow rose from the keel. Resin had been pressed into it once, and the new crack ran through the old repair.

"Will you replace it?" I asked.

"Would you take passage if I did not?"

"No."

"Then perhaps we agree on something."

He stood and told the boy to fetch a broader pot. The boy brought the same one back. Naim turned it upside down. A finger's width of resin fell into the sand.

"We need more than that," he said. "And the man who sells it wants payment before he gives it to us."

I offered to carry the pot to the market. Naim shook his head.

"Money first. Carrying it will not make it full."

I had come expecting to bargain for a place aboard. Instead I watched a man decide whether he could put his own boat in the water. I knew the cost of resin no better than I knew how much rice a crew would eat beyond the mouth. I had spent years learning how to read the counts after someone else made them.

At the next landing, two owners were examining a sail spread between posts. One pointed to a patch that had been sewn across a tear. The other pulled at the cloth until a row of stitches gave way.

"That is not my sail," the second man said. "It is yours now."

The first man swore at him. Neither looked pleased when I approached.

I asked where their boats were going. One said upriver. The other said he would sail north when the wind allowed it. His name was Harun. He owned the deeper vessel drawn alongside the outer posts, where three workers were taking jars from the hold and testing them for cracks.

"How far north?" I asked.

"Past the islands where my buyers wait. Perhaps to the coast of Luzon if there is cargo worth bringing home."

"I need to reach Maynila."

"Then you need more than a place on a boat. I can take a passenger to a coast. I cannot promise the people there will let him go where he wants."

I told him who I was. He knew. He asked what I would pay.

I named the amount of coin I still had. Harun looked at the sail on the ground.

"That would feed you," he said. "It would not pay for turning away from my buyers. If you want my boat to enter a river whose rulers may not welcome you, I need to know who will answer if the cargo is seized."

"I can give you a letter from Siripada."

"Will his letter replace my cargo?"

"No."

"Then it may help us speak when we arrive. It will not feed the crew on the way home."

I thought of the gold carried to the foreign ships. The merchant still claimed it had come from his chest. I had seen the metal and wanted, for an instant, to spend it again. I told Harun that the payment was disputed.

"Then it is not yours to offer," he said.

He turned to a jar with a hairline crack near its foot. He tapped it once and handed it to a worker.

"Put this one ashore. If it breaks at sea, it will wet the sacks beside it."

The worker said there were only four sound jars left. Harun told him to count again. They counted together. There were five. Harun gave the worker a look I recognized from Hasan's yard. It meant the count had been corrected and would be remembered.

I returned to Naim at the lower landing. His men had lifted the bow higher. He had sent the boy to borrow a wedge from a carpenter. He listened while I described Harun's offer, which was no offer yet.

"Harun will not risk his whole cargo for you," Naim said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He gave me a mallet and pointed to a block beneath the hull. We struck it into place while the others steadied the boat. When it held, he let go and walked around the bow to see whether the weight had settled evenly.

"Why do you want to take armed men?" he asked.

"I want people I can trust."

"That is a different answer."

"I may not be welcomed in Maynila."

"Then you must decide what you will do if you are refused."

I had told Siripada I would speak to the people there. I had not told Harun how I would keep a quarrel from becoming another Laoe.

"I would land outside the river first," I said. "Send a message. Ask for those who know me. I will not ask a merchant to force his way upriver on my account."

"Tell Harun that. Then let him decide whether he believes you."

At midday Hasan sent Tahir to find me. He brought a board with the names of the boats still out, and a message from Nasir's sister. The inquiry boat had returned from the traps. Its crew had found a broken paddle and two fishing baskets. Neither had a mark that proved who owned them. They had spoken to men on the far bank and brought back three names to be checked. Nasir's name was not among them.

"She has paid for another question," Tahir said. "She wants Hasan to hear the men separately before anyone tells the market that the paddle belonged to her brother."

"Does she want me there?"

"She said you could carry the names if you were here. She did not ask you to take a boat."

I took the board to Hasan's yard. I copied the names for the men who would question the witnesses and returned the original to Nasir's sister. She checked the marks while I waited.

"Will you be here when they speak?" she asked.

"I may go north."

"Then tell Hasan which names you carried. I will not have a man questioned twice because you have gone."

"I will."

"Do it before you leave."

She kept the original board. I went back to the boats without it.

Harun had moved his sail into the shade. I found him sitting with two men who had been measuring dried fish into baskets. One was his steersman. The other owned a smaller vessel that carried water and goods between the outer settlements. His name was Tamin. He had come to ask Harun for space on the northern passage, not to follow me.

I put my proposal before them. Harun would keep his cargo and his authority over his vessel. I would pay for my own food and for the men who chose to travel with me. We would seek a landing where his trading interests were not exposed to an immediate quarrel. I would send word toward Maynila before asking him to enter its river. If he judged the approach unsafe, he could refuse. I would not take his boat from him.

"What do you bring that helps us?" Harun asked.

"Knowledge of the river and the people who traded there when I left."

"When you left," he repeated.

"Yes."

"That is worth hearing. It may no longer be worth following."

Tamin said he could carry water to the outer islands and return with a cargo of salt. He would not go all the way to Maynila. Harun asked whether Tamin could accompany him as far as the northern stopping place, so the two vessels could exchange supplies if one lost a jar. Tamin looked at his steersman, who shook his head.

"Not unless we have enough water for our own return," Tamin said.

Harun asked me to count the jars that had passed inspection. I counted five. Tamin had three sound jars aboard his vessel. One had been promised to another household. We measured what each crew would need to reach the first agreed stop and return if the northern passage failed. The men disagreed about the time the winds would allow. We did not settle the weather by arguing.

I noticed that Harun had counted a jar as full when its stopper was loose. He had done it while speaking to me. I took it up and felt the wet line below the rim. The water had already gone down.

He frowned when I set it apart.

"You have made my count worse," he said.

"I have made it smaller."

He sent the worker to refill it and seal it properly. When the man returned, Harun asked me to repeat the route with the first stop and the place where he could turn back.

I did. He drew the line with a finger in the dust, then crossed out the direct approach to Maynila.

"This is the passage I might sell," he said. "A trading voyage that may carry you toward your home. No promise of a fight. No promise of a throne. You must find your own companions and bring their names. I will hear them before I take them."

Tamin agreed to discuss the first leg only after his crew had checked the water. He would take no letter from the ruler as payment for a jar.

I went to Jamil with two boards. One named what Harun was prepared to consider. The other named the goods and hands still missing. Jamil read both without speaking. He asked why I had left a space after the number of companions.

"Because none has agreed," I said.

"And the stores?"

"Not counted yet."

"And Harun's return cargo?"

"His. He will not pledge it."

Jamil set the boards beside his own. He did not say I had earned a ship.

"Go back and finish the count," he said. "Then I can tell Siripada what is real."

I went back to the landing. Harun's men were lifting the repaired sail into the late light. They had sewn a new strip where the stitches failed. The cloth held when the wind caught it, but the mast was still bare. I could see the shape of a voyage now. I could also see every space where someone had yet to say yes.

The next morning Harun had drawn a line down the middle of his board.

On one side were the goods he meant to sell. On the other were the mouths he would have to feed. He had put my name on neither.

"You said you would bring companions," he told me.

"I have spoken to one."

"Did he agree?"

"He said he would hear the terms."

"Then you have brought me a man who can listen."

Harun turned the board so I could see it. His steersman had counted the people already promised places. A cook, two men to handle the sail, the steersman himself, a boy learning the route, and the owner. There was room for others if Harun left part of his trade cargo ashore. He would do that only for payment that justified the loss.

I had imagined taking men north with me. I had not imagined asking a merchant to leave profitable goods on the bank to make space for my ambition.

"How much room?" I asked.

"Enough for you and two more, if their gear is small. Three if we take less cargo. I will not decide which goods to leave until I know what replaces their value."

I went to Hasan's yard. Jamil had sent him the two boards from the day before. Hasan was sitting beside a man who claimed to have delivered grain to the palace kitchens. The man had brought a witness who could name the landing but not the household that received the sacks.

Hasan heard them both, made a mark, and sent them to find the receiving count. Only then did he turn to me.

"You want the ruler's stores," he said.

"I want to know whether there is cargo he would trade north."

"That is a better question. Do you know who will buy it?"

"Harun has buyers on the way."

"Does he have buyers in Maynila?"

"He has not promised to enter Maynila."

Hasan nodded. He pointed to two jars and a row of baskets under the eaves.

"Those are already owed. The ruler can send goods to trade, but only what remains after the households and watch boats receive their shares. Count what is unpledged. Bring Harun here to name the return goods he expects. Jamil will decide whether the bargain serves the port."

I started toward the baskets. Hasan stopped me.

"Do not count Laila's food among them."

"I was not going to."

"You came here from her yard yesterday. Men see a path and imagine it is an account."

I carried the empty board to the storehouse and counted with the keeper. We found more baskets marked for delivery than Hasan's first list showed. Two had been moved to a household whose roof had fallen in the rain. The keeper knew the family. He showed me the mark on the receiving board.

We did not move those baskets back.

When I returned, Hasan had found four unpledged bundles of woven cloth and a small measure of resin fit for trade. They were not enough to purchase a voyage. They might give Harun a reason to make a bargain, if he expected a return cargo that Jamil wanted. Hasan sent for him. I waited while they spoke.

Harun offered a portion of his returning goods against the cloth, with the ruler bearing no loss if Harun turned back before Luzon. He would take me and two companions to a northern landing he chose, and would carry a message onward only if his crew judged the passage safe. If he declined the river, I would have to find another way from the coast. The bargain was written on separate boards so neither party could later pretend that a trading voyage had been an order to fight.

Jamil came before midday. He read the terms, asked Harun what he would do if the coast was hostile, and heard the answer without interrupting.

"I would turn my boat," Harun said.

"And if my kinsman says you must stay?"

"Then he may find a boat that belongs to him."

Jamil looked at me.

"He has the right," I said.

Harun's steersman had been silent until then. He asked who would pay if I brought armed men who frightened the buyers away. I said I would bring no one without Harun's agreement. The steersman asked again. I told him I could not promise the buyers would not be frightened by my name. I could promise that no one aboard would be ordered to fight for my claim.

The steersman studied me, then turned to Harun.

"I can sail on those terms," he said.

Harun made a mark beside the crew list. He did not add my name yet.

I found Sulad repairing a basket beside the timber yard. He had finished giving his witness account to Hasan. The owner who owed him for the downriver work had paid the last share that morning. Sulad was sewing a broken handle for the steersman's wife because he had promised her he would.

"Harun will take two companions," I told him. "If the ruler agrees to the trade."

"Where will he land?"

"Somewhere north of his usual buyers. He will not promise the river."

"And if you ask him to go farther?"

"He may refuse."

Sulad pulled the cord tight through the basket.

"That sounds like a boat that belongs to someone."

"It does."

"What will you ask me to do?"

"Travel with me. Help me speak to people who remember Maynila. Tell me when I am wrong about what they say."

He looked up.

"You will hear that from me whether I travel or not."

"I know."

"No, you don't. When we left, you wanted me beside you because you could call us friends before anyone asked what I wanted. I had my own reasons for leaving. I still do."

He set the basket aside.

"I want to know what happened to my family," he said. "I want to see the river. I do not want to make war for a place in your grandfather's house."

"Neither do I."

"You have wanted to."

"Yes."

He watched me for a while. I could hear the cord creak where he had pulled it through the basket.

"I will go as one of Harun's hands," he said, "if he will take me and pay me for the work. I will help you when the work allows. I will not swear to follow you into a fight."

I wanted him to say he would stand with me whatever came. I had brought him a place on a boat and was disappointed that he had kept the rest of his life for himself.

"Come and tell Harun," I said.

He did. Harun asked him to show how he would mend a torn line. Sulad found a damaged length on the deck and made the splice while Harun watched. Harun offered him a working place, food and a stated share of the passage payment. Sulad asked what would happen if the voyage turned back. Harun said he would pay for the work done and return him to the agreed stopping place. They repeated the terms before the steersman. Sulad accepted.

Harun made the first mark beside a companion's name.

The second place went to a young interpreter named Isan, who had worked on trading boats that met Luzon merchants. I had heard him answer questions in the market. I asked whether he would come, but Harun spoke to him alone before accepting. Isan wanted a fixed return place and would not enter a hostile river. He could speak with coastal traders, not promise to make Maynila obey. Harun accepted him after hearing that distinction.

I had imagined choosing two men who would answer to me. Both men had chosen terms with the owner first.

By afternoon Tamin had counted enough water for his own vessel and the first agreed stopping place. He would travel alongside Harun as far as his salt buyers, then turn home. He would not go to Luzon. He wanted the ruler's letter to make clear that neither boat was part of a war fleet. Harun agreed to carry that request to Jamil.

I went to Safi before sunset. His watch boat was being unloaded, its oars laid across the posts to dry. He had been hearing complaints from men who said the foreign ships had taken their goods. Two accounts described the same chest and neither named the same carrier.

"Are you taking your men?" he asked.

"No. Harun has his crew."

"Good. They have work here."

"I wanted to ask whether you would come."

"I know. I would have refused."

He lifted an oar and ran his hand along a split near the blade. The wood would need binding before it could be used again.

"Your grandfather may give you a letter," he said. "That does not make every boat on the river yours to call. When you reach Luzon, there will be people who remember you as a child. Some will want you to remain one."

"And you?"

"I will remember you as the man who came back from Laoe and could not tell me how many roofs were lost. That will be useful to remember."

I wanted to argue that I had done other things. He knew them. I asked whether he would see the names from the inquiry passed to the right people after I left. He said he would see that Hasan received the watchmen's accounts, but he would not take over Nasir's sister's inquiry. It belonged to her.

Ruma was farther upriver. I found her at the landing after dark, returning with baskets she had sold on her own account. She listened to the terms and said Harun was a careful owner.

"That is not the same as a safe voyage," she added.

"Will you come to see us leave?"

"If I am here. If I have work, I will do it."

She took her basket home. I did not ask again.

Laila was measuring grain by lamplight when I reached her yard. She had already heard that Harun was making room aboard.

"Will you take the work here?" she asked.

"No."

She set the measure down. A boy came for a jar. She sent him to the back shelf and waited until he had carried it away before speaking again.

"Then I will find someone after the market."

"I came to tell you before you paid anyone to wait."

"You have done that."

I had rehearsed a speech about the home I needed to see. She had given me a simple account to settle. I wanted her to make it difficult.

"I thought about staying," I said.

"Good. You should have."

"Would you have wanted me to?"

She folded the cloth over the grain.

"I offered you work. I would not have offered it if I wanted you gone."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can answer."

We stood on opposite sides of the jars. Rain had darkened the wall behind her. I could smell the wet earth beneath the house. She had made a place for me to work and I was leaving it. I had wanted a promise from her that I could carry away without paying its cost.

"I may come back," I said.

"You may. Do not ask me to arrange my life around that."

"I won't."

She looked at my hands.

"Did you count the food for your passage?"

"Hasan and Harun have counted what the ruler may trade. I will pay for my own food. Your store is not in it."

"Good."

She took the lamp and moved it nearer the jars. I had to step aside so she could reach them. I stayed long enough to help lift one cracked jar from the shelf. She thanked me for the work, and I went out before she needed to ask me to leave.

At Hasan's yard I left a written copy of the three names brought back by the inquiry boat. Nasir's sister had the original. Tahir would carry any new witness account to her. The older woman whose son had vanished from the junk had agreed to receive questions through the boat's master while he repaired the hull. The woman in blue and the younger woman with the injured ear were still on the list of people whose fate was not known.

I had not found them. I had not made their absence smaller by writing their names twice.

Jamil called me to the palace enclosure after the lamps had been lit. He had Harun's two boards and Tamin's request. He had spoken to the storekeeper and to Hasan. He asked me what I would do if the coast refused my message.

"I would stay aboard until the owner found a safe landing," I said.

"And if he turned home?"

"I would turn with him, unless I could find passage that did not endanger his men."

"Then you understand that your authority does not extend over his hull."

"I do."

Jamil closed the boards.

"The ruler will hear the bargain in the morning," he said. "He may approve the trade. He may refuse it. If he approves, you must still choose whether to take the place you have paid for."

"I have chosen."

"You have spoken as though you had chosen before. Tomorrow you will have to put your name where it costs you something."

I left the palace with no letter in my hand. At the landing Harun's crew had taken down the sail for the night. The boat was ready enough to be repaired again in the morning. Sulad was sleeping beside the other hands. I could have stayed in Brunei and been paid to count what came in from the water. I had told Laila I would not.

The space where my name belonged on Harun's board was still empty. I knew now what I would give to fill it.

The letter was shorter than I expected.

Siripada had it read to me beneath the same raised house where I had first asked for ships. The rotten crosspiece was gone. A new one held the platform above us, and the carpenters had taken their tools away. Jamil stood beside the ruler with the boards Harun and Tamin had marked.

The letter said I was kin to the ruler of Burné. It asked those who received me to hear my words and allow lawful trade if they wished. It did not say that Maynila belonged to me. It did not order anyone to give me a river, a house, or a throne.

"Is that enough?" Siripada asked.

"It is more than I had when I came here."

"That is not an answer."

"It is enough to ask to be heard."

He watched me while the reader rolled the letter. Jamil put Harun's board beside it.

"The trade goods are counted," Jamil said. "Four bundles of cloth and the resin Hasan marked as unpledged. The kitchens, the watch boats and the households with claims have been supplied first. Harun takes the goods on the return terms he has accepted. If he turns back, he owes only what those terms require. Tamin carries his own cargo and water. Neither owner sails under an order to fight."

"Who leads your party?" Siripada asked.

"I do."

"Who commands Harun's vessel?"

"Harun and the men he has appointed."

"And if he will not enter Maynila?"

"I will ask him where he can land us safely. I will not take his boat from him."

"Who will speak for you when you are ashore?"

"Sulad, if he chooses to speak. Isan where the traders need another tongue. I will speak for my own claim."

Jamil looked at the board.

"Isan has agreed to interpret for the passage and for talks he can understand," he said. "He has not agreed to fight. Sulad has accepted a working place aboard. Neither has pledged to your cause beyond the terms he heard."

"I know."

Siripada held out his hand. Jamil gave him the board. The ruler looked at the marks for a long time. He had made other decisions that morning, about boats to repair and men to feed. This was one more bargain among them. I wanted it to be greater.

"When you came here," he said, "you wanted me to send you back with a name that would make other people stand aside. I could have given you more men. They might have died for a claim they had never heard explained."

"I know."

"You say that often now."

"I have had reasons to learn it."

"You have also had reasons to forget."

I remembered the houses at Laoe and the smell of wet ash. I remembered the foreign deck beneath my feet, where I had been called a prisoner in a language I could barely follow. I had returned to Burné alive and wanted that fact to prove I had earned something.

Siripada gave the board back to Jamil.

"I approve the trade and the letter," he said. "You may lead the people who have agreed to your purpose. If you bring violence to merchants who travel under my name, you will answer for it. If you return, you will tell me what you did. If you do not return, the owners will still be owed what was promised them."

I bowed. The reader brought the letter to Jamil for its final mark. Siripada rose and walked toward the steps. He stopped before climbing them.

"Your home may have found a way to live without you," he said.

"I have thought of that."

"Think of it when you see the shore. Not only here, where it costs nothing."

He went inside.

Jamil stayed. He gave me the letter when it was sealed, but kept the cargo board.

"Harun gets this," he said. "The storekeeper gets a copy. Hasan will keep the account. Do not put all three in your own hands."

"I won't."

He looked at the empty place on the board where my name had been waiting.

"You may fill it," he said.

I wrote my mark beneath the terms I had accepted. It was small enough to fit between two other names.

At the landing, Harun's crew had already begun carrying water aboard. A jar slipped from a man's hands and struck the planks. Its bottom broke, and water spread between the boards before anyone could catch it. The man stared at the pieces.

Harun came from the hold.

"How many remain?" he asked.

"Four sound jars and the one we filled yesterday," the steersman said.

"That was the one."

They counted again. The crew had enough to reach the first stopping place only if they did not spend a day waiting outside the river. The wind had turned. Harun said they could leave on the next tide, but not with that count.

I had seen the boat ready and thought of the men in Maynila who might die before I arrived. The thought came without a name or a reason. I wanted to say we should go while the water was high.

Tamin was standing beside his own vessel, watching the broken jar being carried ashore.

"Can you spare one?" I asked him.

"Not without making my return uncertain."

"Can you sell us one?"

"Not one I have promised to my men."

Harun looked at me. I could feel the answer I had almost given: that the ruler's letter should be worth a jar, that the voyage mattered more than a crewman's thirst.

"Then we wait," I said.

Harun nodded once. He sent a boy to the market for a sound vessel and told the crew to lift the remaining jars into shade. I went with the boy. The first seller offered a jar with a crack concealed beneath a strip of cloth. The second wanted more coin than I had. The third had a jar she would sell if we brought back an empty one to replace it. We carried the broken pieces to show her why we could not.

She looked at the shards, then at the coin in my hand.

"I can give you a smaller jar," she said. "Bring me a sound one on your return, or pay the difference now."

I paid the difference. It was money I had saved for my own landing. I would have less to offer when I reached Luzon.

The jar was filled and sealed in front of the steersman. He tested the stopper, turned it once, and put his mark beside the count. Harun did not praise me. He told the men to load it where it would not strike the others.

By the time the tide rose again, the wind had steadied. Harun sent a man to the mast and another to the forward line. Tamin's crew were already aboard their own vessel, settling the baskets of salt they meant to trade. The two owners had agreed where they would meet after leaving the river. If one boat failed to appear, the other would wait only until the next safe departure. After that each would follow the terms of its own voyage.

I went once more to Hasan's yard.

Nasir's sister was there with Tahir. The three names from the inquiry boat had been copied onto separate boards. One witness had come to speak. He said he had seen a man carried toward the far bank, but he could not name him. Nasir's sister had asked him what the man wore. He described a cloth of a color half the men on the river owned.

"That is what he saw," she said. "Do not put Nasir's name beside it."

Hasan agreed. He gave her the board to keep.

"I am leaving," I told her.

"I know. Your boat is at the outer posts."

"I have left the questions with Hasan and Tahir. They know which men still need to be heard."

She looked at the marks.

"You carried them correctly," she said.

"I wish I could have brought you an answer."

"So do I."

She turned to the witness. He had more to say about the bank, and she wanted to hear it before he went home. I waited for her to look at me again. She did not. I left her asking her own question.

The older woman from the damaged junk was at the next landing. The master had found the timber he needed and was sealing the repaired seam. The boy who had once been held aboard the foreign ship carried resin to him. The injured sailor could sit upright now. He was not ready to work.

The woman had decided to stay while the master finished the repair. She would ask each boat coming from the outer settlements whether anyone had seen her son. I had left her name with Hasan as well. She did not ask me to promise that I would return with news.

At Laila's yard the receiving board hung beneath the eaves. A new worker was lifting baskets onto a dry shelf. Laila saw me from the doorway. She came out with a small cloth bundle.

"For the men who carried the jars," she said. "Their payment was short yesterday. Take it to Hasan on your way."

I almost thought she had brought food for me. I took the bundle and felt the weight of coin inside.

"Are you coming to the landing?" I asked.

"I have grain arriving before dark."

"I understand."

She looked toward the river.

"I hope the people you find will listen," she said.

"So do I."

For a moment I wanted to tell her that I might return when I had settled everything. I had no way to know what that meant. I gave her back the words she had given me.

"If I come back, I will tell you before I ask for a place."

She nodded. Then she went inside to check the baskets.

I delivered the bundle to Hasan. The men who had carried the jars would be paid. I had no reason left to walk the yard again.

Sulad was aboard Harun's boat, sitting beside the line he had mended. Isan stood near the steersman, listening while he named the first stopping place. Neither rose when I stepped onto the deck. They had work to finish.

"Did the ruler agree?" Sulad asked.

"He did."

"To what?"

I told him. He listened until I had named the limits of the letter. He asked whether I had paid for the replacement jar. I said I had.

"You will need money when we land," he said.

"I know."

"You say that often now."

I laughed. He did not. He reached for the line and drew it through his hands once more.

Ruma's boat passed behind us, returning from the upper bank. She raised a hand to Harun. He answered. She did not stop. Safi stood on the watch platform at the outer landing, counting the vessels that went past. He had his own boat to send out when the tide turned. He did not come aboard to bless our departure.

Harun asked whether I had brought my gear. I showed him the small bundle at my feet. He lifted it, judged its weight, and put it beside the others.

"Then we can go," he said.

The steersman waited for Harun's order. The crew had the lines in their hands. I looked back toward the houses, the yards, the palace roofs beyond them. Somewhere inside those streets were people whose names I had learned because they needed something from me. They would continue to need it after I was gone. I had left their questions with people who could answer them. That was the most I had done.

Harun asked me where I wanted the first message sent if we reached the northern coast.

"To the people who still know my family," I said. "Ask who holds the river. Ask who can receive us. Tell them I have come to speak."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then we learn why."

He studied me, then turned to his steersman.

"Cast off," he said.

The first line came free. The boat moved a little, held by the second. Tamin's vessel was already turning in the current. Harun called again. The second line dropped into the water, and the crew drew it aboard before it could catch beneath the hull.

I stood with Sulad near the stern. I had not given the order to move Harun's boat. I had chosen to be on it when he did.

The houses of Burné passed slowly at first. Men were calling from the landings. Smoke rose from the kitchens. A child ran along the bank until the planks ended and stopped with both arms raised. Farther out, the river widened. The boats that had gone before us were small dark marks on the water.

Sulad looked north. I looked with him.

Maynila was somewhere beyond the coast and the islands, beyond the names Harun's steersman could give us. I had left it believing I knew what belonged to me. I was returning with a letter that asked to be heard and men who had agreed to travel only so far.

The wind filled the sail. Harun's boat leaned and gathered speed. Burné began to disappear behind the bend.
