The roof held through the first rain. It failed during the second.

A thin stream found the gap beside my knee and ran down the inside of the wall. The older woman was asleep below. I tried to stop it with my palm, then with a fold of dry thatch, but the water went around both and darkened the mat where she kept her binding fiber.

"Move the mat," I called.

She was awake before I finished. She took the fiber into her arms, pushed the mat clear with her foot, and looked up at me through the opening in the roof.

"Did you tie that part?"

"Yes."

"Then you know where to begin."

She went back inside. I had expected her to climb up and tell me how to fix it. She had already shown me twice.

The rain eased. I worked the old knot loose with fingers still swollen from the day before. The strip of fiber broke when I pulled it through. I had no fresh piece within reach. From the roof I could see the landing, where men were already taking covers off baskets. One of them glanced up at me. I raised my hand, but he was looking at the sky, not at me.

Saliha arrived carrying a coil of fiber over one shoulder. She stopped beneath the leaking edge.

"The woman says you were on the roof before daylight."

"She was on the floor before I could warn her."

"Did you tell her it was the rain?"

"I told her I tied it."

Saliha passed the coil up. I caught it badly and almost lost it over the far side. She waited while I brought it beneath my knee.

"You need the long pieces for the corner," she said. "The short ones will pull free when the thatch swells."

I had used the short pieces because they were easier to draw tight. They had looked neat from below.

Saliha went to the landing to meet the carriers. She had already given me what I needed. I could have called her back to check the knot. Instead I made it, pulled on the thatch until the edge held, and poured a little water from a cup onto the join. The first drops ran outward. The last found a new opening a hand's breadth away.

I pulled the binding apart again.

By the time the sun rose clear of the roofs, the woman had put her fiber back on its mat. She climbed the short ladder and inspected the corner from below. I waited for her to say that I had finished.

"It may hold," she said.

"How will we know?"

"When it rains."

I wanted to ask whether that meant I could leave. I was still standing on her roof.

At the landing, a boat lay with its bow raised on two lengths of timber. A man had climbed under it with a shell scraper. Two others held a plank against the side, arguing over where the damaged edge ended. The boat had not been there the evening before.

A woman stood in the shallow water with her skirt gathered above her knees. She was holding a basket of wet wooden pegs. Each time one of the men called, she passed him a peg, then returned to counting the rest. A boy beside her kept dipping water from the boat's belly. There was little water in it, but more came through the seam whenever someone shifted the hull.

Jalal was speaking to the woman. He saw me coming and did not beckon.

I stopped near the baskets. The boy dipped another bowlful of water and emptied it over the side. He looked at me, then at the bowl.

"Do you want me to take it?" I asked.

"Do you know where to pour?"

"Out of the boat."

"Not onto the wood we are trying to keep dry."

He handed me the bowl and pointed toward the lower side of the bank. I climbed in. The floor was slick with mud and shavings. Water stood around the heel of my foot.

The woman looked up from her pegs.

"Who put him there?"

The boy pointed at me. "He asked."

"That is not an answer."

I climbed out again. Jalal had come to stand beside her.

"He worked on my aunt's roof," he said.

"Is it finished?"

"Ask my aunt."

The woman turned toward the house. The older woman was watching from her doorway.

"It held one rain," she called.

"Then perhaps he can carry wood," the boat owner said.

She gave her name only after I asked whether there was work for a stranger. I did not know whether to repeat it to myself or mark it somewhere. I had no board left that was mine alone. She told me I could call her **Mara**, and that it would not make me one of her crew.

Her boat had struck something while being brought in at low water. The crew had taken the load ashore. They had a damaged plank to replace, and a man waiting at another landing for the goods she had promised to carry onward. I asked where she intended to sail.

"When the boat is ready."

"Toward Burné?"

She looked at Jalal. "Is that what he wants?"

"He wants to return," Jalal said. "He has no food for the passage."

"Then he has no passage."

"I can work," I said.

"So can the boy. He knows the boat."

The boy, still holding the bowl, grinned at me. His hands were red from the water.

Mara counted the pegs into two piles. She had more in the first than in the second. She set the smaller pile aside and asked the man under the hull whether he had found sound wood beyond the split. He said the split ran farther than they had thought.

"Then stop scraping there," she said. "We will have to lift it."

The men lowered the plank they had been holding. The boy climbed out of the boat. I could hear the water working under the hull as it settled against the timber.

"I could carry what you take out," I said.

"To where?"

I looked around. There were stacks of boards beside the house and a dry space beneath its floor. I pointed to the dry space.

"Those belong to another owner," Mara said. "Ask before you put my wood there."

I went to the house. The man who owned the space said he would not take wet timber under his floor. He had rice stored there. I returned and told Mara.

She nodded once, as if I had given her a useful answer. Then she pointed toward a bare patch above the highest mark left by the tide.

"Put the wood there, on two stones. If it touches the ground, it stays wet. If the tide reaches it, you carry it again."

I moved the stones. The first length of timber was heavier than I expected. It scraped against my shoulder and left a dark line across my skin. When I put it down, Mara came over and made me lift one end.

"Turn the split side upward," she said. "I need to see it."

I had carried it so that the damage was hidden.

I turned it. The split was longer than I had thought, a narrow black opening that followed the grain. I remembered the line that had cut into Ruma's boat when I was learning to steer. I had been proud to know how a loaded vessel answered the current. This plank had answered a stone, and a man with a scraper had found the damage afterward.

By midday the sun had drawn steam from the wet boards. The workers took turns under the hull. I carried wood, fetched water for washing the mud away, and held a length of timber while they shifted the boat's weight. Once I moved before the man at the stern told me to. The timber slipped from its place, and the boat came down hard enough to send everyone back.

No one was hurt. The man under the hull crawled out on his elbows, his face gray with mud.

"Who lifted that?" he asked.

"I did."

"Why?"

"I thought you were ready."

"Did you hear me say it?"

"No."

He spat into the water and sat down. Mara came to inspect the support. She found a crushed edge where the boat had settled against it.

"We need another length," she said.

"I can fetch one."

"You can ask whose it is."

I wanted her to be angrier. It would have been easier to answer anger than the quiet way she looked at the timber I had spoiled.

The replacement belonged to the house with the dry space. Its owner asked to be paid before it was moved. Mara said she would pay when the boat sailed. He said the wood would not sail until he was paid. I had no rice left to offer either of them.

Jalal came back from the landing carrying a basket of shellfish. He heard the argument and put his basket down.

"I can lend him a length from our house," he said.

Mara looked at him. "Can you?"

"My aunt has wood."

"Has she offered it?"

He looked toward the house. The older woman was not there. He picked up his basket again and went to ask.

He returned without timber. His aunt had said that the wood beside her house was for a new ladder. She would not lend it for a boat that might leave before it was returned.

"She said no," Jalal told Mara.

"I heard her," Mara answered.

I looked at the crushed support. I had made another person bargain for a loss I had caused. There was no way to repair the timber by apologizing to it.

"I can work off its price," I said.

Mara shook her head. "The man who owns it wants rice now. You can work tomorrow and still not put rice in his hand today."

She asked the boy to fetch the owner of the dry space again. When he came, she counted out rice from the parcel set aside for her crew. He watched her measure it. She told him that the payment would be entered against my work, and that I would not be given food from her crew's share until it had been earned back.

"That is your arrangement with him," the owner said. "I have my rice."

He let the men carry the timber.

I wanted to say that I would repay her before she sailed. I had already learned what such a promise was worth when spoken by someone who had no food. I asked what work remained.

"More than you can do today."

"Then I will come tomorrow."

"If you come, you work under the man who knows where the supports go."

I nodded.

"And if he tells you to stop, you stop."

"I will."

She looked at the boy. "Tell him what happened when he moved early."

The boy had seen it. He described the timber falling, the man crawling out, the edge of the support breaking. He did not mention my title or the people I knew in Burné. I had not told him either.

That evening I went back to the roof. The older woman had saved me a bowl of water, but no food. She said the workers had finished the corner while I was at the landing.

"Then it held?"

"It has not rained."

She asked whether I had earned passage. I told her I had earned a debt for a piece of timber.

"That is quick work," she said.

She handed me a coil of fiber and told me to wind it tightly for the morning. I sat beside the doorway and wound it until the last light had gone. My hands hurt. I could smell rice cooking in the house next to hers.

On the second day Mara let me work on the boat's load instead of its hull. On the third she let me help pass baskets aboard, though the boy still chose where they went. I learned to wait for the man's call before lifting the timber, and to ask before moving anything that belonged to another person. My shoulders were sore in places that had not hurt since the first crossing out of Maynila.

When Mara paid the workers, she counted my portion separately. The price of the broken support came out first. The rest was enough for a small parcel of rice and dried fish. I asked how much more I would need for a passage to Burné.

"I am not sailing straight to Burné," she said.

"Where will you go?"

"To the landing where the man is waiting for his goods. After that I will take the work I can find."

"Would you carry me as far as that landing?"

"With what food?"

I showed her the parcel.

She asked the boy to count it. He did, then looked at Mara.

"For the first leg," he said.

"And if we cannot leave again?" she asked me.

"I will have to find work there."

"Will you give the food away before we reach it?"

I thought of the roof worker who had taken my last parcel. I could still see him pulling my knot loose with one hand.

"I might," I said. "If I choose to, you may put me ashore."

Mara studied me for a moment. She looked toward the boat, where the repaired plank lay against the new support.

"You will not be carried because you know Jalal," she said. "He did not ask me to carry you. You will work when my crew asks, and you will not direct them."

"I understand."

"The next landing is all I promise."

I held out the parcel for her to inspect. She did not take it. She told the boy to find a dry place for it among the food, marked separately so that no one would eat it by mistake.

I went to tell the older woman I would be leaving. She was standing beneath the corner of her roof, looking up at a new cloud. When I told her what Mara had agreed, she asked whether the boat was sound.

"The men have repaired it," I said.

"Did you inspect their work?"

"I carried what they told me to carry."

"Then say that."

She went inside and came back with the piece of fiber I had wound the night before. She put it in my hand.

"Your knot was loose," she said. "This one is tight. Remember the difference."

I could have told her I had helped save the roof. The corner still needed another rain before anyone would know.

At the landing the boy was making space for my parcel. Mara's crew had already begun loading. No one looked up when I came aboard. I found the place the boy had shown me, set down the coil of fiber, and waited to be told what to lift.

I was going back by one shore at a time.

---

The first landing was not Burné.

Mara told me so before we left, but I had been watching the repaired plank and had heard only the part of her answer that allowed me aboard. At the landing she called for the owner of the goods, and the boy began carrying baskets ashore. I waited for her to tell me which to lift.

"Your food is counted to here," she said.

"I know."

"Then eat it before it spoils."

"Will you go farther?"

"Not with these goods. The man who owns them has to decide what comes next."

I had spent the voyage imagining the next boat. There was no next boat waiting. There were only a row of covered baskets, two women mending a net beneath a roof, and a man arguing with Mara about the condition of a jar.

The boy came back for another load. He looked at my parcel, still tied where he had put it.

"Are you staying?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Then take your food ashore. If it is on our boat when we leave, Mara will think it is ours."

I took it. The boy did not help me carry it. He had a basket to move.

Mara stayed long enough to speak with a boatman who sometimes carried goods toward Burné. He was leaving after his cargo arrived from inland. She told him what work I had done, including the support I had broken. He listened and asked whether I could keep a list of passengers who paid for their own food.

"Yes," I said.

"That was not a question for you," he answered.

He asked Mara whether I could follow an instruction when I disagreed with it.

"On the third day he could," she said.

The boatman laughed. Mara did not.

He had no place for me until the inland cargo came, and he would not promise that it would come. I spent the next days carrying water to the men repairing a landing post and helping the women with the net when they let me. The knots they used were different from those on the older woman's roof. I made them too tight. The first woman loosened one with a bone pin and made me watch her hands.

"That will pull the mesh out of shape," she said. "A net has to give."

I tried again. By the time the cargo came, I had enough rice to eat on another passage and enough blisters that the boatman believed Mara's account of my work.

He took me as far as the river mouths from which men went on to Burné. At the last change of boats, the owner would carry me only because I could help unload his jars. I knew what they cost him if one broke. I carried them carefully.

When I saw the houses on piles again, I was not standing at the steering place. I was crouched beside a jar with both hands around its neck, keeping it from striking another as the boat came alongside.

I had once left this city thinking I could return with a name worth more than my father's. I came back smelling of wet rope and fish.

Nasir's sister was at the lower landing. I recognized the basket before I saw her face. She had tied a new piece of cord around its handle. I waited while she sold two fish to a woman with a child on her hip. She counted the payment, put it away, and turned toward me.

"Did you find him?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you where he went?"

"No one I could verify."

She looked at my hands. "You have been working."

"To pay for the passage."

"Did you send the account?"

"Samad carried it for Hasan. I do not know whether Hasan has received it."

"He has. He sent a man to tell me you had heard nothing."

I had asked Samad to remind Hasan of that duty. I had imagined the message arriving as a kindness. Nasir's sister had already heard it, and her brother was still missing.

"Have the men at the traps said anything new?" I asked.

"One says he saw a boat after the shouting. Another says he saw the same boat before. They cannot both be right unless the boat stayed all day."

"Who is asking them?"

"I am."

She lifted her basket. I stepped aside to let her pass.

"I was going to see Hasan," I said.

"Then tell him I will come when there is something he can show me. I have listened to enough men explain what they did not see."

She went up the steps. I did not follow. The smell of the fish stayed with me after she was gone.

Hasan received me in the room where I had first been asked to separate two men's accounts. My copy of the eastern landing's record lay on a shelf beside a jar of dry sand. It had been kept flat. I had carried it in my mind through every change of boat, wondering which marks would be questioned. Now I could see that someone had already made notes beside it.

"Samad was careful," Hasan said. "He read it to me before he left."

"Did Yusuf agree with it?"

"He agreed that you wrote what was said. He disputes what should follow."

"Karim received five rolls."

"His receiver says so."

"And the sixth is still held."

"As far as the last message tells us."

I waited for Hasan to ask whether I had made the right bargain. He asked whether I had seen Nasir.

"No."

"Then we have not gained a witness to his fate."

I felt the words more sharply than I expected. I had gone east, spent food, and returned by three separate passages. The answer was still no.

Hasan took another board from the shelf. It listed goods lost with Hamzah's boat and the names of those who claimed them. One line had been crossed through, then written again with a smaller amount.

"Who changed that?" I asked.

"The owner. He found some of the goods elsewhere. The loss is less than he first reported."

"That is good."

"For him. It does not tell us where Nasir is."

He put the board down.

"Jamil wants to hear from you. Safi has already given his account. You will be asked about the shorter passage again."

"I will answer."

"You answered before. The question now is what you have learned that can be trusted when no one has time to question you."

I thought of the timber falling beside Mara's boat. The man beneath it had come out with mud on his face. I had wanted to move before he was ready.

"I learned that I can be useful and still make the work more dangerous," I said.

Hasan looked at me for a moment.

"That is a beginning. Do not make it the end of your answer."

Jamil was beside the lower store when I found him. Safi stood with two boatmen under a shade cover. They had laid out jars, cord, and folded mats for a patrol that would leave when the water rose. One jar had a chipped rim. A man had set it apart.

Jamil saw me approach. He finished speaking to the boatmen before he turned.

"You came back."

"Yes."

"Whose boat?"

I named Mara and the men who had carried me on the later passages. He asked what I had paid. I told him about the broken support and the work that followed. Safi listened without smiling.

"Do you still believe a prince can give a boat an order because he sees a shorter way?" Jamil asked.

"No."

"Would you give one if you thought the crew would die without it?"

I had expected the first question. The second left me looking at the jars.

"I would tell the owner what I saw," I said.

"And if he did not hear you?"

"I would make him hear."

"How?"

I could not answer without imagining the boat, the water, the people in it. The man with the chipped jar lifted it and turned the rim toward the light.

"I might shout," I said. "I might take hold of him. I do not know."

Jamil nodded toward the jars.

"Then do something you can know. We have a patrol to provision. The owners will say what their boats can carry. You will tell Hasan how much water and food the men have agreed to take, and what remains here. You will not decide their departure or the order in which they travel."

I looked at the boats. It was a smaller assignment than the one I had held before Hamzah disappeared.

"I accept."

"Then ask them."

The first boatman told me his crew had counted water for two days. The second said his men would not take the same amount because their boat sat lower when loaded. I asked how many men he carried. He gave me a number. I began to multiply it by the first boat's ration.

Safi stopped me.

"Did he say he would stay out two days?"

"No."

"Ask."

The second boatman said his vessel would return by the following tide if the weather held. If it did not, he would take shelter at a landing he knew. He had already set aside food there with a relative. He would not carry extra jars at the cost of making the boat harder to handle.

I had almost written that he was short of water. He was not short according to the journey he intended to make.

The first boatman watched me correct the count.

"Your prince is learning," he said to Safi.

Safi did not answer. He took the chipped jar from the man who had set it aside and asked whether the rim would cut a hand when the boat moved. The man ran a finger over the edge and said it would. Safi told him to leave it.

Jamil watched all of us. He made no sign that I had passed a test.

When the jars had been assigned, Hasan checked my marks against the boatmen's spoken counts. He found that I had left one vessel's reserve water in the wrong column. I corrected it before the men loaded. The work took most of the afternoon.

At the end, Jamil asked the boatmen whether they accepted the count. They did. One added that he would change it if the wind shifted before departure.

"You may," Jamil said. "It is your boat."

I had thought the count would be the last word. The boatman was already preparing for the possibility that it would not be.

A messenger came down from the upper houses as the men began to load. He carried a strip of dyed cloth tied around a small bundle. He was out of breath. Jamil sent him to drink before he spoke.

The man said two parties had arrived from a settlement farther along the Borneo coast. One claimed to speak for merchants who had sent goods toward a place called Laoe. The other said those goods had been taken under the authority of a ruler across the water. They had come separately, and each had asked the court to hear him before the other.

"Who sent them?" Jamil asked.

"Each says the other has no right to speak."

"That is not what I asked."

The messenger named the households where they were staying. Jamil looked at Hasan.

"Find out who has seen the goods," he said. "Not only who claims them."

Safi asked whether vessels were being called together.

"Not yet," Jamil said. "We have heard a quarrel, not an order."

He turned to me.

"You will bring the counts you made today when I ask for them. Nothing else."

I wanted to ask whether Laoe was the place men had been speaking of in the market, the shore that had refused Burné's ruler. I wanted to know whether the king would send ships, and whether there would be a place for me among them. The messenger had not even finished drinking.

"Yes," I said.

Jamil went toward the upper houses with Hasan. Safi stayed to help the boatmen settle the last jars. He lifted one by its handles, waited for the man in the boat to call, and passed it down.

I took the other handle. The jar was heavier than I expected. Safi adjusted his grip without looking at me. We lowered it together, and neither of us spoke until the man below had it safe.

Then Safi said, "You still put your weight on the wrong side."

"I know."

"Good. Move your foot."

I moved it. The man in the boat shifted the jar into its bed of matting. Above us the messenger was gone. I kept looking toward the upper houses until Safi told me there was another jar to lift.
