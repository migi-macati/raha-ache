The roof held through the first rain. It failed during the second.

A thin stream found the gap beside my knee and ran down the inside of the wall. The older woman was asleep below. I tried to stop it with my palm, then with a fold of dry thatch, but the water went around both and darkened the mat where she kept her binding fiber.

"Move the mat," I called.

She was awake before I finished. She took the fiber into her arms, pushed the mat clear with her foot, and looked up at me through the opening in the roof.

"Did you tie that part?"

"Yes."

"Then you know where to begin."

She went back inside. I had expected her to climb up and tell me how to fix it. She had already shown me twice.

The rain eased. I worked the old knot loose with fingers still swollen from the day before. The strip of fiber broke when I pulled it through. I had no fresh piece within reach. From the roof I could see the landing, where men were already taking covers off baskets. One of them glanced up at me. I raised my hand, but he was looking at the sky, not at me.

Saliha arrived carrying a coil of fiber over one shoulder. She stopped beneath the leaking edge.

"The woman says you were on the roof before daylight."

"She was on the floor before I could warn her."

"Did you tell her it was the rain?"

"I told her I tied it."

Saliha passed the coil up. I caught it badly and almost lost it over the far side. She waited while I brought it beneath my knee.

"You need the long pieces for the corner," she said. "The short ones will pull free when the thatch swells."

I had used the short pieces because they were easier to draw tight. They had looked neat from below.

Saliha went to the landing to meet the carriers. She had already given me what I needed. I could have called her back to check the knot. Instead I made it, pulled on the thatch until the edge held, and poured a little water from a cup onto the join. The first drops ran outward. The last found a new opening a hand's breadth away.

I pulled the binding apart again.

By the time the sun rose clear of the roofs, the woman had put her fiber back on its mat. She climbed the short ladder and inspected the corner from below. I waited for her to say that I had finished.

"It may hold," she said.

"How will we know?"

"When it rains."

I wanted to ask whether that meant I could leave. I was still standing on her roof.

At the landing, a boat lay with its bow raised on two lengths of timber. A man had climbed under it with a shell scraper. Two others held a plank against the side, arguing over where the damaged edge ended. The boat had not been there the evening before.

A woman stood in the shallow water with her skirt gathered above her knees. She was holding a basket of wet wooden pegs. Each time one of the men called, she passed him a peg, then returned to counting the rest. A boy beside her kept dipping water from the boat's belly. There was little water in it, but more came through the seam whenever someone shifted the hull.

Jalal was speaking to the woman. He saw me coming and did not beckon.

I stopped near the baskets. The boy dipped another bowlful of water and emptied it over the side. He looked at me, then at the bowl.

"Do you want me to take it?" I asked.

"Do you know where to pour?"

"Out of the boat."

"Not onto the wood we are trying to keep dry."

He handed me the bowl and pointed toward the lower side of the bank. I climbed in. The floor was slick with mud and shavings. Water stood around the heel of my foot.

The woman looked up from her pegs.

"Who put him there?"

The boy pointed at me. "He asked."

"That is not an answer."

I climbed out again. Jalal had come to stand beside her.

"He worked on my aunt's roof," he said.

"Is it finished?"

"Ask my aunt."

The woman turned toward the house. The older woman was watching from her doorway.

"It held one rain," she called.

"Then perhaps he can carry wood," the boat owner said.

She gave her name only after I asked whether there was work for a stranger. I did not know whether to repeat it to myself or mark it somewhere. I had no board left that was mine alone. She told me I could call her **Mara**, and that it would not make me one of her crew.

Her boat had struck something while being brought in at low water. The crew had taken the load ashore. They had a damaged plank to replace, and a man waiting at another landing for the goods she had promised to carry onward. I asked where she intended to sail.

"When the boat is ready."

"Toward Burné?"

She looked at Jalal. "Is that what he wants?"

"He wants to return," Jalal said. "He has no food for the passage."

"Then he has no passage."

"I can work," I said.

"So can the boy. He knows the boat."

The boy, still holding the bowl, grinned at me. His hands were red from the water.

Mara counted the pegs into two piles. She had more in the first than in the second. She set the smaller pile aside and asked the man under the hull whether he had found sound wood beyond the split. He said the split ran farther than they had thought.

"Then stop scraping there," she said. "We will have to lift it."

The men lowered the plank they had been holding. The boy climbed out of the boat. I could hear the water working under the hull as it settled against the timber.

"I could carry what you take out," I said.

"To where?"

I looked around. There were stacks of boards beside the house and a dry space beneath its floor. I pointed to the dry space.

"Those belong to another owner," Mara said. "Ask before you put my wood there."

I went to the house. The man who owned the space said he would not take wet timber under his floor. He had rice stored there. I returned and told Mara.

She nodded once, as if I had given her a useful answer. Then she pointed toward a bare patch above the highest mark left by the tide.

"Put the wood there, on two stones. If it touches the ground, it stays wet. If the tide reaches it, you carry it again."

I moved the stones. The first length of timber was heavier than I expected. It scraped against my shoulder and left a dark line across my skin. When I put it down, Mara came over and made me lift one end.

"Turn the split side upward," she said. "I need to see it."

I had carried it so that the damage was hidden.

I turned it. The split was longer than I had thought, a narrow black opening that followed the grain. I remembered the line that had cut into Ruma's boat when I was learning to steer. I had been proud to know how a loaded vessel answered the current. This plank had answered a stone, and a man with a scraper had found the damage afterward.

By midday the sun had drawn steam from the wet boards. The workers took turns under the hull. I carried wood, fetched water for washing the mud away, and held a length of timber while they shifted the boat's weight. Once I moved before the man at the stern told me to. The timber slipped from its place, and the boat came down hard enough to send everyone back.

No one was hurt. The man under the hull crawled out on his elbows, his face gray with mud.

"Who lifted that?" he asked.

"I did."

"Why?"

"I thought you were ready."

"Did you hear me say it?"

"No."

He spat into the water and sat down. Mara came to inspect the support. She found a crushed edge where the boat had settled against it.

"We need another length," she said.

"I can fetch one."

"You can ask whose it is."

I wanted her to be angrier. It would have been easier to answer anger than the quiet way she looked at the timber I had spoiled.

The replacement belonged to the house with the dry space. Its owner asked to be paid before it was moved. Mara said she would pay when the boat sailed. He said the wood would not sail until he was paid. I had no rice left to offer either of them.

Jalal came back from the landing carrying a basket of shellfish. He heard the argument and put his basket down.

"I can lend him a length from our house," he said.

Mara looked at him. "Can you?"

"My aunt has wood."

"Has she offered it?"

He looked toward the house. The older woman was not there. He picked up his basket again and went to ask.

He returned without timber. His aunt had said that the wood beside her house was for a new ladder. She would not lend it for a boat that might leave before it was returned.

"She said no," Jalal told Mara.

"I heard her," Mara answered.

I looked at the crushed support. I had made another person bargain for a loss I had caused. There was no way to repair the timber by apologizing to it.

"I can work off its price," I said.

Mara shook her head. "The man who owns it wants rice now. You can work tomorrow and still not put rice in his hand today."

She asked the boy to fetch the owner of the dry space again. When he came, she counted out rice from the parcel set aside for her crew. He watched her measure it. She told him that the payment would be entered against my work, and that I would not be given food from her crew's share until it had been earned back.

"That is your arrangement with him," the owner said. "I have my rice."

He let the men carry the timber.

I wanted to say that I would repay her before she sailed. I had already learned what such a promise was worth when spoken by someone who had no food. I asked what work remained.

"More than you can do today."

"Then I will come tomorrow."

"If you come, you work under the man who knows where the supports go."

I nodded.

"And if he tells you to stop, you stop."

"I will."

She looked at the boy. "Tell him what happened when he moved early."

The boy had seen it. He described the timber falling, the man crawling out, the edge of the support breaking. He did not mention my title or the people I knew in Burné. I had not told him either.

That evening I went back to the roof. The older woman had saved me a bowl of water, but no food. She said the workers had finished the corner while I was at the landing.

"Then it held?"

"It has not rained."

She asked whether I had earned passage. I told her I had earned a debt for a piece of timber.

"That is quick work," she said.

She handed me a coil of fiber and told me to wind it tightly for the morning. I sat beside the doorway and wound it until the last light had gone. My hands hurt. I could smell rice cooking in the house next to hers.

On the second day Mara let me work on the boat's load instead of its hull. On the third she let me help pass baskets aboard, though the boy still chose where they went. I learned to wait for the man's call before lifting the timber, and to ask before moving anything that belonged to another person. My shoulders were sore in places that had not hurt since the first crossing out of Maynila.

When Mara paid the workers, she counted my portion separately. The price of the broken support came out first. The rest was enough for a small parcel of rice and dried fish. I asked how much more I would need for a passage to Burné.

"I am not sailing straight to Burné," she said.

"Where will you go?"

"To the landing where the man is waiting for his goods. After that I will take the work I can find."

"Would you carry me as far as that landing?"

"With what food?"

I showed her the parcel.

She asked the boy to count it. He did, then looked at Mara.

"For the first leg," he said.

"And if we cannot leave again?" she asked me.

"I will have to find work there."

"Will you give the food away before we reach it?"

I thought of the roof worker who had taken my last parcel. I could still see him pulling my knot loose with one hand.

"I might," I said. "If I choose to, you may put me ashore."

Mara studied me for a moment. She looked toward the boat, where the repaired plank lay against the new support.

"You will not be carried because you know Jalal," she said. "He did not ask me to carry you. You will work when my crew asks, and you will not direct them."

"I understand."

"The next landing is all I promise."

I held out the parcel for her to inspect. She did not take it. She told the boy to find a dry place for it among the food, marked separately so that no one would eat it by mistake.

I went to tell the older woman I would be leaving. She was standing beneath the corner of her roof, looking up at a new cloud. When I told her what Mara had agreed, she asked whether the boat was sound.

"The men have repaired it," I said.

"Did you inspect their work?"

"I carried what they told me to carry."

"Then say that."

She went inside and came back with the piece of fiber I had wound the night before. She put it in my hand.

"Your knot was loose," she said. "This one is tight. Remember the difference."

I could have told her I had helped save the roof. The corner still needed another rain before anyone would know.

At the landing the boy was making space for my parcel. Mara's crew had already begun loading. No one looked up when I came aboard. I found the place the boy had shown me, set down the coil of fiber, and waited to be told what to lift.

I was going back by one shore at a time.

---

The first landing was not Burné.

Mara told me so before we left, but I had been watching the repaired plank and had heard only the part of her answer that allowed me aboard. At the landing she called for the owner of the goods, and the boy began carrying baskets ashore. I waited for her to tell me which to lift.

"Your food is counted to here," she said.

"I know."

"Then eat it before it spoils."

"Will you go farther?"

"Not with these goods. The man who owns them has to decide what comes next."

I had spent the voyage imagining the next boat. There was no next boat waiting. There were only a row of covered baskets, two women mending a net beneath a roof, and a man arguing with Mara about the condition of a jar.

The boy came back for another load. He looked at my parcel, still tied where he had put it.

"Are you staying?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Then take your food ashore. If it is on our boat when we leave, Mara will think it is ours."

I took it. The boy did not help me carry it. He had a basket to move.

Mara stayed long enough to speak with a boatman who sometimes carried goods toward Burné. He was leaving after his cargo arrived from inland. She told him what work I had done, including the support I had broken. He listened and asked whether I could keep a list of passengers who paid for their own food.

"Yes," I said.

"That was not a question for you," he answered.

He asked Mara whether I could follow an instruction when I disagreed with it.

"On the third day he could," she said.

The boatman laughed. Mara did not.

He had no place for me until the inland cargo came, and he would not promise that it would come. I spent the next days carrying water to the men repairing a landing post and helping the women with the net when they let me. The knots they used were different from those on the older woman's roof. I made them too tight. The first woman loosened one with a bone pin and made me watch her hands.

"That will pull the mesh out of shape," she said. "A net has to give."

I tried again. By the time the cargo came, I had enough rice to eat on another passage and enough blisters that the boatman believed Mara's account of my work.

He took me as far as the river mouths from which men went on to Burné. At the last change of boats, the owner would carry me only because I could help unload his jars. I knew what they cost him if one broke. I carried them carefully.

When I saw the houses on piles again, I was not standing at the steering place. I was crouched beside a jar with both hands around its neck, keeping it from striking another as the boat came alongside.

I had once left this city thinking I could return with a name worth more than my father's. I came back smelling of wet rope and fish.

Nasir's sister was at the lower landing. I recognized the basket before I saw her face. She had tied a new piece of cord around its handle. I waited while she sold two fish to a woman with a child on her hip. She counted the payment, put it away, and turned toward me.

"Did you find him?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you where he went?"

"No one I could verify."

She looked at my hands. "You have been working."

"To pay for the passage."

"Did you send the account?"

"Samad carried it for Hasan. I do not know whether Hasan has received it."

"He has. He sent a man to tell me you had heard nothing."

I had asked Samad to remind Hasan of that duty. I had imagined the message arriving as a kindness. Nasir's sister had already heard it, and her brother was still missing.

"Have the men at the traps said anything new?" I asked.

"One says he saw a boat after the shouting. Another says he saw the same boat before. They cannot both be right unless the boat stayed all day."

"Who is asking them?"

"I am."

She lifted her basket. I stepped aside to let her pass.

"I was going to see Hasan," I said.

"Then tell him I will come when there is something he can show me. I have listened to enough men explain what they did not see."

She went up the steps. I did not follow. The smell of the fish stayed with me after she was gone.

Hasan received me in the room where I had first been asked to separate two men's accounts. My copy of the eastern landing's record lay on a shelf beside a jar of dry sand. It had been kept flat. I had carried it in my mind through every change of boat, wondering which marks would be questioned. Now I could see that someone had already made notes beside it.

"Samad was careful," Hasan said. "He read it to me before he left."

"Did Yusuf agree with it?"

"He agreed that you wrote what was said. He disputes what should follow."

"Karim received five rolls."

"His receiver says so."

"And the sixth is still held."

"As far as the last message tells us."

I waited for Hasan to ask whether I had made the right bargain. He asked whether I had seen Nasir.

"No."

"Then we have not gained a witness to his fate."

I felt the words more sharply than I expected. I had gone east, spent food, and returned by three separate passages. The answer was still no.

Hasan took another board from the shelf. It listed goods lost with Hamzah's boat and the names of those who claimed them. One line had been crossed through, then written again with a smaller amount.

"Who changed that?" I asked.

"The owner. He found some of the goods elsewhere. The loss is less than he first reported."

"That is good."

"For him. It does not tell us where Nasir is."

He put the board down.

"Jamil wants to hear from you. Safi has already given his account. You will be asked about the shorter passage again."

"I will answer."

"You answered before. The question now is what you have learned that can be trusted when no one has time to question you."

I thought of the timber falling beside Mara's boat. The man beneath it had come out with mud on his face. I had wanted to move before he was ready.

"I learned that I can be useful and still make the work more dangerous," I said.

Hasan looked at me for a moment.

"That is a beginning. Do not make it the end of your answer."

Jamil was beside the lower store when I found him. Safi stood with two boatmen under a shade cover. They had laid out jars, cord, and folded mats for a patrol that would leave when the water rose. One jar had a chipped rim. A man had set it apart.

Jamil saw me approach. He finished speaking to the boatmen before he turned.

"You came back."

"Yes."

"Whose boat?"

I named Mara and the men who had carried me on the later passages. He asked what I had paid. I told him about the broken support and the work that followed. Safi listened without smiling.

"Do you still believe a prince can give a boat an order because he sees a shorter way?" Jamil asked.

"No."

"Would you give one if you thought the crew would die without it?"

I had expected the first question. The second left me looking at the jars.

"I would tell the owner what I saw," I said.

"And if he did not hear you?"

"I would make him hear."

"How?"

I could not answer without imagining the boat, the water, the people in it. The man with the chipped jar lifted it and turned the rim toward the light.

"I might shout," I said. "I might take hold of him. I do not know."

Jamil nodded toward the jars.

"Then do something you can know. We have a patrol to provision. The owners will say what their boats can carry. You will tell Hasan how much water and food the men have agreed to take, and what remains here. You will not decide their departure or the order in which they travel."

I looked at the boats. It was a smaller assignment than the one I had held before Hamzah disappeared.

"I accept."

"Then ask them."

The first boatman told me his crew had counted water for two days. The second said his men would not take the same amount because their boat sat lower when loaded. I asked how many men he carried. He gave me a number. I began to multiply it by the first boat's ration.

Safi stopped me.

"Did he say he would stay out two days?"

"No."

"Ask."

The second boatman said his vessel would return by the following tide if the weather held. If it did not, he would take shelter at a landing he knew. He had already set aside food there with a relative. He would not carry extra jars at the cost of making the boat harder to handle.

I had almost written that he was short of water. He was not short according to the journey he intended to make.

The first boatman watched me correct the count.

"Your prince is learning," he said to Safi.

Safi did not answer. He took the chipped jar from the man who had set it aside and asked whether the rim would cut a hand when the boat moved. The man ran a finger over the edge and said it would. Safi told him to leave it.

Jamil watched all of us. He made no sign that I had passed a test.

When the jars had been assigned, Hasan checked my marks against the boatmen's spoken counts. He found that I had left one vessel's reserve water in the wrong column. I corrected it before the men loaded. The work took most of the afternoon.

At the end, Jamil asked the boatmen whether they accepted the count. They did. One added that he would change it if the wind shifted before departure.

"You may," Jamil said. "It is your boat."

I had thought the count would be the last word. The boatman was already preparing for the possibility that it would not be.

A messenger came down from the upper houses as the men began to load. He carried a strip of dyed cloth tied around a small bundle. He was out of breath. Jamil sent him to drink before he spoke.

The man said two parties had arrived from a settlement farther along the Borneo coast. One claimed to speak for merchants who had sent goods toward a place called Laoe. The other said those goods had been taken under the authority of a ruler across the water. They had come separately, and each had asked the court to hear him before the other.

"Who sent them?" Jamil asked.

"Each says the other has no right to speak."

"That is not what I asked."

The messenger named the households where they were staying. Jamil looked at Hasan.

"Find out who has seen the goods," he said. "Not only who claims them."

Safi asked whether vessels were being called together.

"Not yet," Jamil said. "We have heard a quarrel, not an order."

He turned to me.

"You will bring the counts you made today when I ask for them. Nothing else."

I wanted to ask whether Laoe was the place men had been speaking of in the market, the shore that had refused Burné's ruler. I wanted to know whether the king would send ships, and whether there would be a place for me among them. The messenger had not even finished drinking.

"Yes," I said.

Jamil went toward the upper houses with Hasan. Safi stayed to help the boatmen settle the last jars. He lifted one by its handles, waited for the man in the boat to call, and passed it down.

I took the other handle. The jar was heavier than I expected. Safi adjusted his grip without looking at me. We lowered it together, and neither of us spoke until the man below had it safe.

Then Safi said, "You still put your weight on the wrong side."

"I know."

"Good. Move your foot."

I moved it. The man in the boat shifted the jar into its bed of matting. Above us the messenger was gone. I kept looking toward the upper houses until Safi told me there was another jar to lift.

---

The man from Laoe had brought a piece of broken timber.

He set it on the floor of the audience house with both hands, as carefully as if it were a sleeping child. One end had been cut square. The other had split along the grain. Salt had dried white in the crack.

The man who had come to accuse him stepped over it.

"That is not what was taken," he said.

"It is what came back."

"Where is the cloth?"

"Ask the men who carried it."

The court attendant told them both to stand away from the timber. Neither moved until the ruler looked up.

I had been sent for because Hasan wanted the provisioning counts. I carried them under my arm and found a place behind the men who had brought the petition. The room was open toward the water. A breeze kept turning the corner of a hanging mat. Each time it lifted I could see the boats below, their crews moving between the shade and the glare.

The first petitioner was called Awan. He had supplied cloth and worked metal to merchants sailing toward Laoe. He wore a clean length of woven cloth across one shoulder. When he spoke of his goods, he named their owners and what each expected in return. When he spoke of Laoe, he called its people disobedient.

The second man was Dima. He had come on a boat that had stopped at two other landings before Burné. He said he spoke for his mother's household and for the men who had agreed to send him. When the attendant asked whether he spoke for all Laoe, he said no.

Awan smiled.

"Then he has no answer to give us."

Dima bent and picked up the timber.

"My mother's brother cut this from his own landing," he said. "Your men broke the post when they took the goods. I can answer for that."

Awan turned toward the ruler. "He has admitted the taking."

"I have admitted a broken post."

"Will you deny that Laoe refused what was owed to Burné?"

"I will ask what was owed, and to whom."

I watched Awan's smile leave his face. I had once thought a man who spoke calmly must be the stronger of two men. Dima's hands shook around the timber. Awan had not yet touched anything.

Hasan stood near the ruler with two pieces of wood. One bore the names of Awan's witnesses. The other had the names Dima had brought. They were not the same names. I could tell that from where I stood, though I could not read all the marks.

The ruler asked Awan who had carried the goods.

He named a boat owner who was waiting outside.

"Did you see them seized?"

"No. My receiver came back without them."

"Did he see them seized?"

"He says he did."

"Then let him speak."

Awan looked toward the door. His receiver had already been summoned, but he was at the landing, arguing with a man who wanted payment for keeping his boat in the shade. An attendant went to fetch him.

Dima set the timber down again.

"Before he comes," he said, "may I tell you why our people did not send the goods onward?"

"Tell us what you know," the ruler said.

Dima said a boat had arrived carrying a demand for dues that no one at his mother's landing had agreed to pay. Some households had paid a previous collector for the right to use the approach. Others had sent gifts to a ruler across the water. The collector who came with Awan's goods said the payment belonged here. Men at the landing refused to unload the bundles until someone could tell them which payment would be recognized.

"Who took the bundles?" Hasan asked.

"Men who said they were keeping them until the quarrel was settled."

"Your men?"

"Some were my kin."

"Where are the bundles now?"

"I do not know."

Awan made a sound through his nose. Dima looked at him without lowering his eyes.

"Your receiver says the goods are gone," Dima said. "I say the men who held them have not returned. Both can be true."

Awan stepped forward.

"The men of Laoe send their gifts to the king of Java Major. They have chosen their master. Why should our merchants risk their lives carrying goods to people who laugh at Burné's ruler?"

Dima's fingers tightened on the split wood.

"Which people?"

"The people of Laoe."

"My mother has never seen the king you name. She has seen the men who come asking for his gifts. She has seen your collectors too. Both tell her the other has no right to take from her."

The attendant made him lower his voice. Dima obeyed. Awan looked satisfied, as if the raised voice had proved his claim.

The receiver arrived smelling of sweat and wet hemp. He said that he had been pushed away from the landing when he demanded his goods. He had seen men carrying two bundles up the bank. He had not followed because the boatman would not leave his vessel unattended.

"Did you see Dima there?" Hasan asked.

"No."

"Did you see his mother's brother?"

"I do not know his mother's brother."

"Then whose men took your goods?"

"Men of Laoe."

"How did you know?"

"They were at Laoe."

I could hear Safi move behind me. He had been standing against a post where I had not noticed him. When the receiver finished, Safi asked whether the boatman had come to testify. He had not. He was still at the landing, with a vessel whose owner had hired him for another voyage.

The ruler ordered that he be heard before the claim of seizure was entered as settled fact.

Awan bowed, but I could see the effort in his neck.

"While we hear boatmen," he said, "other ships will turn away from our markets."

"Some already have," the ruler said.

He spoke without anger. That frightened me more than a shouted order would have.

Hasan called me forward. I thought he meant to ask about my journey east. Instead he took the provisioning counts from me and laid them beside Awan's list.

"These are the boats we have already promised food and water," he said. "If the court sends a party to inquire at Laoe, how many can be spared without taking supplies from their owners?"

I looked at the marks. They were the same counts I had made under Jamil's supervision. A man at the landing had changed his water load before departure. I knew because I had watched him carry one jar back ashore.

"These are not the boats we have now," I said.

Awan looked at me. "Are you refusing to count?"

"The second boat changed its load. The owner said he would decide after the wind."

"Then ask him."

"He has gone."

Awan turned to Hasan. "Why bring a count that cannot be used?"

Hasan did not answer for me.

"Because it tells us what was agreed yesterday," I said. "It does not tell us what can sail tomorrow."

The ruler asked who could tell him. Jamil, who had entered during the receiver's account, named three boat owners he could approach. He did not say they would agree.

Awan asked for enough vessels to make Laoe listen.

"Enough for what?" Jamil said.

"To recover what was taken."

"And if the goods have already been traded?"

"Then take their value."

"From whose house?"

Awan opened his mouth. He had been ready with an answer to the first question. The second held him still.

Dima stood beside his broken post. He had brought it to show what had been done to his landing. He had not said whether his mother's household would help return the cloth.

The ruler asked him directly.

"If men from Burné come to hear the matter, will your household receive them?"

"If they come without taking what they please."

"And if they come with men who can fight?"

Dima looked at the timber. "Then the households that have weapons will meet them with weapons. The rest will hide their children."

He said it as though he were describing rain. I looked toward the water, where a small boy was carrying a jar with both arms around it. He could not see into the audience house.

Awan said Dima was threatening the court. Dima answered that he had been asked what would happen.

The ruler told them both to wait outside. Dima picked up his timber. The attendant made him leave it on the floor.

When they were gone, the room seemed larger. I could hear the water moving against the piles below us. Someone outside had begun beating dust from a mat.

Jamil spoke first.

"A small party could go to ask for the goods and the men who took them. If it returns without an answer, we will know more."

Awan's older sponsor, who had remained inside, said a small party would be laughed away.

"Then a large party may be fought before it asks a question," Jamil said.

The sponsor wanted the ruler to call the settlement to obedience. Hasan wanted the boatman's account. Safi said that a party carrying only gifts and questions might not be allowed to leave if the claim of the other king had already been accepted by armed households.

I waited for someone to ask me which was right. No one did.

The ruler asked Jamil how he would protect the men sent to speak.

"By sending vessels whose owners agree to the work, by taking a guide who knows where they may land, and by keeping enough men together to withdraw if they are refused," Jamil said.

"Will you promise they can withdraw?"

"No."

Safi glanced at him. Jamil did not look away from the ruler.

I thought of the shorter passage I had allowed Hamzah to take. I had promised safety because the water had looked open. A man was still missing because of what followed.

The ruler asked Safi what he would do if the boats were attacked while trying to leave.

"Fight them clear," Safi said. "But first I would ask why we had gone so close that leaving required a fight."

Jamil almost smiled. The ruler did not.

He looked at me then.

"You have heard two claims. Which is true?"

I could have told him that Awan's receiver had seen bundles carried up the bank, and that Dima's kin had held goods until a collector's right was proved. Both men had spoken plainly when the questions suited them.

"I believe the goods were withheld," I said. "I cannot tell whether the men who held them thought they were taking or guarding them. I cannot tell which household will return them. And I do not know whether Dima can speak for the men who have weapons."

"What would you do with that ignorance?"

I looked at the timber on the floor.

"Ask Dima which landings would refuse us. Ask the boat owners which approach they will trust. And take enough food to come back even if no one sells us any."

The ruler turned to Jamil. "Does he know how to do that?"

"He knows how to ask," Jamil said. "He has not yet shown he can bring the answers together when men are frightened."

I felt the words as a rebuke. Then I remembered what he had asked me to do at the lower store. He had not told the ruler I was fit to command.

The ruler gave Jamil leave to seek vessels and men for a guarded embassy. Its first purpose was to demand an account of the detained goods and the release of any merchants or boatmen held against their will. It could protect itself and withdraw if threatened. It was not given permission to seize the houses of people who had not fought it, nor to take cargo as payment without another hearing.

He also ordered that the men who claimed rights at Laoe be told what had been demanded in his name. No one in the room could say exactly who had carried every earlier demand.

Awan's sponsor bowed. He did not look pleased.

I was relieved. I imagined the party going, speaking, and returning with the cloth. Then Jamil asked what would happen if the envoys were detained and the boats fired upon.

The ruler held his gaze.

"Then you will return if you can. If you cannot, those who remain here must decide what to send after you."

No one offered a guarantee. The audience ended.

Outside, Awan was speaking to a cluster of merchants. Dima sat beside the timber the attendant had returned to him. He had laid it across his knees and was rubbing the salt from the split with his thumb. I wanted to ask whether his mother's house stood near the landing where the cloth had been held. I stopped before I reached him. I had been told to bring counts when asked, nothing else.

Safi came down the steps behind me.

"Jamil wants the owners at the lower landing," he said. "He wants you to ask whether they will carry food for the embassy."

"Will I sail?"

"He has not said."

I looked toward the water. One of the owners had pulled his boat into shade to work on a seam. A man inside was pressing fiber into the opening with a blunt tool. A second man held the plank steady. They had stopped to listen to the argument above, but now they were working again.

"How many jars should I offer them?" I asked.

"Ask what they can take."

"And if they say no?"

"Find another owner."

Safi started down toward the landing. I followed him, leaving Dima with the broken timber and Awan with the men who wanted their goods back. From the steps I could see both of them. Neither had moved toward the other.

I had thought the ruler's words would settle what the party was for. By the time we reached the boats, I was already wondering which men would hear those words as permission to take more.

---

Ruma came to see the boats before she came to see Jamil.

She walked along the landing with Tahir beside her, looking into the hulls that had been drawn together for the voyage. A man asked whether she had come to carry the ruler's soldiers. She told him she had come to see whether anyone had put the water jars beneath the heavier goods.

He laughed. She did not.

When Jamil arrived, she named her price for carrying stores and a small party of men. She would not put fighters on her own vessel until they had told her where their weapons would lie. She would not leave her boat tied to another if the weather changed. She wanted a place to take her crew ashore if the embassy failed.

Jamil listened to each condition.

"Will you take the work?" he asked.

"If Tahir approves the load."

Tahir had been kneeling in the boat with his hand against a plank. He stood and said that two of the water jars must be moved forward and one basket left ashore. The owner of the basket protested. Tahir asked him to come aboard and stand where he expected the boat to sit when the tide fell. The man stepped in, felt the vessel shift, and climbed out again. He took the basket with him.

Ruma accepted the work.

I had imagined that the ruler's order would bring every boat to the landing. By the time we left, two owners had refused, one had offered a vessel without its crew, and another had agreed only to carry a messenger as far as the next settled shore. Jamil did not punish them. He had enough trouble with the owners who had said yes.

The guards carried spears and shields wrapped against the spray. They were fewer than Awan had wanted. Some belonged to households whose goods had been delayed; others were men Jamil trusted to hold a landing if the embassy needed a place to speak. None of them had been promised that Laoe would yield.

Awan watched from the upper steps. He had given the expedition food and expected the return of his cloth. He asked why Ruma's vessel was not at the front.

"Because she carries water," Jamil said.

"Water can follow."

"Only until someone needs it."

Awan looked at me, perhaps hoping I would offer him a faster arrangement. I was carrying a bundle of spare bindings to Ruma's boat. I passed him without speaking.

Dima had agreed to travel with us as far as the outer approach. He said he would tell us which landing his mother's household used and where strangers were likely to be challenged. He had refused to lead armed men directly to her door.

The court had accepted his condition. Awan had called it proof that he was hiding something.

Dima carried no timber now. He had left the broken post in the audience house. His hands were empty except for a small bag of food. I wondered whether he had brought enough to return if Ruma put us ashore.

At the last moment Nasir's sister appeared between two men carrying water. She had heard that I was leaving with Jamil. I set down my bundle.

"Have they found him?" she asked.

"No."

"Then why are you going?"

"To another shore. The ruler has sent men to ask about goods held there."

She looked at the weapons. "Those men are carrying questions?"

"They are meant to protect the ones who ask."

She watched a guard testing the edge of a spear against his thumb.

"Bring back the names of anyone who does not return," she said.

I had no answer that would make her look less tired.

"I will ask for them."

"Ask before you leave," she said. "It is harder afterward."

She went back up the landing. I stood holding the bindings until Tahir called for them.

We traveled by stages. At each stop the boat owners checked what they had used and what they still carried. I had been assigned to help keep Ruma's stores with the other supply vessel, whose owner was a broad-shouldered man called Banu. He had accepted Jamil's guards aboard but would not let them sit on his covered jars.

Safi traveled with the men who would go ashore to speak. Jamil moved between the vessels when conditions allowed. I was not told where to steer. I was told to make sure the stores did not become separated from the people who needed them.

The first time Banu moved ahead of Ruma, I called for him to wait. He did not hear me. I was about to wave a pole across the water when Tahir caught my arm.

"That is the signal for a boat to come alongside," he said.

"What should I do?"

"Tell Ruma what you see."

Ruma looked toward Banu's boat and said the space between us was safe while the water remained open. She would ask him to close it before we entered the next sheltered passage. I watched him moving farther away, but I held my pole down.

The next day the wind changed while we were under a low coast. Banu brought his boat close without being called. He said he had seen a squall building over the water behind us. Ruma looked at the same sky and asked Tahir to shorten the loose covering. We passed into shelter before the rain arrived.

I had thought Banu was the one who needed watching. He had been watching the weather.

Dima spoke little during the voyage. When men asked how many houses stood at Laoe, he told them he had not counted every house. When they asked how many fighting men it could raise, he said he knew only who had weapons near his mother's landing. A guard asked whether the people of Laoe feared the ruler of Burné. Dima asked which people he meant.

By the time we reached the last sheltered water before the disputed shore, the guards had stopped asking him questions.

I found him at the stern, looking at a line of trees above a narrow stretch of water.

"Is that where your mother lives?" I asked.

"Beyond it."

"Can we pass?"

"Not by the way Awan's men have described."

"Why?"

He pointed to the water. I saw only a dark line running across the surface. He made me wait until a small patch of foam moved toward it and broke apart.

"There is a hard place beneath," he said. "The water crosses it when the tide is high. The men who know it take their own boats through. They do not bring strangers with loaded vessels and armed men."

"Is there another way?"

"Yes."

"Will you show it?"

"I will show you where I stop."

He looked at the guards. One had taken off his covering and was drying it in the sun.

"I told your ruler what I would do," Dima said. "If you ask me to lead them to my mother's door, I will go ashore alone."

I wanted to tell him we had come to hear the quarrel. The guards had their weapons laid out where they could reach them. He could see that without my help.

Jamil came to the stern with Safi. They had been speaking to the owners about how far the vessels could go before the water turned.

Safi favored waiting in the outer shelter while a smaller boat carried the first message. He said the loaded boats should remain where they could leave without passing close to the hard ground. Jamil agreed that the message should go first, but he wanted the guards near enough to protect the messengers if they were pursued.

I watched the foam break over the hidden place again.

"If the guards go by that short water," I said, "the supply boats cannot follow them when the tide falls."

Safi turned toward me. "I did not ask them to go by it."

"No. Awan's men did."

"Then tell Awan's men to ask Dima."

Dima did not look pleased to have his name brought into the argument.

Jamil asked Ruma whether her boat could take the longer water and remain near enough to hear a call from shore. Ruma said she could do one or the other while the tide was falling, not both. Tahir agreed. Banu said he would not anchor on ground he had never seen.

The men argued over where the embassy could wait. The guard leader wanted the smaller boats close enough to rush a landing. Safi wanted room for them to turn away. Ruma wanted her water jars out of reach of anyone who might mistake them for a prize.

Jamil listened until everyone had said what he would refuse.

"Ruma and Banu will stay outside the narrow water," he said. "The speaking party will go in with two light boats. They will not land until Dima has shown them a place he is willing to approach."

Dima shook his head.

"I will show the outer marker," he said. "I will not choose the place where you put armed men ashore."

Jamil looked at him for a long moment.

"Then show us the marker."

I was given charge of carrying messages between the supply boats while they held position. Ruma would decide where her own vessel stayed. Banu would answer for his. If one had to move, I was to make sure the other knew before the distance became too great for a call.

It was a small authority. It was also the first time since Hamzah's boat had vanished that Jamil had asked me to carry a decision between two owners who might disagree.

Dima pointed out a low place on the shore where the water had worn a pale line across the rocks. He said his mother's household used it as a mark when coming from the outer sea. Beyond it the shore curved out of sight.

The smaller boats went forward. Safi rode in the first with two men who had been told to keep their weapons covered unless they needed them. Jamil stayed in the second. Dima sat beside the bowman, watching the water rather than the shore.

I could see people moving above the landing when the boats came near. A man stood with a long pole. Two others were carrying baskets away from the water. A woman drew a child behind a house. No one raised a greeting that I could understand from where I stood.

Ruma brought her boat to a place where the current ran steadily past the hull. Tahir tested the depth with his pole and told her they had room to swing. Banu stayed farther out. I moved between them in a small boat with one of Ruma's younger crew, a man who knew how to keep his vessel steady while I spoke.

"Tell Banu we will hold here," Ruma said.

We crossed the short stretch. Banu listened and said he would remain where he was until the tide changed. He would not follow if the guards went inland. I repeated his words to Ruma.

She nodded. "That is what he agreed."

From the shore came a shout. A man had walked down to the edge and was speaking to Dima. The words did not reach us. Dima stood in the bow of the first boat. He lifted both hands, empty. The man on shore pointed beyond the curve of land.

Safi's boat edged nearer. Jamil's followed at a distance. I saw the guard leader raise his arm, then lower it when Safi spoke to him.

The man on shore disappeared behind the houses.

"Are they letting us land?" I asked Ruma.

"We cannot hear."

I wanted to take our small boat closer. Ruma said no.

A second man came out carrying a bundle wrapped in cloth. He held it over his head. The first boat drifted toward him. Dima leaned forward. The man stopped at the water's edge and set the bundle down.

Then someone behind the houses shouted again. A cluster of people appeared on the raised path. One carried a spear. Another was pulling a boy by the arm. The boy was struggling to reach the bundle.

The man at the water turned and ran back up the path.

I could not tell whether he had been called away or driven off.

Safi stood. He called toward the shore. A stone struck the side of his boat. Another splashed beside it. One of his men reached for his spear, but Safi pushed the shaft down.

Jamil's boat began to turn. The guard leader shouted to follow him. The bowman tried to push away from the shallows. His pole caught beneath something and bent.

From our position I could see the bow shift sideways. The water was dropping. The first boat had come too close to the pale line of rocks.

"Can they get clear?" I asked Tahir.

"If they stop trying to turn against the current."

He spoke to Ruma, not to me. She called for the younger crewman to bring our small boat back alongside.

Banu had already seen the trouble. His boat moved closer, though he had said he would hold farther out. I thought he was coming to help. Ruma said he was moving to keep the guards in sight as the curve hid them. He had not agreed to enter the shallows.

I stood with one foot on the thwart, looking at Safi's boat. A third stone came from the shore. This one struck a guard on the shoulder. He dropped to his knees, and the man behind him caught his shield before it fell overboard.

Safi called to Jamil. Jamil shouted something back. I heard only the last word.

Tahir took the pole from my hands.

"Sit down," he said.

I sat. The smaller boat was coming back to us, and there was no room for my foot where it had been.

Ruma asked whether the guards had room to back away. Tahir watched the water around their sterns. He said one boat did. The other would need to be pushed off the hard ground while the water still covered it.

I could see Jamil looking toward us. He raised his arm and called for a boat to come, then pointed to the deeper water beside the stranded bow.

Ruma turned to me.

"Tell Banu we are sending the small boat to help the men push free," she said. "He must keep the outer passage open. Do not tell him to follow us."

I repeated the words to the younger crewman. He nodded, and we pushed away.

The water beneath us was clear enough to see dark shapes moving over the bottom. I watched the rocks come nearer. I could hear the people on shore shouting to one another. I could not tell whether they wanted us gone or wanted the boat held there until more men arrived.

When we reached Banu, he heard Ruma's message and gave us a coil of rope. He would not send his own boat inward.

"Take this," he said. "Bring it back if you can."

I held the coil between my knees as we turned toward the stranded boat. Safi had climbed into the water. It reached above his knees. He was trying to lift the bow while the men aboard pushed with their poles.

Dima stood in the boat with his hands on the gunwale. He was looking toward the houses, where someone had begun dragging the bundle away from the landing.

The guard who had been struck was still kneeling. Blood ran down his arm into the water.

I had come to this shore to carry messages. Now I had a rope in my lap, men trapped against stone, and a boat whose owner was waiting for me to return. I could see exactly where the rope needed to go. I could not yet see how to get it there without bringing another boat onto the same ground.

---

The rope was wet before I reached Safi.

I had thrown it too soon. The end fell short of his hand and sank beside the stranded boat. He reached for it and missed. The younger crewman pulled it back while I gathered the slack across my knees.

"Wait until he looks," he said.

Safi was pushing against the bow with both shoulders. The water had fallen enough to leave a strip of the bottom exposed. A man in the boat drove his pole into the mud and leaned until the shaft bowed. Nothing moved.

I called Safi's name. He turned. This time I threw the rope toward his open hand. He caught it and passed it around the forward timber. I made the other end fast where the younger crewman showed me.

We pulled. The small boat shifted under us. Its bow dipped toward the hard ground.

"Stop," the crewman said.

I stopped.

Safi called for us to move farther into deep water before pulling again. The younger man turned our boat with his pole. We paid out the line as we went, until the rope lay across the current and I could feel the stranded boat pulling against it.

The first pull did nothing. On the second, a man aboard the stranded boat shoved with his pole at the same moment. The bow lifted. Water came beneath it in a dark rush. Safi climbed aboard as the boat slid free.

Someone on shore shouted. A spear struck the water behind him. I saw its shaft standing for an instant before it fell sideways and vanished.

Jamil ordered the boats out.

Dima was still in the bow, holding the gunwale. He had not moved when the stones came. Now he pointed toward the houses and called a name. A woman appeared on the raised path, carrying a small bundle. She stopped when she saw the guards, then went back out of sight.

"That is my aunt," he said when our boats drew near enough for me to hear him.

"Will she come?" Safi asked.

"Not while you are here."

The wounded guard had a swelling shoulder and a cut along his arm. His companions bound it with cloth. He was angry enough to insist he could still fight. Jamil told him to sit.

We reached the outer water before the tide left the shallows bare. Ruma took the rope back. Banu counted his crew and found them all. The embassy had brought away no cloth, no prisoner, and no answer from the men who held the landing. It had also left behind the bundle that someone had tried to put at the water's edge.

Dima asked to be set ashore at a place beyond the disputed landing. Jamil refused to leave him among men who had just thrown spears at the embassy. Dima said that bringing him back to Burné would make his mother's household look as though it had guided the attack.

"I did not guide you to the houses," he said.

"I know," Jamil answered.

"Will the men at Laoe know?"

Jamil had no answer. He promised only to take Dima to a shore where he could find passage without returning with the armed boats. Dima accepted that because he had no boat of his own.

I watched him leave us at the next landing. He took his bag of food and no payment. Before he went he told me that the woman I had seen on the path had two children. I asked whether he wanted me to carry a message to her.

"What would you say?"

"That we did not come to take her house."

He looked at the weapons behind me.

"Then do not make me a liar."

He stepped ashore.

We returned to Burné with the wounded man and the rope. The guard leader said the people of Laoe had attacked an embassy. Safi said that someone had thrown stones after armed boats came close to a landing whose people had been arguing over dues. Jamil said both things had happened. The ruler listened to all three.

Awan demanded that the goods be recovered by force. His receiver said the boatman who had seen the bundles carried away had still not been heard. Hasan found the boatman and brought him before the court. He said he had seen the bundles taken up the bank but had not seen them opened. He could not name the men.

The ruler asked whether the men who had thrown spears were those who had held the goods.

No one could say.

For several days messengers went between the court and the households willing to speak for Laoe. One came back with a demand that Burné's collectors stop approaching the settlement. Another said a party loyal to a ruler across the water had seized the goods and would not return them. A third brought no words at all. He had been turned away before he reached the landing.

Then one of the smaller boats that had carried the first embassy's guards failed to return from a later attempt to find the missing boatman. Its owner came to the court with the names of his crew. A man who had escaped said the boat had been taken and its people held at the settlement. He had seen two of them led away. He could not say what had happened to the rest.

Jamil asked to lead a larger force.

I was in the lower store when he told me. I had been sorting the jars that could be moved without breaking their rims. I put one down too quickly and felt the shock travel through my fingers.

"Will you take me?"

"That depends on what you can do."

"I brought the rope to Safi."

"The younger crewman brought the boat. Ruma gave you the rope. Safi told you where to throw it."

"I know."

"Then say what you did."

"I waited until Safi could catch it. I held the line when we pulled."

Jamil nodded.

"That is what I can ask you to do again."

He gave me the vessels that would carry water and the men who would take wounded people away from the landing. I was to keep them close enough to be useful and far enough from the fight that a single rush from shore would not take all our supplies. Ruma agreed to carry stores again, but she refused to put her crew in the first assault. Jamil accepted her refusal. Banu agreed to carry guards, and made them promise to keep their blades away from his cargo.

Safi would take the first fighting party ashore. I wanted his place. I knew he could see that.

"Do you think you could lead them better?" he asked.

"I think I could help."

"Then help where Jamil has put you."

He went to inspect the guards.

The ruler's new order was spoken before the owners and men who would sail. The force could compel the release of the detained boatmen, break armed resistance, and take the goods and securities needed to answer the claim. The ruler forbade harm to people who laid down their weapons. He demanded that goods taken afterward be brought for judgment.

Awan said the order was generous. I thought of the spear thrown at Safi and did not think it generous enough.

We sailed when the owners were ready, not when I wanted. More boats had joined us. I did not count them as though the number would make the result certain. I knew which carried water, which carried food, which had men able to fight, and which had agreed to take the injured. I knew also that two owners would turn back if the weather made the return passage unsafe.

At the disputed shore, we found the outer water watched. Small boats moved between the landing and a sheltered place beyond the curve. Men stood with weapons above the houses. There were people moving baskets away from the water. I could see smoke rising from cooking fires. Nothing looked abandoned.

Safi took his men toward the landing that had driven us away. A second party approached another place where the shore could be reached without crossing the hard ground. Its boatmen had agreed to the approach after watching the water for a full tide. I stayed with the stores, carrying Jamil's orders between Ruma and Banu.

The first fighting began before I could see who struck.

A shout went up from the shore. One of our boats came backward out of the shallows with a man lying across its middle. The men aboard were pushing with their poles. Another boat turned to protect it. Spears flashed above the water. A guard fell between two vessels and disappeared for a moment beneath their sides.

Ruma brought us closer to take the wounded. She did not wait for my permission. Tahir called for the boatmen to leave room at the side. We hauled the fallen guard aboard. He was breathing, but his leg had been cut. He kept trying to sit up and look toward the shore.

"Lie down," Ruma said.

"I can still hold a spear."

"You cannot stand in my boat."

He lay down.

I heard Jamil's call from the fighting boats. He wanted the second party to move along the bank and prevent armed men from carrying the detained boatmen inland. The path between the two landings was narrow and partly hidden by houses. I could not see where Safi's men had reached it.

Banu asked whether he should carry his guards closer. I had been told to keep the supply vessels out of the fight. I also knew that if the second party failed, the men we had come to release might vanish beyond the houses.

"Wait for Jamil's word," I said.

Banu looked at the shore. "His word may come too late."

I was ready to tell him to go. I remembered Hamzah's boat entering the shorter passage because I had allowed it. I asked Banu how close he could bring his vessel without grounding it.

He pointed to a stretch of deeper water and said he could hold there long enough to put men ashore, but not if the tide fell further. I sent that answer to Jamil with the younger crewman. I did not send an order.

Jamil called back for Banu to take the guards in. Banu moved at once. He had already chosen the approach. His men knew where they would step.

From our position I saw them land behind the first defenders. Some of the men on shore turned toward them. Safi's party pushed up from the water. The men who had held the path began to give way.

A house near the landing caught fire.

I did not see who set it. The flame ran along a dry roof edge and rose through a gap. A woman came out carrying a basket, then went back in without it. A boy followed her. The men fighting on the path moved around them as though the two were part of the ground.

I called for water. Tahir looked at our jars.

"That is for the wounded," he said.

"The house is burning."

"We cannot carry enough from here to stop it."

I looked toward the smoke. A second roof had begun to burn where the wind carried sparks.

Jamil sent for me. His messenger said that Safi had reached the path and needed the stores moved nearer to the shore. Ruma refused to take her boat into the shallows while people were still fighting. She offered to put the jars into smaller craft and let the guards carry them.

"That will take longer," I said.

"It will keep my crew alive," she answered.

I took the offer to Jamil. He accepted. I worked beside the men unloading jars, passing them down into the smaller boats while the smoke grew thicker.

When I reached the landing, the fighting had moved beyond the first houses. Men lay on the path, some in Burné's cloth and some without any mark I recognized. A guard was dragging a wounded companion by the shoulders. Another had a sack of goods under his arm. He was laughing.

"Whose is that?" I asked.

"Taken from the house of a man who fought us."

"Did you see him fight?"

He stopped laughing. "His door was barred."

He moved past me.

I had no order to stop him. I could have called Safi. Safi was farther along the path with men who were still being struck at from behind the houses. I watched the guard disappear toward the boats.

Near the burning roof, the woman I had seen earlier was trying to pull a child from beneath a fallen beam. The boy who had followed her was beating at the flames with a wet mat. The mat caught fire at its edge. He dropped it and cried out.

I set down the jar I was carrying and went to them.

The beam was too heavy for me to lift alone. I called to two guards. One said he had been ordered to carry water to Safi. The other came. We raised one end while the woman dragged the child clear. The child's leg was twisted beneath him. He screamed when she touched it.

The guard who had helped me looked toward the path.

"We have to go," he said.

"Help me carry him to the boats."

"Safi needs water."

I looked at the jar I had left on the ground. We could carry the child or the water first. The guard would not do both.

"Take the water," I said. "I will carry him."

The woman would not let me lift the child until she saw where I meant to go. I pointed to Ruma's boats beyond the landing. She shook her head. She had seen the men carrying sacks from the houses.

"They will take him," she said.

"They are taking the wounded."

"Your wounded."

I had no answer. I took off the cloth from my shoulder and laid it beneath the child so that she could lift him without my hands. She did. I helped her carry him down the path, stopping whenever he cried too hard to breathe.

At the water, Ruma saw us. She told Tahir to make room. The woman refused to board until Ruma said she could stay beside the child. Ruma said she could, but that the boat would leave if the fighting came down to the landing. The woman climbed aboard.

I went back for the jar. It was gone. A guard had carried it toward Safi. I did not know whether he had heard my order or had simply needed water.

By the time the armed resistance broke, the first landing was full of men carrying goods. Some brought the detained boatmen. One was limping. Another had a bandage around his head. A third name was still missing. The men who had held them had fled or been taken; I could not tell which without asking.

Awan's receiver came ashore with the second party. He was pointing at houses, claiming that the cloth had been kept in one of them. Guards entered the house he chose. They came out with bundles, jars, and a chest whose lid had been broken. The receiver said the chest was not his.

The guards carried it anyway.

Jamil ordered the men to bring what they had taken to the landing for judgment. Some obeyed. Others had already hidden things in their boats. He sent men to search the boats, but those men were also tired, frightened, and eager for reward. I saw one return with a bracelet around his wrist that had not been there when he went ashore.

I wanted to believe the ruler's order could reach every house. I had heard it spoken. I had helped carry the food that brought these men here. I had watched them enter the settlement.

Safi found me near the water. His sleeve was torn and his face streaked with smoke. He asked whether the wounded had been taken aboard.

"Those I saw."

"And the others?"

"I do not know."

He looked toward the burning houses. "Then find out."

I started up the path. Behind me men were calling the settlement ours. I could see the roof where the woman had carried out her child. It had fallen in. The people who lived beside it were standing in the water with their baskets, waiting for someone to tell them where they could go.

We had won the landing. I had not yet found the names of everyone we had lost.
