The keeper woke us by putting her shoulder against the door.

It opened halfway and struck Isan's sleeping mat. He rolled away before the wood caught his hand.

"Tools," she said.

"Which ones?"

"Mine."

She stepped over his legs, took a coil of cord from the peg and pulled a short wooden mallet from beneath the bench. She had no reason to explain herself. We had paid for a floor, not for the right to keep other people out of their work.

Outside, the landing woman was arguing with a boatman over a broken basket. He said the basket had been sound when he left it. She held it up by the rim and showed him the place where the weave had split. He wanted her to pay for the fish that had fallen into the mud.

"Bring me the fish," she said.

"The dogs ate it."

"Then ask the dogs what they will pay."

He did not laugh. Neither did she.

I had promised myself that I would ask the keeper for work before I asked anyone to hear my claim. The landing woman saw me waiting and pointed toward a pile of poles beside the steps.

"Those need carrying above the tide."

"I can do that."

"Then do it before the water takes them."

Isan helped me. We lifted each pole between us and set it on the dry bank, where the keeper was laying out the lengths she needed. The work left red mud on our hands. One pole had been buried so deeply that we had to loosen it with another before it would come free.

When we finished, the keeper gave us water. I asked what she would pay.

"That was for the breakfast you ate yesterday," she said.

"I carried water for that."

"You carried one jar. You ate twice."

Isan looked at me and waited. I could have argued about the number of bowls. Instead I asked whether she needed anything else.

"After the people come," she said. "You may be busy with your words."

I had not told her about the man from the far bank. News had reached her without needing my help.

Amaya came before the sun stood high. She carried the folded cloth again, but this time there were two women behind her. One was older than I remembered any of the girls who had worked beneath our hall. The other had a small boy holding her skirt. They stopped where the path entered the shelter and waited for Amaya to speak.

"This is the man I told you about," she said.

The older woman looked at me from my wet feet to the mud on my arms.

"He has been carrying poles," she said.

"I have."

"Good. The old floor never mended itself."

I wanted to ask whether she knew my mother. I wanted to ask whether she had seen the day I left. Instead I asked her name and listened to her say it. I knew the name of her father. He had once carried bundles for the household. I had not known he had a daughter old enough to speak before strangers about our hall.

The younger woman held her boy close when I turned toward him.

"He has never lived anywhere else," she said.

"At the house?"

"At the house. His father repaired the roof over the cooking place. The roof was not given to us. We made it."

"I have seen it."

"You looked at it as though someone had put it there to insult you."

I had.

Amaya unfolded the cloth on the bench. The red piece of wood lay between us, with its worn hole and the paint left deep in the grain.

"I brought this so you would know which house we mean," she said.

The older woman touched the wood with one finger.

"Your mother made us take the post down. It leaned toward the path. A child could have been killed."

"Was she there?"

"She watched us cut it."

I had been away. For years I had remembered that post standing upright, as if the house had continued to hold my place because I could still see it in my mind. These women remembered the day it had become dangerous.

"She told you to mend the hall," I said.

"She told us to keep it open," Amaya answered. "That is what I will say. If you want me to say she gave you the floor again, I cannot."

"I have not asked you to."

"You asked me yesterday whether she said it was no longer yours."

"I wanted to know."

"Then you know what I heard."

A boat struck the lower steps. The men aboard held it against the current while a passenger climbed out. He was a broad man with a narrow strip of dark cloth tied around one wrist. He carried a small bag and no visible weapon. Banu followed him, holding a pole that was too long for the boat he had come in.

The passenger stopped when he saw the people gathered under the shelter.

"Which one is Ache?"

I answered before anyone pointed.

He looked at the landing woman. "I was told there would be a hearing."

"There will be people speaking," she said. "You may hear them if you keep your boat out of the unloading place."

He glanced at Banu.

"You said a hearing."

"I said they wanted one," Banu replied. "I told you I would carry their question."

The man took a moment to decide whether to be angry. Then he ordered his paddlers to move their boat downstream.

"I speak for a house that keeps boats across the river," he told me. "The men who work for us have been told you are returning to take the old landing."

"Who told them?"

"Men who heard you had returned."

"That is not what I said."

"What you said may matter less than what men expect you to do."

He was right, and I resented him for saying it before I could.

The landing woman brought out two mats. She gave one to Amaya and the women from the house. She put the other beneath the shelter's open side and told the visitor to sit there. He did. I remained standing until the keeper pushed a stool toward me with her foot.

"Sit," she said. "You cannot listen well while you are deciding whether to stand above everyone."

The man from the far bank watched me lower myself onto the stool.

Amaya began with the roof. She named the people who had repaired it and the work they had done. The older woman described the day the red post came down. The younger one told us how many families used the cooking place and where they slept when the water rose.

I knew some of the names. Others belonged to children born after I had left. They had lived in the place I had been calling mine.

"Did my mother say who should receive the work of the house?" I asked.

Amaya looked at me.

"She said the people working there must be fed from the work when there was enough. When there was not, she said the stores should be opened before the children went hungry."

"Did she say who could speak for the hall?"

"She spoke for it while she was there."

"And after she left?"

"She told us to keep doing the work."

The man from the far bank leaned forward.

"Then you have no answer about who owns it."

"I have an answer about who kept it standing," Amaya said.

He looked at the red wood.

"Does a piece of post prove what a woman said?"

"No," she answered. "That is why I brought the people who heard her."

The older woman nodded. The younger one held her boy's hand beneath the edge of the mat.

I felt the old desire to make the argument simple. My father had held the house. My mother had held it after him. I had been born beneath its roof. The men across the river had taken land while I was away. I could have said all of it. The words were ready.

Instead I asked the younger woman how many families still worked there.

"Five under the roof," she said. "Others come when there is fish to dry or wood to carry."

"Would they lose their work if I came back?"

"That depends what you mean by coming back."

I had no answer she could trust.

"I will not drive them out," I said.

The man across from me shook his head.

"Then what have you returned to take?"

I looked toward the steps. A loaded boat had arrived. Its crew waited because the shelter was full of people and nobody had cleared the place where they usually counted the jars. A boy called for the keeper. She rose at once, leaving us to finish without her.

"Not the floor beneath their feet," I said.

"That is an easy promise."

"It is the first one I can make."

"What is the second?"

"That I will hear who paid for the repairs, and that I will not call those payments gifts to me."

Amaya studied me. She had heard me promise many things when I was younger. She knew how often I had meant them until someone asked me to give something up.

The visitor folded his hands.

"My house has boats that use the lower crossing. If you begin collecting a fee because you say this shore is yours, those boats will stop coming. The people here will lose trade. We will find another place."

"I have not asked for a fee."

"You will need one if you mean to rule."

I thought of the carved box I had given away for three nights. I thought of Harun inspecting the cloth he had brought at his own expense. A claim that could not feed its workers would soon become a demand that someone else feed them.

"Then I must learn what this landing costs before I ask what it owes," I said.

The man did not seem impressed.

"Anyone can say that while he has no men."

"I have no men."

"That at least I believe."

The younger woman laughed once and stopped when the boy looked up at her. I felt my face grow hot. There was a time when I would have answered a remark like that with a threat borrowed from my father's name.

Banu stood near the edge of the shelter. He had said nothing since bringing the visitor. When I looked toward him, he shook his head slightly. He would not rescue me from a question I had invited.

I turned back to the women.

"Will you let me come to the hall and hear the work that needs doing?"

The older woman looked at Amaya. Amaya did not answer for her.

"You may come when we are there," the older woman said. "You may listen. You may help if you know how. You may not tell the children to move because you want to sit where your father sat."

"I agree."

The younger woman spoke next.

"And you will ask before bringing armed men."

"I have none to bring."

"Then promise for the day you do."

I looked at the visitor from the far bank. He was listening carefully now.

"I will not bring armed men into the working hall without warning the people who live there and hearing their objections," I said.

"That is a longer promise," he said.

"It will be harder to keep."

The landing woman returned from the jars. She heard the last words and told us the shelter would be needed for the afternoon catch. If we meant to keep talking, we would have to make room for the work.

Amaya wrapped the red wood again. She did not give it to me.

"We can meet at the hall tomorrow," she said. "There are things you have not seen."

"Will the man from across the river come?"

He stood.

"I came to learn whether you had soldiers and whether you meant to take the landing. I have heard answers. I will tell my house what I heard. I have not agreed to anything."

"Will you give me your name?"

He told me. I repeated it to be sure I had heard correctly. He did not offer his family's other names.

As he went down the steps, he stopped beside the boat waiting to unload. Its owner had been standing in the sun with his jars.

"You should have moved that mat sooner," the owner said to me.

"I should."

"Will you remember next time?"

I looked at the people clearing the shelter, at the keeper carrying away the stools, at Amaya waiting for the women to gather their things.

"Yes," I said.

He did not thank me. He called for his crew to bring the jars ashore.

I helped carry the first one. Its weight pressed against the bruise on my forearm where the poles had slipped that morning. When I set it down, I could still see the dark cloth tied around the visitor's wrist as his boat moved toward the far bank.

I had been allowed to hear the people who kept the hall. Tomorrow they would let me see their work. Neither permission belonged to me by birth. I would have to return with my hands free.

* * *

The roof was better than the one I remembered.

Amaya stood beneath the new beams and watched me look up. Rain had darkened the old timber near the eaves. The newer pieces were pale and straight. A cord ran from one post to another, holding strips of fish where the wind could reach them without letting the dogs take them.

"Your mother said the roof should be mended," Amaya told me. "She did not say which trees would give us the wood."

A woman at the far end was beating dust from a mat. Two men carried a basket between them, one walking backward to clear the cooking fire. Nobody stopped when I came in.

"Who paid for the beams?" I asked.

"People who needed the roof. The timber came from a man who had no place to dry his fish. He gave us two lengths and took space under the eaves for a season."

"Does he still use it?"

"His daughter does. She will tell you herself if you ask."

I had imagined a hall where every object might prove that something had been stolen from me. Amaya was showing me things that had been made after I left. The work had owners even when the floor did not.

She took me beneath the raised room. A man was shaving a paddle with a curved blade. His daughter held the wood steady. He did not rise.

"Can you repair a boat?" Amaya asked me.

"I can help."

"That is what I asked."

"I can mend a seam. I know how to set a steering line. I have helped move a hull out of mud."

The man looked up.

"Can you pay for a seam you tear while learning?"

"I would have to."

"Then you can help after I have finished the part that matters."

Amaya smiled. She did not tell him who I was.

By the time we reached the landing, the woman who had given us the tool room was standing beside two owners whose boats lay on the mud. One owned a broad ferry with a raised platform. The other was a woman with two narrow boats that carried jars and dried fish between the upper landing and the bay. The broad ferry's owner had brought a measuring pole. The woman had brought her younger brother, who knew the depth of the channel after every rain.

Banu stood apart from them. He had not promised to bring anyone. He had come because he needed to take a load across the river before noon.

"I asked them to hear what you want," the landing woman said. "I did not ask them to give it to you."

"Thank you."

"Save that until you know what they say."

I told them I wanted the landing kept open while the households decided what place I might have in Maynila. I said I would help repair the lower steps and clear the mud where the boats turned.

The ferry owner lifted his measuring pole.

"Whose men?"

"Those who agree to work."

"Whose food?"

"I can pay for some."

"With what?"

I had kept the beads and cloth from my gifts. I could sell them, but once they were gone I would have little left. I had been counting on my name to open a way to the next day. The owner waited while I thought.

"With what I earn," I said. "And what people choose to contribute."

He made a sound through his nose.

"Then you have a day's work and a day's food. You do not have a landing."

The woman with the narrow boats crouched beside the water. She put her hand into the mud and brought up a broken stake. Its point had rotted.

"This is why I lose time here," she said. "My men cannot see where the bank falls away. Last week one of my boats grounded and we had to lift half the jars out before it would float."

"Would you pay to replace the stakes?"

"I would pay for one. The people who use the landing would have to pay for the rest. I will not mend the river for a man who says he has returned to own it."

"I have not said that."

"You may say it after I have paid."

She broke the stake across her knee. The wood fell apart where it had been under water.

The ferry owner pointed his pole toward the far bank.

"Your mother's people once asked us to keep the channel open when the water fell. We did. Then other men came and said we had no right to carry passengers without paying them. If you take back the landing, will you demand the same?"

"I do not know who demanded it."

"That is why I am asking you."

The landing woman stepped between us to let a porter pass with a basket. She waited until he was clear.

"Tell him what you will do," she said to me. "Do not tell him what you hope he will believe."

I asked the ferry owner what it cost to bring his boat across. He told me how many men he fed, what he paid for replacement paddles, and how much time he lost when the landing was crowded. I listened. I had crossed the river as a passenger and called the fare reasonable or unfair depending on the coin in my hand. I had never owned a boat that had to come back whether anyone paid or not.

"I will not charge you for landing while the steps are being repaired," I said. "After that, no new fee without the owners who use them being heard."

"And if the men across the river refuse?"

"Then there is no new fee."

The ferry owner laughed.

"You have made yourself a poor ruler."

"I have not been made a ruler."

"That may be why you can still say it."

He drove the measuring pole into the mud, took it out, and showed us the mark. He would lend it for one morning if someone promised to bring it back. He would not lend his boat.

The woman with the narrow boats offered a different bargain. She would pay for two new channel stakes if the keeper and the landing workers chose where to place them. She would carry a load of timber on one boat when it was not needed for trade. She would not give Ache the use of the second boat, and she would not take passengers into a quarrel with the far bank.

"Who will make the stakes?" I asked.

"The man you saw shaving the paddle," Amaya said. "If he agrees."

"Who will put them in?"

The woman's brother looked at the water.

"Someone who knows where the bottom is."

Sulad came up the path carrying a net over his shoulder. The net was wet and heavy. A boy followed with the floats. Sulad had been working since before sunrise. He stopped when he saw us.

"You have found people to give you boats," he said.

"They have not given me any."

"Then you are learning."

The woman pointed to the broken stake.

"Can you set a new one without putting it in the wrong water?"

Sulad looked at the mud, then at the bank beyond it.

"Not without taking a boat out at low tide and feeling the bottom."

"Would you do it?"

"Who pays?"

She named a price for a half day's work. Sulad looked at the boy with the net.

"I have this to finish first."

"We can wait until tomorrow," she said.

He agreed to sound the channel for one morning. He would bring his own pole. He would be paid whether the owners decided to use his measurements or not.

I wanted to tell him I was glad he had joined me. He had joined a job. The difference was plain enough that I kept quiet.

Isan had been listening from beside the shelter. He came forward when the woman with the narrow boats asked whether the people from Burné would be bringing more goods.

"Harun will sell what he has," he said. "He has not promised another voyage."

"Can you speak for him?"

"I can tell you what he told me. I cannot sell his goods or make his promises."

The woman nodded. She asked whether Isan would help her brother speak with a trader whose words he understood poorly. Isan asked what she would pay. They agreed on a basket of rice and a small piece of cloth for two days' work, provided the trader came before Harun left.

I looked at him.

"Will you still come with me to the hall?"

"When I am not working for her," he said. "If you need me at the same time, ask me first."

He had come from Burné to help me find my shore. Now he had found work on it. I could not object without proving that I had brought him here as a servant.

The ferry owner lifted his pole.

"I will take passengers tomorrow," he said. "I will not take your quarrel. If the far-bank house sends men with weapons, I will turn my boat away."

"Then tell them I have asked for a hearing," I said.

"They already know. Their man came yesterday."

"Did he tell you what he thought?"

"He told me you had no soldiers."

"He was right."

"He also said you listened while a woman spoke of the roof. That was harder for him to believe."

The owner left with his pole. I had not won his boat. I had won nothing from him except the promise that he would keep doing his own work, which he had meant to do anyway.

Amaya watched the narrow-boat owner walk toward the carpenter.

"She will have the stakes made," Amaya said. "But if you want men to help put them in, you must ask the people who use the steps."

"I will."

"And if they say no?"

"Then I will carry the stakes with those who say yes."

"Do not carry them into deep water to prove you are willing. Ask Sulad where they belong."

I looked toward the nets. He was already walking away with the boy, the heavy mesh slung between them.

In the afternoon I went to Harun's anchorage. He had sold two bundles of cloth and was repairing the wrapping on a third. A trader from the upper river was inspecting his resin. The buyer did not look up when I climbed aboard.

"How long will you stay?" I asked Harun.

"Long enough to finish this trade."

"I have found work on the landing."

"Good."

"Some of the owners will repair the channel."

"Better. It will make their boats easier to bring in."

"I may need to send word upriver."

Harun looked at me then.

"With whose boat?"

"That is what I came to ask."

He shook his head before I finished.

"My boat goes where I can sell my cargo. I will not carry messages into a dispute over your mother's house."

"I could pay."

"With what?"

I had been asked the same question twice that day. I did not answer quickly enough.

"When you have something to pay with," he said, "we can speak about a voyage. Until then, ask the people who work that river."

I stood beside the wet cloth, feeling the deck move under my feet. It had carried me from Burné. I had been grateful for it until I began to need it for something its owner had never promised.

"Will you leave soon?"

"When the wind is right and the goods are sold."

"Then I should thank you while you are here."

"You paid for your passage," he said. "Keep the rest for when you have a passage of your own to arrange."

He went back to his buyer.

At dusk I returned to the tool room. The keeper had set the mallet back beneath the bench. Isan was sitting on his mat, drying the cloth he had earned. I told him about the two stakes, Sulad's half-day work, the boat that would carry timber and the ferry that would not carry me.

"How many boats do you have?" he asked.

"None."

"How many will work with you?"

"One for a load of timber, when its owner has no other trade."

"That is not the same thing."

"I know."

He folded his cloth carefully and put it away.

"Then tomorrow you should ask what the timber is for."

I lay down where the roof did not leak. The landing was still someone else's. The hall was still full of people who had kept it standing. But two stakes might soon mark a safer channel, and I had begun to learn whose hands could put them there.

* * *

The first stake broke before we could drive it in.

The carpenter had shaped it from sound wood. Sulad had marked the bottom with his pole and told us to wait for the water to fall. I was impatient. I went into the shallows with two men who had come to help and asked them to hold the stake while I struck it.

It leaned on the third blow. On the fourth, the head split.

The carpenter took the mallet from me. He did not say I had struck badly. He cut away the ruined top and measured what remained against his own arm.

"It may serve for the bank," he said. "It will not reach the channel."

The woman who owned the narrow boats had paid for two stakes. She looked at the broken one, then at me.

"You can put your own wood in its place."

"I will."

"Before my boat comes back with jars."

"Yes."

"You do not know when that will be."

She went to find her brother. I stood in the mud with a piece of wood too short for its purpose.

Sulad had warned me that the bottom changed when the tide went out. He had also warned me that a stake struck from the wrong side would split along the grain. I had listened to the first warning and forgotten the second because I wanted the men watching to see me do the work.

The carpenter made another stake after I bought him a length of timber. It cost most of the cloth I had intended to keep for a gift to the household. He let me shave the bark from the offcuts. He did not let me drive the replacement.

We put both markers in at the next low water. The narrow-boat owner's brother stood in the bow of her working boat and called the depths. Sulad worked his pole from the stern, correcting him twice where the mud had piled up beside an old post. The owner stood on the landing with her arms folded. When the first boat passed between the markers without touching, she nodded to her brother.

She did not nod to me.

The repaired landing was only a beginning. A jar broke against the lower step three days later. The keeper said the step had always been too high for a boat at that water. The owner of the jar said it had been safe the week before. They were both right. The bank had fallen away beneath the lowest plank.

I asked the people at the hall to help lift a new beam into place. Four came. Amaya sent two more after they had finished drying fish, and warned me that they would have to leave before dark to prepare the cooking place. I said we could finish without them. We could not. We wedged the beam above the water and left it until morning.

By then the first of our three nights in the tool room had become a memory. I had moved into a corner beneath the hall, where Amaya permitted me to sleep if I helped mend the roof and did not take space from the families who lived there. I had been afraid that accepting such a place would make me seem less than my father's son. The children had been afraid I would wake them by moving in the dark. They were right about that.

I learned where the floor creaked and stopped stepping there.

At the end of the second week, Isan came back from the trader's shelter with a new pair of sandals and a complaint. The merchant whose speech he had translated had paid him less than agreed because the sale had gone badly.

"Will you speak to the landing woman?" I asked.

"I already did."

"What did she say?"

"That she heard the bargain, and that the merchant must pay me before he takes his last bundle away."

"Then he will pay."

"He says he will leave by another landing."

"Will he?"

"Perhaps. I asked the boat owners to hear it too. They do not want him carrying his goods past the keeper without paying her."

Isan sat on the edge of the platform and inspected the thong of one sandal.

"You could have asked me," I said.

"To do what?"

"To speak for you."

"You would have told them I came with you from Burné. I wanted them to hear what I had done here."

I had no answer. By the next afternoon the merchant had paid Isan in full. Isan used part of it to buy food for the workers who had helped him. He gave none to me, and I was glad he had not felt obliged.

Harun left in the third week.

He came to the hall before sunrise, carrying a folded cloth he had not been able to sell. He said he could leave it with a trader who would pay later, but he did not trust the trader's brother to keep the promise. He would take it home.

"Do you want passage?" he asked me.

The question was generous. I could have returned to Burné and said I had tried.

"No."

"Then do not use my name when you ask for a boat."

"I will not."

He looked around the hall. Amaya was dividing a basket of fish between two households. A boy had brought a roof tile from the upper path and was showing it to the carpenter as if it were a shell he had found on the beach.

"You have work," Harun said.

"Not the work I came for."

"Perhaps you will know when it is."

He did not leave me a ship. He left me the length of cord he had used to bind his unsold cloth. I tried to refuse it. He said the cord had worn too much to trust around cargo. He had no use for it on the return passage.

I used it to tie a loose roof panel. It held.

The fourth week brought rain hard enough to run through the hall's new roof in two places. The carpenter's daughter climbed into the rafters and found where the thatch had lifted. Amaya ordered everyone below to move the dried fish before the water reached it. I climbed after the girl. She told me to stop touching the bindings until she had shown me which one was loose.

I obeyed. The water came through my hair and down my neck. From below, the children laughed at the sound I made when a cold stream found the back of my shirt.

Afterward the carpenter's daughter asked whether I could carry the ruined thatch to the fire. She paid me with a portion of cooked rice that was already mine to eat. I accepted it and made her laugh again.

By the sixth week we had repaired the lower step and the roof, and the narrow-boat owner had carried her promised load of timber. She had chosen the day herself, between two trips for paying customers. The landing workers had put a line of stones beside the steps where men could stand while a boat rose with the water. A porter with a bad knee could now unload a jar without jumping from the gunwale.

There was no ceremony. The first boat came in, the porters used the stones, and the keeper told a child to clear the empty baskets from their path.

The work had made me known. That was a different thing from being wanted.

A family living behind the old hall had used a narrow path to carry their catch to the upper market. When the new timber was stored beside the path, they had to go around the cooking place. Their eldest daughter asked Amaya to move it. Amaya asked me.

"The wood is for the steps," I said.

"Then put it by the steps."

"It will be wet there."

"Cover it."

"With what?"

"With the cloth you have been keeping dry beneath our roof."

I had meant to trade that cloth for food for the workers. I said so. The daughter listened and then told me how much fish her family had lost because their baskets had to pass through the dogs near the cooking place.

I moved the timber.

The next morning I proposed that each boat using the repaired steps contribute a little toward replacing the broken planks. I had seen a share collected at other landings. The keeper said the people who used this one must agree to the share before anyone took it. I asked the owners to meet.

Some came. The ferry owner sent his younger brother, with instructions to listen and promise nothing. The narrow-boat owner came herself. A man who had supplied rope said he would accept a share of the catch in place of money. The family from the path sent their eldest daughter.

She asked who would keep the collection.

"The keeper," I said.

The keeper looked up from her cord.

"Did you ask me?"

I had not.

We began again.

The keeper agreed to hold the collection if two workers counted it with her and if she could refuse a contribution that would leave a crew without food. The narrow-boat owner accepted the arrangement for boats carrying goods. The ferry owner's brother said he had no authority to agree. The rope supplier agreed to contribute work rather than coin.

The family from the path refused.

"We do not use the steps for trade," the daughter said. "We carry fish from the bank above them. You have made our way longer and now you want us to pay."

"Your boats use the channel," I said.

"Our boats used it before you came."

I wanted to tell her that the markers had made it safer. They had. I had also made her family's path worse for a week and then asked them for a share.

"We will not collect from your boats," I said.

The narrow-boat owner frowned. "Then others will ask the same."

"They may."

"How will you mend the next plank?"

"I will find another way."

The daughter rose. She said her family would no longer send workers to the hall until they knew whether the next improvement would be made at their expense. Two men who had helped carry the beam left with her. They had been among the strongest workers we had.

Amaya did not ask them to stay.

When I complained that the refusal would delay the steps, she told me to visit their house and look at the path they used when the water rose. I went. The bank was steep. A woman carrying a child could not descend it without another person to hold the basket. The daughter's father showed me a scar on his shin from a fall the year before.

"I do not want your new steps," he said. "I want the old way kept open."

I could not promise both immediately. I promised to return when we had moved the remaining timber.

By the ninth week, the repaired channel brought boats to the lower steps in water that had once kept them waiting. The keeper had begun recording contributions in cuts on a strip of wood, with two workers present when she took them. I did not hold the strip. The carpenter had made a low rail beside the landing, paid for by people who had nearly lost goods there. Amaya had let me help replace a post beneath the hall, but she kept the red fragment wrapped in her own cloth.

A boat from the far bank came late one afternoon and stopped beyond the new markers. Its crew did not enter. The man who had attended our first hearing stood in the bow. He looked at the stones, the rail, and the roof of the hall.

"You have made it easier to land," he said.

"Yes."

"Then more boats will come."

"I hope so."

"Whose passage will they use?"

"The channel."

"That is a piece of water. I asked whose passage."

He pointed with his chin toward the narrow reach beyond the markers, where the far-bank house's boats sometimes waited for a crossing. We had cleared one way in and made the other more crowded.

"We have not closed it," I said.

"Not yet."

He told me that the house across the river had ordered its boats to hold their place at the next low water. It would not let the new landing take the only easy turn into the channel.

"Will your house send someone to speak?" I asked.

"I am speaking."

"Will you come ashore?"

"Not while your workers are moving the bank."

He looked at the place where the two stakes stood. Sulad had marked them to guide a loaded boat clear of the mud. The far-bank man saw them as a claim.

The narrow-boat owner came down the steps behind me. She heard the last of the exchange.

"My boats have lost time there for years," she said. "I paid to mark the bottom. I did not pay to make a war."

"Then tell him to take the stakes out," the man answered.

She did not.

He returned to his boat. The paddlers turned it across the water and went back the way they had come.

At dusk, Sulad came from the nets and stood beside the markers. He watched the wake from the departing boat settle against the stones.

"Can they block us?" I asked.

"With two boats in the wrong place, yes."

"Can we move them?"

"With whose boats?"

I thought of the ferry owner who had refused me, the family that had stopped sending workers, the narrow-boat owner whose cargo still had to earn its passage. I could name every boat that might help and every reason its owner might decline.

"We will have to ask," I said.

Sulad put his sounding pole against the rail. He was tired from his own work. He did not offer to go with me.

Across the river, a lamp appeared above the bank. A second light moved toward it. I watched until they stood still, and understood that the next people to decide whether this landing could remain open would not all be standing on our shore.

* * *

At low water the far-bank boats lay across the turn like two doors shut against each other.

Neither carried a sail. One was long enough to take a dozen men, though I counted only five aboard. The smaller one was tied to it by a short rope. Their sterns rested near the mud bank. Between their bows and our channel markers was a strip of water too narrow for the jar boats to pass without touching.

The narrow-boat owner arrived with her brother before the keeper opened the shelter. She took one look at the boats and ordered her crew to unload the jars they had intended to carry upriver.

"You cannot leave them here," I said.

"I can leave them on my own platform. I will not put them in that gap."

"Will you let me speak from your boat?"

"No."

"I could take a small boat from the landing."

"Ask its owner."

I turned to the workers who had come down to see what was happening. Some were angry because the repaired steps were empty. Others had already begun moving baskets to the upper path. Nobody offered a boat.

The far-bank representative stood on the larger hull. He raised a hand to show that he saw us. He did not call across.

I asked the keeper for the measuring pole. She pointed to the ferry owner's younger brother, who had borrowed it the evening before. He was standing beneath the shelter with his arms crossed.

"The pole is mine to return," he said. "My brother said nothing about lending it for a quarrel."

"Then return it when you are ready."

I went to find Sulad.

He was at the nets with his sleeves rolled above the elbows, working a torn line through his fingers. He had already caught enough fish for the morning and was trying to save the mesh before it dried crooked.

"They are there," I said.

"I saw them."

"Can a boat pass?"

"Not one carrying jars."

"What about a boat with only three men?"

He put down the net and looked at me.

"To do what?"

"Speak to them."

"You can call from the steps."

"They will make me call like a child asking permission to cross."

"They are making you ask permission to cross."

I could have left him to his work. Instead I asked whether he would sound the channel with me if I paid for the morning. He asked whose boat I had found. I said none yet.

"Then find one," he said, and returned to the net.

I found a fisherman with a shallow canoe. He agreed to carry me and one other person to the edge of the turn, provided we brought no weapons and paid him before leaving the steps. He said he would turn back if the far-bank men lifted their poles against us. I paid him from the little I had earned carrying goods at the landing.

Sulad came after finishing his net. He had not agreed to join my quarrel. He said he wanted to see whether the far-bank boats were resting on the mud or holding themselves with their poles.

The fisherman took us out.

The river was quiet enough that I could hear a woman beating cloth against a stone upstream. A kingfisher flew low along the bank. The far-bank men watched us approach without moving.

The representative called out before we were close enough to touch.

"Have you come to take our boats?"

"I have come to ask you to move them."

"Why?"

"They keep the jar boats from the landing."

"They keep the jar boats from taking the whole turn."

"The turn was used before either of us put markers there."

"Then remove your markers."

I looked at the stakes. They stood in the shallow water where Sulad had placed them. If we removed them, the narrow-boat owner's crew would again have to guess where the bottom fell away.

"We can mark another passage," I said.

"Where?"

I had no answer that Sulad would trust. The fisherman looked back toward the steps, ready to turn.

"Come ashore and we will measure it together," I said.

"We measured it before you came."

"Then show me where."

The representative stepped aside. A younger man came forward and pointed his pole at a line of dark water beside the mud bank.

"That is our way," he said.

Sulad leaned over the canoe and lowered his own pole. The bottom came up hard beneath it.

"Not for a loaded boat," he said.

The younger man laughed. "Then lighten the boat."

"And carry its goods across whose bank?" Sulad asked.

The representative's face tightened. He ordered the younger man back.

"We will hold this place until the people who use it agree," he said.

"So will we," I answered.

The fisherman began turning us around. I wanted to stay, to make the far-bank men hear the cost of every idle jar boat. Instead I watched our bow swing toward the landing.

Back on shore, the narrow-boat owner was loading jars onto her second boat. She meant to take them to the upper market by the longer way.

"That will cost you half a day," I said.

"It may cost me a day. My customers will still receive their jars."

"Could we take one boat through if we moved the smaller hull aside?"

"With whose men?"

"Those who agree."

She looked toward the turn.

"I will let my brother take my empty working boat as far as the markers. You may ask the far-bank men to shift their smaller boat. If they refuse, he comes back. He will not ram them."

I agreed. The brother asked Sulad to sound ahead. Sulad accepted a day's wage from the owner. I helped the crew move the empty boat from its mooring. Three landing workers volunteered to push from the bank where the water was shallow. I promised them payment for the morning's work whether we succeeded or not.

We went out with the tide still falling. The brother stood in the stern and called each stroke. Sulad sounded from the bow. I sat near the middle with a rope coiled at my feet, watching the far-bank boats grow larger.

The representative saw us and raised his pole.

"Stop there."

The brother stopped.

"We need room to turn," I called.

"You have room to go back."

"Shift the smaller boat and we will pass without touching yours."

"No."

I stood. The brother told me to sit down. I sat.

We waited while the water fell another hand's breadth. The empty boat began to settle near the mud. The brother swore and ordered the crew to push us back into deeper water. One of the far-bank men laughed.

I heard it and felt the old heat rise in my throat.

"There is water behind their smaller boat," I told Sulad.

"There is a shallow shelf."

"Could we pull its stern clear?"

"Not without touching their rope."

"They have tied it across the passage."

"Yes."

I asked the brother whether he would let me wade across the shelf and carry a line around the stake on the near bank. We could use the line to hold our own boat while the crew pushed its bow toward the deeper side. He agreed to that, and only that.

I went over the side. The mud closed around my feet. I felt for the bottom with a pole, then took the line from the crew. The current pulled it hard across my hands. I found the stake and passed the rope around it.

The brother called for the men to push.

Our bow came round. For a moment the passage opened. Then the line tightened against the far-bank boat's short mooring rope. I had not seen where their rope lay beneath the water. Our line caught it and pulled it upward.

The smaller boat swung sideways. A man aboard shouted. Its bow struck the larger hull with a hollow crack.

"Release it!" Sulad shouted.

I tried. The rope had twisted under the pull. I could not free it from the stake. The brother ordered the crew to stop, but the current kept turning the hull.

The smaller far-bank boat lurched toward the mud bank. Its stern struck a narrow fishing platform hidden behind reeds. I heard the first stake break, then another.

A woman screamed from the bank.

I dropped the line and waded toward the sound. A rack of drying fish had gone into the water. A child stood on the bank clutching an empty basket. Two men were trying to keep the platform from falling farther while the boat ground against it.

The woman who had shouted was knee-deep in the mud, reaching for a bundle of nets trapped beneath the broken boards.

"Leave them," I called. "The boards will fall."

"They are mine!"

The child began crying. I went to the rack and caught a piece of it before it slid under the boat. A board struck my shoulder. I let go. The woman dragged the net free and stumbled toward the bank.

Sulad came behind me with a pole. He braced the broken platform while the men pulled the child away. The far-bank crew hauled their smaller boat off the wreckage. The brother had already taken the narrow boat back beyond the markers.

The water carried the fish away in small silver flashes. The woman stood beside her child with the wet net over one arm.

"You came to open a channel," she said.

"Yes."

"You have opened my floor."

I wanted to tell her I had not meant to touch her rack. I wanted to point to the far-bank boats and say their refusal had made the danger. Both things were true. Neither would put her fish back on the wood.

"Who owns this platform?" I asked.

"My mother built the first one. I replaced the boards last year. My son sleeps here when the catch is drying."

"Was he on it?"

"He had gone for water."

I looked at the child. His basket was still empty. He had been a few steps from the rack when it broke.

The representative came down from his boat. He looked at the wreckage and then at me.

"Now you see why we kept the turn."

"Your boat struck it," I said.

"Your rope pulled my boat."

The woman raised her voice.

"Both of you can stop speaking over my fish."

She pointed to the broken rack.

"Who will pay for that? Who will dry the next catch? Who will give my boy a floor tonight?"

I had promised the narrow-boat owner that we would not ram anyone. We had not. I had found another way to move a boat without permission, and it had broken a home beside the water.

"I will pay for the boards," I said.

"With what?"

I could hear the familiar question and knew I had no answer ready. The cloth I had kept was gone. The work I earned bought food. I could offer my hands. She needed boards before the next catch.

The representative spoke.

"Our house will bring timber if the boats are left where they stand."

The woman turned on him.

"Your timber does not buy my silence."

He stepped back.

She faced me again.

"You will both come here tomorrow with people who can count what was lost. I will tell them. My son will tell them. You will listen."

"I will."

"And you will leave the broken rack where it is until they see it."

I nodded.

The narrow-boat owner had brought her boat back to the steps. She stood there watching. When I came ashore, she did not ask whether we had opened the turn.

"You took my boat into their rope," she said.

"I did."

"I told my brother to come back if they refused."

"He stopped. I asked for the line."

"Then you have made a quarrel with my boat in it."

"I will tell them it was my decision."

"You can tell them anything. They saw my hull."

She ordered her crew to pull the boat above the tide. It would carry no more timber or jars for my work until the owner had inspected the bottom and heard what the far-bank house intended to do.

The ferry owner watched from the upper path. He had not lent his boat, and now he had a reason to be glad.

Amaya met me beneath the hall. She had heard about the rack before I arrived. News moved along the bank faster than a man wading through mud.

"Will you bring the woman here?" I asked.

"She has a place of her own. You can go there."

"Tomorrow."

"Before tomorrow, find out what she needs tonight."

I went back with a dry mat and a portion of food from my supper. The woman accepted the food for her child. She refused the mat.

"It belongs to the people who gave it to you," she said. "Bring boards."

The broken platform stood above the mud, one side hanging loose. The child was asleep in a neighbor's house. I watched the water rise around the posts I had helped pull apart.

For nine weeks I had been trying to make a way into Maynila. By evening I had made a family afraid to sleep beside its own river.

* * *

The woman made us wait until she had finished sorting the fish that could still be eaten.

She spread them on a clean mat borrowed from a neighbor. Some were bruised and covered with mud. Others had been held in a basket above the water and were sound. Her son separated them by size, putting the smallest in a pile near his knee. Each time I reached to help, she told me to leave them alone.

"You do not know which ones were drying for sale," she said. "Those are the ones you must count."

I sat where she pointed. The far-bank representative sat on a broken board from the rack. The narrow-boat owner had come with her brother. She kept the damaged boat at the upper steps and had brought no crew. Amaya stood with two workers from the hall. The keeper had come because she had seen the morning's rope carried from her landing.

Nobody had called this a court. The woman had invited people who could say what they saw.

She gave us her name. Dala. Her son was called Pawi. She told us the rack had belonged to her mother and that its boards had been replaced twice. The old posts were still standing under the mud, but one had split below the waterline. The new boards were scattered along the bank. The nets had caught beneath them and two had torn when she pulled them free.

Pawi lifted his hand.

"The third net was under the boat," he said.

His mother nodded.

"Tell them."

"The men pulled it out. I saw the fish go through the holes."

The far-bank representative looked toward his boat.

"Our men can mend nets."

Dala held up the torn mesh.

"Can they mend fish that have swum away?"

He lowered his eyes.

I offered the carpenter's help to replace the platform. Dala said she had already asked him. He could make the new boards, but the timber would have to come from somewhere else. She named a neighbor who would lend a sleeping mat until the floor was safe. The neighbor had asked that it be returned before the next market day.

"That is three days," Amaya said.

"It is."

The keeper set a strip of wood on the mat and made marks as Dala named the losses. Dala watched every cut. She made the keeper cross one out when Pawi remembered that a basket had been carried indoors before the rack fell.

I was ashamed of the relief I felt. One basket less to replace.

The narrow-boat owner spoke for the first time.

"My boat was taken against my instructions. My brother stopped when the other men refused to move. Ache took a line from it and pulled against their rope."

"I did," I said.

"I want that said in front of everyone who uses my landing."

"It will be."

"I also want my brother paid for the day. He lost the trip he could have made."

Her brother looked uncomfortable. He had been paid by her for the morning, and he did not like being discussed as a loss.

"You can pay him yourself," he told me.

"She already did," I said. "I owe her."

"That is between you and her," he said.

Dala looked from one of us to the other.

"Are you finished speaking about your boat?"

The owner folded her arms and let Dala continue.

The far-bank representative offered timber from his house's stores. Dala asked whether it would be given or lent. He said given, if the channel markers were removed and the landing workers stopped using the narrow turn.

"Then it is not given," she said.

"It is a condition."

"It is a price for a thing I do not own."

He looked at me.

"Will you move the stakes?"

I had wanted those stakes to stand. I had watched men work for weeks to put them in, and I knew how many boats could now reach the landing without grounding. If I removed them, I would be telling the narrow-boat owner that her expense and the carpenter's work had been wasted.

"I will leave them until the people who use both sides have measured another way," I said.

"Then our boats remain."

"For now."

The representative shrugged.

"You cannot open your landing while we hold the turn."

He was right. That morning I had tried to change that fact by force, though I had called it something else.

I turned to Dala.

"I can work for the boards," I said. "The carpenter will let me help him. I can carry them and set the new posts."

"You will not set the posts," she said. "You broke one yesterday."

I could not argue.

"I can pay him for the work."

"How long will that take?"

"Longer than three days."

"Then I will not wait for you to earn it."

She looked at the far-bank representative.

"Your house's boat struck my rack. His rope pulled it. I will take boards from your stores today, without a promise about the channel. If you refuse, I will tell every crew that dries fish on this bank why you refused."

The representative opened his mouth, then closed it. He had come expecting to bargain with me. Dala was speaking to the people whose fish and labor made his boats worth owning.

"I can give boards without a condition," he said.

"And the net?"

"Our men can mend it."

"Pay for new cord. I will choose who mends it."

He nodded.

She turned to me.

"You will pay the carpenter for shaping the boards. You will pay the woman whose mat my boy is using if we keep it longer than three days. You will help carry the timber but will not strike a post unless the carpenter asks."

"I agree."

"And you will tell the people at the landing that you pulled the rope."

"I will."

The narrow-boat owner nodded once. She had heard the answer she needed, though it did not repay her lost trip.

Amaya asked how the timber would be carried. The far-bank representative said his house would bring it to the near bank. The narrow-boat owner said she would not lend her boat for that purpose. The ferry owner's brother offered to ask whether his family would carry it as an ordinary paid load. Nobody promised to give a boat away.

Dala stood and gathered the fish into two baskets. She gave the sound ones to Pawi to carry inside. The rest she set aside for the cooking fire.

"You may go now," she told us. "Come back when you have the boards."

We had not settled the channel. We had only begun to mend what we had broken.

The carpenter worked for two days. I carried his offcuts and held a length of wood while he cut the notches. His daughter did most of the fitting. Dala stood beside her and showed where the old rack had been level. Pawi kept asking whether the new boards would float away. His mother told him to test each one with his own weight before sleeping there.

I had expected to feel better as the rack rose again. Instead I remembered how quickly the first one had gone down.

On the third day the narrow-boat owner came to inspect the lower steps. Her brother had sounded the channel again. The far-bank boats still held the turn, but they had moved enough to let a canoe pass.

"I will carry jars by the upper way," she said. "I will not lend a boat to clear that turn again."

"I will not ask you to."

"Good. Then we can speak about the landing without speaking about your quarrel."

She asked whether the keeper was still holding the repair contributions. I said yes. She said she would resume paying for the stakes when there was a passage she could actually use. Until then she would contribute only to the steps.

"That is fair," I said.

"It is a price. You do not need to bless it."

I smiled despite myself. She did not.

That afternoon Banu brought word that the far-bank representative would meet us at the shelter if Dala and the landing workers attended. He wanted to mark two passages, one for loaded boats entering our landing and another for the far-bank boats crossing. He would not remove his boats before the lines were agreed.

I went to Dala. She was tying the repaired nets to the new rack.

"Will you come?" I asked.

"Why?"

"To speak about the turn."

"I do not own it."

"You know what happens when people who claim it forget who works beside it."

She pulled the last knot tight.

"I will come if Pawi may speak."

"Yes."

"And if you will let the other people who dry fish there speak too."

"Yes."

She studied me.

"You said yes quickly."

"I have learned it takes longer to keep a promise."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh.

At the shelter the keeper spread a mat on the floor. The far-bank representative brought two boatmen and a cord marked with knots. Sulad came after his paid work at the nets. He had been asked to sound the water and had agreed for a fee paid jointly by both sets of owners. He had refused to be paid by me alone.

The ferry owner came himself. He sat near the entrance so he could leave if his boat was called.

The far-bank representative drew two lines in the damp sand. One entered the lower landing between our stakes. The other followed the outer bank and crossed above the shallow shelf. At high water both were passable. At low water the second became too narrow for a loaded boat.

Sulad corrected the drawing with the end of his pole.

"That shelf is longer than you have marked," he said.

The representative asked how he knew.

"I stood on it."

"You were in Ache's boat."

"I was in the mud."

The ferry owner laughed. The representative did not.

The narrow-boat owner proposed that her boats use the inner passage for unloading, while the far-bank boats retain an outer crossing at set times when the water was sufficient. The representative wanted a guarantee that no new fee would be taken from his house's boats.

The keeper said she would collect only the agreed repair share from boats using the steps. Boats passing through without landing would pay nothing. The far-bank representative asked who would enforce that promise.

Amaya spoke.

"The people who keep the landing open. If Ache tries to collect something else, we will tell the owners. If your men block the passage again, we will tell them that too."

I had not asked her to promise that. She had given both sides a way to hold me to my word.

The ferry owner asked who would decide when a boat had blocked the passage deliberately. Dala said the people whose work had been interrupted must be heard before either house sent men with poles.

The representative looked at her.

"You have had your rack rebuilt."

"Yes," she said. "I would like it to stay standing."

Pawi raised his hand. Everyone turned toward him.

"If a boat gets stuck again," he said, "will the men pull it out before it hits our boards?"

The ferry owner said he would send his crew if they were close enough and paid for their work. The narrow-boat owner said her brother could show where to push. The far-bank representative said his house would keep a pole on the outer bank.

No one promised there would be no accidents. Pawi lowered his hand.

We agreed to mark the passages the next morning and try them with empty boats before carrying cargo. The far-bank representative would move one of his boats out of the turn during the trial. If it failed, the boats could return to their positions while the owners argued again.

The agreement was small enough that each person could remember what had been promised. I was afraid it would fail because of something we had not imagined.

When the meeting ended, I walked with Amaya toward the hall.

"You could have kept the turn," she said.

"For a day."

"Perhaps longer, if you had men willing to push."

"I had them yesterday."

"Some of them will not come again."

She meant the workers from the family whose path I had blocked. They had not returned.

I stopped beside the repaired rail. Beyond it the river was dark. I could hear the carpenter's daughter speaking to Dala at the new rack.

"I thought that if I could take the landing," I said, "people would know I had come home."

"They know," Amaya said. "They are deciding what to do with you."

She went inside before I could ask whether she had decided.

* * *

The first empty boat went through without touching either stake.

It belonged to the woman who had paid for the markers. Her brother stood at the stern, one hand on the steering paddle and the other raised so the men on shore could see he was not carrying a pole to strike anyone. Sulad stood in the bow and called the depth. The far-bank representative watched from his own boat, which had been moved out of the turn as promised.

The second boat came from across the river.

It was wider. Its bow swung toward the mud where Dala's rack stood above the bank. Pawi watched from the platform with his mother. The boatman lifted his paddle and waited while the current turned him. He could have forced his way through. Instead he backed into deeper water, let the narrow boat clear the passage, and tried again.

The second time he passed.

The people on shore made no great sound. The keeper counted the boats and asked whether there would be any more before the market boats arrived. She had baskets waiting to unload.

The ferry owner came down the steps with his measuring pole. He tested the depth on the outer passage, then told the far-bank representative that a loaded ferry could not use it at the lowest water.

"We did not promise it could," the representative said.

"Then do not tell my passengers it is a crossing for every tide."

The representative nodded. The ferry owner went back to his boat.

I had imagined a day when men would shout my name as I came ashore. Now the sound I heard was a porter telling another to move a basket out of the path. It was a better sound than the one the breaking rack had made.

The narrow-boat owner brought a load of jars through the inner passage after the trial. She did not let me touch her steering line. She set her boat beside the steps, gave the keeper the agreed share for their repair, and asked for a dry place to stack the jars. The keeper pointed to the shelter.

When the far-bank representative's boat crossed above the turn, no one asked it for a fee.

At noon the keeper told us the shelter was needed for a meeting. She had invited the people who worked the hall, the owners who used the steps, Dala and Pawi, and the far-bank representative. Banu came with two older men from the houses farther upriver. One had known my mother. The other said he remembered my father but had not agreed with him often.

I had not called them. Banu said they had come because a decision about the old household would affect the boats that brought goods through their stretch of water.

Amaya stood near the entrance holding the wrapped piece of red post. She had brought it to the first hearing. Today she did not unwrap it.

The keeper asked me what I wanted them to decide.

I could have said that I wanted my father's place. I had said it in my head for years, on the water out of Burné, beside the fires of men who had never seen Maynila, and in the nights when I wondered whether anyone remembered my name.

"I want to speak for the landing and the hall," I said. "I want the people who use them to be able to bring disputes to me, and I want the work that keeps them open to continue."

The far-bank representative lifted his head.

"Speak for them against whom?"

"Against anyone who takes their work without hearing them. Including me."

A few people smiled. The representative did not.

"Can you keep a boat from blocking the turn?"

"Not by myself. The owners agreed on passages and witnesses. If one side breaks the agreement, we must bring the people who use both sides together before anyone takes a rope to another boat."

"You took a rope."

"Yes."

Dala sat with Pawi beside her. She did not look at me.

The older man who had known my mother asked whether I meant to move into the hall and take its stores. Amaya answered before I could.

"The hall's stores belong to the work and the households that filled them," she said. "Ache may live there only as we agree. He will not empty them to feed men he has brought from elsewhere."

I looked at her.

"Do you want me to speak?" she asked.

"I do."

"Then listen."

She named the people who had mended the roof, the carpenter who had made the posts, the women who kept the cooking place and the children who carried water. She named the people who had left when they thought my work would cost them their path. She did not say they would return.

"His mother told us to keep this house open," she said. "She did not tell us to keep a seat empty for him. He has helped us repair what was failing. He has also damaged things that were not his. I will agree to let him hear claims concerning the hall if the people who live and work there can refuse him when he is wrong."

The older man asked whether she would give me the red wood.

"No," she said.

I had thought I was prepared for that answer. I was not.

"Why keep it?" I asked.

"Because it is what remains of a post we took down to keep children safe. You remember it standing. We remember it falling. Both memories will be needed."

She held the cloth against her side.

The keeper asked who would collect the repair share. The owners answered that she would, with two workers counting beside her. The far-bank representative asked again whether his boats would be charged for passing without landing. The keeper said no. The narrow-boat owner said the two markers belonged to the people who used the channel, not to Ache. Sulad confirmed where the water was deep enough at the measured tide. He would help sound it again when paid to do so; he did not promise to be there every day.

Isan stood near the doorway. He had been translating a buyer's question for a merchant that morning. When the far-bank representative asked him to repeat a phrase he had used earlier, he did, then told the man that he was speaking as a witness to the words, not as Ache's servant.

The older man who remembered my father watched him.

"Who will carry your messages?" he asked me.

"Whoever agrees to carry them."

"And who will guard your house?"

"The people who live there must decide what protection they will accept. I will not bring armed men into it without warning them and hearing their objections."

"You said that when you had none."

"I have none now."

"Then you have made the same promise twice."

"Yes."

He looked at the far-bank representative.

"Would your house accept this man at the lower landing?"

"I was sent to learn whether he would take our crossing," the representative said. "He has not. We have marked two ways. My house accepts the passage arrangement for now. It does not recognize him as master of our boats."

"That was not asked of you," the keeper said.

The representative looked as though he wanted to argue. Then he said, "No. It was not."

The narrow-boat owner spoke next.

"I will use the steps while the keeper keeps the share as agreed. I will bring complaints about the landing to Ache if he will hear the people who own the boats. I will stop coming if he changes the terms alone."

The ferry owner said he would continue to cross as before. He had refused to lend his boat to the quarrel, and he wanted that refusal remembered as part of why his passengers had not been hurt. Nobody challenged him.

The keeper turned to Dala.

"Do you agree?"

Dala took Pawi's hand and stood.

"My rack stands again," she said. "The nets are not all mended. My boy slept in a neighbor's house for two nights because men who wanted a passage did not look where their rope went."

I wanted to say I had paid the carpenter. She knew that.

"I will bring a complaint to Ache," she said, "because he is the one who pulled the rope. I do not know yet whether I will trust his answer."

The keeper nodded as if this were a complete response.

Banu asked whether anyone who had worked at the hall wished to object. The daughter from the family whose path I had blocked came forward. She had not returned to work. She said the timber was gone from the path but the ground beneath it had been left rutted. She would not let her younger brothers carry fish there until the footing was repaired.

"I will help mend it," I said.

"I did not ask whether you would help. I asked who will see that it is done."

The keeper named two workers who knew the path. They agreed to inspect it the next morning. The daughter sat down without thanking me.

The meeting lasted until the shadows reached the jars under the shelter. By then everyone knew what the agreement did and did not give me.

The people of the working hall accepted me as the person who would hear their claims, carry their decisions to other households and answer publicly when my own acts harmed them. I could use the place where my father had once received visitors, but Amaya and the workers would keep the stores, rooms and repairs under their care. The landing owners accepted the keeper's collection and the two passages. The far-bank house retained its boats, crossing and right to contest any new levy. No one had surrendered a shore.

Banu asked the older men whether they would carry word to the other houses along the river. They agreed to tell what they had heard. They did not promise that every house would accept it.

"Then what do we call this?" the older man who knew my mother asked.

I thought he meant a title. I had been ready for titles since I was a boy.

Amaya answered.

"Maynila has a man to answer for it again."

The older man looked at me.

"Will you?"

"I will."

He held out his hand. I took it. His palm was dry and rough, and his grip hurt the place where the rope had burned my skin.

The keeper rose and told us to clear the shelter. There were jars to count and fish to bring in before dark. We moved the mats, folded the cloth and carried the stools back beneath the roof.

When the others had gone, Amaya led me to the old hall. The doorway had been widened since I was young. A new beam crossed above it, pale where the carpenter's blade had cut it. The cooking fire was burning inside. A woman was telling two children to wash their hands before touching the rice.

Amaya stopped at the threshold.

"You may sit where your father sat when people come to speak," she said. "Do not move the sleeping mats to make room."

"I will not."

"And you will come when the keeper sends for you, even if the complaint is against you."

"I said I would."

"You say many things."

She stepped inside. I followed.

There was no empty throne. The place where I remembered my father's seat held a low bench with a repaired leg. A basket of cord lay beneath it. I sat and waited while a porter brought in a question about a load that had been left at the wrong landing.

I asked him to begin again from the moment he took the cargo aboard.

He did. The woman who owned it came to hear him. They argued about the place where the boat had turned. I listened until I knew what each had seen, then asked the keeper to bring the strip on which the load had been marked.

Outside, boats moved through the passage we had opened. Across the river, the other house kept its lights burning.

I had come home to take back Maynila. The people who lived here had given me a place in its work and a duty to answer for what I did. They could take both away.

When the porter finished, I called for the woman to speak.
