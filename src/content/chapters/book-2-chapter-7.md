Ruma had taken the largest of the water jars off her boat when I arrived.

I had seen the jar on board the evening before. Its neck was bound in rattan, and the men had wedged it between two baskets of dry rice so it would not roll. Now it stood on the landing with a wet ring spreading beneath it. Ruma was kneeling beside the hull, examining a seam.

"Who moved it?" I asked.

"I did."

"Has it cracked?"

"No."

"Then why is it here?"

She stood and showed me a length of wet fiber. It had come away from the seam beneath the jar. She could have hidden it in her palm.

"Because I will not carry the jar over a hole," she said.

I crouched beside the hull. There was no opening I could see. The boards were dark from the night's water, and the seam looked much like the others. Ruma pressed a thumb against it. Water appeared in a thin bright line, then vanished.

"We can have another man bring binding," I said.

"We have binding. We need the boat dry."

The tide had begun to fall. We would lose the morning's departure if she hauled the vessel clear of the water, and the owners of two other boats had agreed to leave on the same tide. I looked toward them. Tahir's crew were taking the last covers off their baskets. A young man beside the third boat had already put his pole aboard.

"I could move your goods to Tahir," I said.

"You could ask him."

"I will."

"Ask him about his free space, not the space you have decided he has."

She went back to the seam. I walked to Tahir and asked what he could carry. He looked at the baskets on his deck, then at the line of men waiting with bundles.

"One light parcel," he said. "Or one more passenger who brings no baggage."

"Not the water?"

"Water is heavy when it stays in a jar. Heavier when it is in my boat."

He called to his crew to shift a basket and showed me how little room remained. I thanked him. He watched me return to Ruma, perhaps to see whether I would report his answer correctly.

The new order had made me responsible for keeping goods and messages together on the routes toward Luzon. The ruler had said that the owners must agree to each voyage. I had repeated those words to myself often enough to think I understood them. That morning I had three boats on the list and could not make the first one leave.

Jamil came down the landing with Hasan's clerk. The clerk carried two wrapped messages and a board with the names of the men who had promised to carry them. Jamil saw the jar on shore.

"How long?" he asked Ruma.

"Until the seam holds."

"Can it be made to hold today?"

"Yes, if we stop asking when it will hold."

Jamil nodded. He took me aside, where the men moving baskets could not hear.

"The messages can wait one tide," he said. "One of them cannot wait three days."

"Then we can send it with Tahir."

"If Tahir agrees, and if the man receiving it knows that it has changed hands."

"I have asked Tahir."

"Did you ask him to take the message?"

I had asked only about space. I went back. Tahir agreed to take the smaller wrapping if Hasan's clerk put the new carrier's name on the board. The clerk scratched out one mark and made another. I stood close enough to see that the first mark remained visible.

I was still watching him when a boy came running along the shore path.

He had salt drying in a white line above his knees and a cut on one heel. He stopped before Jamil, bent forward, and tried to speak while drawing breath.

"The tall one," he said. "It has not turned."

Jamil looked at him. "What tall one?"

"The ship beyond the market landing. They told me to tell you."

"Who told you?"

"A man with the boat that sells water."

"His name?"

The boy opened his mouth, then closed it.

"Did he pay you to carry this?"

"No. He said you would."

Jamil gave him water from a cup, not money. The boy drank, waited, and finally pointed along the harbor.

"The men there thought it would swing when the water changed," he said. "The other boats did. That one stayed where it was."

I had heard about the strangers' ships. Everyone at the landing had. Men who carried food to them said their sides rose higher than a house roof. Others said the sailors wore iron on their bodies and could make a noise like thunder. One man claimed he had watched a stranger drink from a vessel of clear stone. I had seen glass before. I did not know what the man had seen.

The strangers had been at Burné long enough for merchants to argue about their prices. I had not gone to look at them. After Laoe, I had spent my days answering questions about bundles, witnesses and names. At night I had gone through the lists for the next voyage. I told myself the strangers were a matter for the ruler and the harbor men.

Now Jamil was looking at me.

"Go with Safi," he said. "See what has happened. Do not take the message boats."

"I can leave the messages with Tahir."

"You can leave the messages with their carriers. They are not yours to leave."

He sent the clerk back to Hasan. The boy led us along the shore.

Safi caught up at the fish landing, carrying a coil of cord that he had been delivering to a boat owner. He listened to the boy's account without asking why the stranger's ship ought to turn.

"Which way did the other boats move?" he asked.

The boy pointed first one way and then the other.

"With the water," he said.

"All of them?"

"The small ones."

"Did the tall ship move at all?"

"I did not see it move."

Safi gave him a small coin from his own pouch. "Show us where you stood."

The boy smiled and ran ahead.

I asked Safi why he had paid him.

"He has shown us where he was," Safi said. "That may be worth more than what he thinks he saw."

The shore path was crowded. Two women were selling fish from shallow baskets. A man carried lengths of timber over one shoulder, calling for people to move before he struck them. Between houses I caught glimpses of the harbor: low hulls, poles, a woven sail drying on a frame, the broken reflection of the sun.

We reached a landing where the boards were wide enough for men to pass without stepping into the water. The boy stopped beside a post.

"Here," he said.

At first I saw only the ordinary traffic. A small boat was turning with the tide. Beyond it, a junk lay with a cover drawn over part of its deck. A man on its stern raised an arm to another boat, which altered its course and passed behind him.

Then I saw the foreign ship.

Its side stood above the water with a height that made the nearby boats appear unfinished. The bow was shaped differently from the boats I knew. A line ran from its front into the water, and above its deck rose tall spars crossed by others. The cloth hung gathered, so I could not tell how it would take the wind. Men moved along the rail, small against the hull.

I had expected it to resemble a great trading junk. It did not. That was the first thing I knew about it, and it was not enough.

"Why should it turn?" I asked the boy.

"Because the others did."

I watched the water against a nearby post. It ran past one side of the wood and curled back on the other. The foreign ship's bow held its place while the small craft changed their headings.

"It has a strong anchor," I said.

Safi leaned against the post, looking at the water rather than the ship.

"Perhaps. Or two. Or the wind holds it against one."

I felt irritated that he had found three answers before I could settle on one. I was about to ask whether he had ever seen such a ship when a man on the junk beyond us called for room. Its crew were preparing to move, and their stern was coming nearer the landing.

Safi stepped back. I took the boy by the shoulder and moved him clear. A pole struck the edge of the boards where he had been standing.

The boy looked at the mark in the wood.

"That is why I came to tell them," he said.

"Because the ship did not turn?"

"Because they were trying to move the junk around it. The men on the tall ship shouted. The junk men shouted back. No one moved."

The report had changed. A ship that would not turn was a curiosity. A junk unable to clear it might stop the passage of other vessels, and angry men could mistake an ordinary maneuver for a threat.

"Did anyone understand the shouting?" I asked.

The boy shook his head.

"Who sent you?" Safi asked again.

This time the boy pointed to the far side of the landing, where a water seller was pulling a rope through a handle. The man saw us and came over. He had not sent the boy to Jamil, he said. He had told him to fetch a harbor watchman. The boy had decided Jamil would pay more.

Jamil would need to hear that part.

The water seller said the strangers had bought provisions and allowed boats alongside on earlier days. Today they had shouted when a junk approached from behind. He had heard one interpreter speak to a merchant, but he could not tell us what was said. The interpreter was gone before the junk tried to move.

"Then no one has threatened anyone in words you understand," Safi said.

"I understand when a man points at my boat," the seller answered. "I moved it."

He showed us the space where he had tied his craft. The rope had left a pale mark on the post. A second mark, older and lower, showed where the water had stood.

I looked again at the foreign hull. It had not moved while we spoke. The men on its deck were still watching the junk. One of them lifted an arm. The others gathered near him.

I wanted to get closer. From another landing, I thought, I might see whether they were preparing a weapon or merely handling a line. I had already begun to choose which boat to borrow.

Safi saw me looking.

"Whose boat?" he asked.

"I have not asked yet."

"And whose messages are waiting for you?"

"Ruma is repairing a seam. Tahir can carry one."

"He agreed to one. Not the others."

The sight of the ship had made the three boats at our landing seem very far away. I had been given responsibility for them because I could keep account of what each carried and who had agreed to carry it. I was already treating that responsibility as something I could set aside to follow a more interesting question.

"We will send a runner for the harbor watch," I said. "I can ask the owner of the next landing to let us watch from there."

Safi nodded. He did not praise the decision. He was looking past the ship.

"What is it?" I asked.

He pointed farther along the line of houses, where the river opened toward the wider anchorage.

There was another high hull beyond the first. I had mistaken its dark side for a warehouse standing on piles. Now a strip of water showed between it and the shore, and above it I could see a second forest of spars.

I had thought we were trying to understand one stranger's boat. We had not even counted them correctly.

---

The watchman arrived with two men who disagreed about where we ought to stand.

One said we could see the strangers best from the outer landing. The other said that landing belonged to a household whose men had gone to carry rice, and we had no right to crowd it while they were away. Both were right about something. Neither would give way.

Safi asked who had authority to open the landing. The second man went to find the household's eldest daughter. She came with a basket of washed shells on her hip, listened to what we wanted, and told us to stand on the lower steps. We were to leave before her brothers returned with their load.

"You may look," she said. "Do not call my landing yours when you report what you saw."

I thanked her. She had already turned away.

From the lower steps the second foreign ship was plain. It lay farther out, its high stern rising above a row of low craft. The two great hulls did not resemble each other in every part. One had a darker side and a broken line of openings near the deck. The other showed more cloth bundled above. I could not judge which carried more men. The water between them was crossed by small boats bringing provisions.

A boat with two baskets of fruit came alongside the nearer ship. Someone above lowered a line. The boatman held up a basket, then pulled it back when the line came too close to his face. Men on the foreign deck laughed, or shouted, or both. The boatman made them lower the line again before he tied on the basket.

I watched the exchange until the empty basket came down.

"They trade," I said.

"They have been trading," Safi answered. "That tells us what they did this morning."

The watchman said the foreigners had first been welcomed with gifts. Later they had been permitted to take on water and wood. He had heard that some went to the ruler's house and were shown its wealth. Another man said the ruler had not allowed them ashore at all.

"Which man saw the visit?" I asked.

"Neither," the watchman said. "They heard it from people who carried the gifts."

I had once been ready to settle a dispute on the strength of two men who had heard the same story from a third. I remembered Hamzah's empty mooring, and the place where Nasir's sister had stood waiting for a name. I asked whether any of the gift carriers were nearby.

One was. He came down from a house above the landing with a cloth wrapped around his neck. He had taken baskets to the foreigners' ships but had not gone inside their great hulls. He said a few strangers had stepped into a local boat to visit the ruler. He had watched them leave. He had not watched them return.

"Did they threaten the harbor?" I asked.

"They bought chickens."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I have."

He said the strangers paid differently from the merchants who came through the port. Sometimes they gave metal or cloth. Sometimes they asked to exchange one thing for another and waited while an interpreter searched for the right words. Once a man held out a piece of glass to a seller who wanted iron. They had argued until the seller left.

The gift carrier showed us the direction of the market where the argument had taken place.

"Their words sound like a man talking with stones in his mouth," he said. "One of them can speak a little to the traders from the west. The rest point."

Safi asked whether the foreigners had ever tried to seize a boat.

"I saw them hold one by a rope," the man said. "The owner was still in it. He got it back when the water was brought."

"Was he afraid?"

"He was angry. I cannot tell you whether he was afraid."

He looked at me, as though waiting to see if I would write down a stronger answer than he had given.

I had no board with me. I asked him to repeat the part about the rope. He did, with fewer words.

At the market a woman was sorting salt from a broken jar. She had sold food to the strangers twice. She said they did not want to come ashore because they feared the men of Burné. Her nephew, who had carried a sack for one of their boats, said they were waiting for a fair wind and cared nothing for the people here. A third seller insisted that the strangers were measuring the harbor for an attack.

"Did you see them measure it?" Safi asked.

"No. Why else would they ask how deep the water is?"

"Because they have ships that sit deep," the woman said, without looking up from her salt.

The third seller left in disgust. He had not been asked to remain.

The woman scooped the dry salt into a new vessel and pushed the wet part aside. She said she had seen one stranger look toward the houses on the water with an expression she could not read. He might have been amazed. He might have been counting them. She had kept selling.

I thought of the woman at Laoe who had lost her fish store. A man from Burné might have looked at her house before setting it alight. If she had survived to tell the story, she would not have called him merely curious.

"Have you heard of Laoe?" I asked.

The salt seller stopped sorting.

"Everyone has."

"I was there."

"I know who you are."

Her voice gave me no place to stand. I wanted to tell her that I had helped carry a child out of the fire. I could have told her about the disputed goods and the names we had taken. None of it would change what she knew.

She turned back to the salt.

"Then you know a stranger can come to buy and come again with men," she said.

"Yes."

"And you know a man can tell himself he has been sent for a good reason."

"Yes."

"Ask them what reason they have. Then ask the people who must live here after they go."

I could not ask the foreigners anything from that landing. The man who spoke to the western traders had not come ashore that day, and the harbor watchman would not send a boat into their anchorage without the ruler's permission.

Safi suggested we find a merchant who had already dealt with them and ask whether he would carry a message. We found one preparing a load of beeswax. He had heard that the strangers wanted to leave soon. He would take a greeting if the harbor watch provided the boat and someone else paid for his lost time.

"What greeting?" he asked.

I began to say that we wished to know whether they intended harm.

"Would you answer that question from a man who came to your ship with no name?" Safi said.

The merchant waited.

I told him we would first ask whether their water and wood were sufficient, and whether a boat might approach without being taken for an enemy. It seemed a small question. It was also the only one whose answer we could test.

The merchant repeated it back in the trade speech he knew, then shook his head.

"I can ask for water," he said. "I cannot promise they will hear the rest."

"Do not promise," I said.

He looked at me curiously. "Then I will not."

The watchman refused to let him go until he had spoken to his superior. We waited beside the wax while a runner went to the harbor office.

Across the water, several junks lay at anchor beyond the foreign ships. Some had arrived the previous day. I recognized a kind of cargo cover used on vessels that traded toward Luzon, but I did not know their owners. Their sterns rose and fell with the tide. A small boat moved between them carrying men with bundles.

Farther out, more boats were gathering. They came in separate groups, with different cloths and marks, and did not all keep the same distance from one another. A man on the landing said they were going to fight the settlement across the harbor. Another said they were going to honor the ruler. A third said they had come to see the strangers.

"How many know where the boats are going?" I asked the watchman.

"Ask the men who sent them."

"Can you bring one?"

"Not while I am guarding this landing."

He pointed to a line of boats edging into the channel. The crews were busy keeping clear of the junks, which now lay between the foreign ships and the inner harbor.

A runner returned from the harbor office. The merchant could carry our greeting, but only after the next watch change. The superior had ordered the small boats to keep out of the passage until the larger traffic cleared.

We had gained permission to ask a question after the time when its answer might have mattered.

I went back toward Ruma's landing. Safi walked beside me without speaking. The men at the market were still arguing over what the strangers wanted. A girl carrying a basket of fish slipped between them, reached the water first, and sold her catch to a boat that was leaving.

When we came in sight of Ruma's boat, it was drawn above the wet line. The water jar still stood on shore. Ruma was under the hull with a length of fresh binding in her hands.

"Did you find what you wanted?" she called.

"Two ships," I said. "And many people who disagree about them."

"Then you found a harbor."

She pushed the binding through the seam and asked me to hold the loose end. I took it. Beyond the houses, the tops of the strangers' spars rose above the roofs. Between us and them, the harbor was filling with boats whose owners had their own business, and whose paths were beginning to cross.

---

The seam held when Ruma poured the first bowl of water over it.

She poured a second. I watched the line where the new binding disappeared between the planks. A drop formed, hung, and fell into the hull. Ruma waited. No second drop followed.

"Will you sail?" I asked.

"I will load it."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can promise."

She told two men to lift the water jar back aboard. One went to fetch a wooden wedge. The other asked whether the jar should be put where it had been.

"No," she said. "I moved it because the weight was wrong. Do you want to make the same hole twice?"

The man set it nearer the middle. She shifted it again by a hand's breadth and made him test whether it would move when the boat leaned. He pushed with both palms. It stayed.

I had been ready to call the seam repaired as soon as the first bowl ran off the outside. Ruma had been testing the whole boat.

Tahir came to the landing carrying the smaller message he had agreed to take. Hasan's clerk had wrapped it in oiled cloth and tied a second cord around the first. Tahir asked me to read the mark on the outer covering. I could recognize the carrier's sign but not the full account inside.

"Then do not tell me what it says," he said.

"I was going to tell you whose message it was."

"That I know."

He placed it under a cover where it would stay dry and asked whether I still intended to leave with the tide.

"That depends on the passage," I said.

"It always depends on the passage. Who is watching it?"

I had been to see the foreign ships. I had spoken with a watchman and merchants. I had not asked who would give the boats leaving our landing a safe way through the traffic.

We walked to the end of the boards. Beyond the houses, the tall spars stood over the harbor. I could see a broad sail on a local junk drawing air, then collapsing when the vessel turned. Several small craft were passing behind it, too close for their owners to be pleased.

"I will ask the harbor watch," I said.

"Ask before my men lift another basket."

I went with Safi to the watch post. Jamil was already there, speaking to a man whose shirt was wet across the back. The man had been sent from the outer anchorage. He said the junks had shifted closer to the strangers during the night. Their owners were waiting for a place to unload. Some said they were there to protect their goods. Others said they had been ordered to remain.

"Ordered by whom?" Jamil asked.

"I did not see an order."

"Then tell us who told you."

The man named a harbor officer. Jamil sent a runner to fetch him.

Safi asked about the boats coming from the far side of the water. The man said they had been gathering since morning. Some carried armed men. Others had baskets and rolled mats. He had not spoken to their leaders.

"Have they threatened the foreign ships?" Safi asked.

"Not where I could hear."

"Have the foreign ships threatened them?"

"They shout when a boat gets close."

"Have they struck anyone?"

"Not that I saw."

The watchman held his palms apart as though we had asked him to measure a thing that would not stay still.

Jamil took us to a higher platform above the harbor. The ruler's men used it to watch arrivals and departures. From there I could see more of the water, but I could also see how much of it lay hidden behind sails and roofs.

The foreign ships were farther apart than I had thought from the landing. Between them lay an open stretch of water crossed by small craft. Behind the nearer hull, several junks had anchored in a loose line. Beyond those, the harbor opened toward the outer passage.

Ruma's boat was a small shape near the inner shore. I found it by the pale cover over her jars. Tahir's was beside it. A third owner, Banu, had brought a shallow vessel with a load of goods that belonged to several merchants. He had agreed to carry them only as far as the next trading stop. He had not agreed to wait indefinitely while I looked at foreign ships.

"Can they pass behind the junks?" I asked the watchman.

"At low water, perhaps. There are shoals."

"Could they go outside the foreign ships?"

"They could. Whether they should is another question."

I looked at the passage again. I had navigated crowded water before. I knew how a boat moved when a current caught its stern. I could not see the bottom beneath that water, or the line of stones that the watchman said lay near the far side. The familiar shape of the harbor did not make every path through it safe.

Jamil asked what I intended to do.

"Hold the message boats at the inner landing until the outer passage clears."

"Who will tell them?"

"I will."

"And if one owner decides to leave?"

"I will ask him to wait."

"Ask him why he is leaving first."

I looked at him. He was not smiling.

"Ruma's boat has been delayed by a seam," I said. "Tahir is carrying a message that can wait one tide. Banu has cargo that he must deliver by the next market day. He may not wait."

"Then you know three reasons," Jamil said. "Now find out what each owner chooses."

He sent me down with Safi. We found Banu already moving his load.

"Where are you taking it?" I asked.

"To the far landing. I will wait there for an opening."

"The passage is crowded."

"I have eyes."

"There are shoals beyond the junks."

"I know where the shoals are. I was carrying goods here before you learned to count them."

His crew had lifted two baskets from the boat and were carrying them along the shore path. He had decided to move the load by land to another vessel rather than risk the passage. I had mistaken activity for an attempt to sail.

"Will you still carry the goods onward?" I asked.

"From the far landing, if the owner there agrees. I have already paid for the carrying."

I wanted to tell him that the message boats ought to remain together. He had not agreed to carry a message, and the goods were not mine.

"Then I will put your name on the new account," I said.

"Put the other owner's name there too."

He went to help the men with the baskets.

Ruma said she would wait. Tahir said he would wait if Hasan's clerk accepted that the message would leave on the next tide. The clerk did. We sent a runner to Jamil with the three answers.

Safi had been watching the outer water while I spoke. He told me a group of boats had moved closer to the junks. Their sails were furled; men worked poles along their sides. Another group remained farther out. I could not tell whether they were joining or passing.

"Do you think they mean to fight?" I asked.

"Some of them carry weapons."

"So do we."

"That is why I did not answer."

He called to a man on the watch boat and asked him to carry a warning to the inner landings: keep clear of the junks until the passage was opened. The watch boat's owner refused to go between the foreign ships and the anchored vessels.

"I will go along the shore," he said. "The men at the far landing can pass the word."

"It will take longer," I said.

"It will reach them."

Safi agreed. He did not ask the man to prove his courage by taking the shorter route.

I thought of the man at Laoe who had refused to sail until his brother's name was accounted for. We had called the delay costly. We had been able to leave only because the man finally chose to board. The water did not owe us a quick answer because our business was important.

A small craft came in from the outer harbor. Its rowers were sweating, and the man at its bow called for the watchman. The boats beyond the junks were approaching in separate groups, he said. One of the foreign ships had sent men to its deck and gathered its lines. He had heard shouting, but he had not seen a weapon fired.

Jamil came down to the landing to hear him. He listened without interrupting, then asked whether the foreign ship had raised sail.

"Not when I left."

"How long ago?"

The man looked toward the sun and gave a time that could only be an estimate.

I watched Jamil decide what to do with an answer that had already begun to grow old.

"Keep the inner boats where they are," he told me. "Safi, take the watch boat along the shore and tell the outer posts not to let traffic press toward the anchored junks. If the strangers want room, give them room."

Safi went at once. He did not wait for me to approve the order.

I stayed beside Ruma's boat while the men finished lashing its load. A breeze came down the channel and moved the loose end of the cover. Tahir's messenger sat on a basket, holding the wrapped letter between his knees. He had been ready to leave since morning.

"Are we going?" he asked.

"Not yet."

"Will the message be late?"

"It will be later than we hoped."

He looked down at the cloth. "That is not the same thing."

I had no answer.

A cry came from the outer landing. Then another. People were pointing beyond the junks. The nearer foreign ship had begun to move. Its bundled cloth opened high above the deck, taking the wind in a shape I had not seen before. The great hull turned slowly, then faster, and the water broke white along its side.

For a moment I admired the skill of it. Men on that ship had found a way to make so much weight obey a sail.

Then I saw where its bow was pointing.

The junks lay between it and the inner harbor, and our small boats were waiting on the shore behind them. The stranger was moving into the same water that we had spent the morning trying to keep clear.

---

The foreign ship moved faster than I had expected.

It did not rush like a light boat under a fresh wind. Its weight seemed to delay each change of course. Then the broad cloth above it filled, and the delay ended. Water pressed out from its bow. Men on the decks of the nearest junks began shouting.

The boat owners on our landing had been watching the same movement. Ruma told her men to stop loading. Tahir took the wrapped message from beneath its cover and placed it inside a dry basket.

"Are we to stay?" he asked me.

"Jamil ordered us to stay."

"Until when?"

"Until the passage clears."

"Then tell the people beyond the passage. They are still sending boats through it."

He pointed toward the outer landing. A small craft was leaving with a man standing in its bow, waving a piece of pale cloth. From our side I could not tell whom he meant to signal.

Safi's watch boat was farther along the shore. He had reached the next post and was trying to get the men there to turn back a pair of loaded craft. One of the craft obeyed. The other continued, its crew bent over their poles.

I could not shout far enough for them to hear me.

A young man at Tahir's stern said the pale cloth meant that the way was open. His cousin had used the same signal at another landing. Tahir said that at this landing it meant a boat should wait for the watchman.

"Which landing is he signaling?" I asked.

Neither knew.

I sent a runner along the boards to ask the watch post. The runner returned almost at once. The watchman had gone to the outer steps, and the man waving the cloth was not one of his crew.

Ruma looked at the moving ship.

"You have an answer," she said. "The water is not open."

She ordered her men to pull the boat farther under the landing's shelter. Tahir's vessel lay just beyond hers, with its stern exposed to the wash of passing craft. If the outer traffic turned suddenly, a boat could be driven against it.

"Can you move behind Ruma?" I asked him.

"Not without passing her bow."

"There is room while the tide is low."

"There is room where you stand. Come and see it from my boat."

I climbed aboard. The space looked smaller from there. A line ran from Tahir's bow to a post near Ruma's stern. Between the two hulls the water was moving faster than it had seemed from above. The shallow bottom beyond the post showed a pale patch beneath the surface.

Tahir put a pole in my hands.

"Touch there," he said.

The pole struck bottom sooner than I expected. I pushed again a little farther out and found deeper water.

"One side is enough," I said. "We can draw her around the post."

"We can try," he said. "If the men on Ruma's boat hold their line."

I asked Ruma. She came to look and agreed to have two men hold the line, but she would not move her own boat again. Tahir's crew began shifting the vessel. One man pushed with a pole while another eased the bow rope. The hull swung slowly toward the deeper patch.

For a moment the passage worked as I had imagined.

Then a small boat came hard along the landing, trying to escape the traffic outside. Its bowman shouted for room. Tahir's sternman pulled his pole clear to keep it from striking the other boat. Our hull drifted sideways. The bow rope tightened against the post.

"Hold!" Tahir shouted.

I put my pole into the water and pushed. The bottom was too soft. The pole sank into mud. I pulled it up, losing the pressure that had kept our stern away from the pale patch.

Something struck beneath us. It was a dull blow, not the crack I expected. The boat stopped moving.

Tahir looked at me once, then ordered the men to keep still.

The small boat passed. Its crew did not look back.

Water began to seep around one of Tahir's floorboards.

"We are on the edge," he said. "Do not push again."

I wanted to explain that the other boat had come without warning. Tahir had seen it. So had I. He had also told me to come aboard and judge the room.

A crewman crouched to feel the hull. He found the place where it had scraped and lifted his hand wet with mud. There was no great hole, but the board had opened enough for water to enter.

"Take the message ashore," Tahir told his nephew. "Take the rice after it. We can free the boat when the tide comes up."

His nephew reached for the wrapped message. A basket shifted as the hull leaned, trapping his fingers against the side. He cried out. I caught the basket before it fell, but the man's hand was already bleeding.

Tahir pulled him clear. The injury was shallow along two fingers, yet the nephew could not close them properly. He looked at the blood as if it belonged to someone else.

"Go ashore," Tahir said. "You will not handle rope today."

"I can still carry the message."

"You can carry it with the other hand."

The nephew climbed onto the landing, holding the wrapping against his chest. Ruma sent one of her crew to help him. She did not ask me whether the man needed help.

I looked at the water in Tahir's hull. We had moved the boat to protect it. It now sat aground, with its message delayed and one crewman's hand cut.

"That was my suggestion," I said.

"It was my boat," Tahir answered. "I agreed to it."

He looked past me to the foreign ship.

"Do not make me argue over blame while the harbor is moving."

We took the rice ashore. A woman who had been carrying fish offered us a shallow bowl to bail with. I thanked her and passed it to the youngest man aboard. He began dipping water from beneath the floor.

From the outer harbor came a series of calls. Safi's watch boat was turning back toward us, its rowers pulling hard. He stood in the stern, one hand on the gunwale, pointing toward the junks.

Jamil appeared at the head of the landing with two harbor men. He saw Tahir's vessel leaning toward the shallows.

"What happened?"

"I asked him to move behind Ruma," I said. "The bottom rose sooner than I judged."

Jamil looked at Tahir.

"He is telling it correctly," Tahir said. "We are taking the load off."

"Can the men get clear?"

"Yes, if the other boats leave us room."

Jamil sent a harbor man to keep the landing clear. He did not ask me to take command of the rescue. I went back to bailing.

Safi's boat reached the near steps. He jumped ashore before it had fully stopped.

"The strangers are moving toward the junks," he said. "Some of the boats outside are turning back. Others are still coming."

"Have you spoken to their leaders?" Jamil asked.

"Not all of them. The outer watch says the approaching groups have different orders."

"From whom?"

"They do not know."

Jamil sent the second harbor man to the ruler's officers. Safi looked at Tahir's grounded boat and the baskets on shore.

"Was anyone hurt?"

"Tahir's nephew," I said. "His hand."

Safi nodded. He had no time to tell me what he thought of it.

The nearer foreign ship had altered course again. Its high side was now angled toward the line of junks. Men on its deck moved together, pulling at ropes and gathering near openings in the hull. I could see their arms and backs, but not what they handled.

A local boat tried to cross ahead of it. The rowers stopped, then pulled backward. The foreign hull passed where they had intended to go. The boat rocked in its wake.

Someone on the outer landing waved a cloth. Another man answered with a shout. The words broke apart over the water.

I looked for the merchant who had offered to carry our greeting. He was standing beside his wax, watching the same movement. He had never been given a safe passage to the strangers. The question we had prepared lay somewhere between him and the watch office, still unanswered.

Jamil told the men on our landing to get away from the outer edge. Ruma called her crew in. Tahir's nephew had already gone to have his hand bound. The youngest man continued bailing because he had not heard the order.

I reached down and took the bowl from him.

"Come up," I said.

He looked at the water around his ankles.

"The boat is not empty."

"It will not be empty if you stay in it."

He climbed out. I followed, carrying the bowl.

A flash came from the foreign ship's side.

For an instant I saw only a pale opening and smoke pushing outward. The sound reached us after the light, hard enough that the boards seemed to jump beneath my feet. Men shouted from the junks. A flock of birds rose from the roofs along the water.

I had heard guns fired in Burné. I knew what a gun could do. I had never heard that sound come across the harbor from a moving ship.

The nearest junk turned its bow toward the shallows. Another remained where it was.

I could not tell what had been struck. I could see that the strangers had fired.

---

The first man to fall was not on the junk.

He had been standing at the end of the landing with a basket of fish at his feet. When the gun sounded he tried to run, caught his ankle in a mooring line, and fell against the boards. His basket went into the water. The fish floated for a moment before the current carried them beneath the steps.

He got up without help. I watched him look for the basket, then for the path home.

A second sound came from the foreign ship. This time I saw the smoke before I heard the report. Something struck the side of a junk. A splintered board flew into the water. Men on its deck threw themselves down.

Ruma caught my sleeve.

"Get away from the edge," she said.

"I can see the junks from here."

"So can the men who are firing."

She pulled me behind a post. A line of people was already moving up the shore path, carrying baskets, children and bundles they had not stopped to tie. A woman shouted for her husband. He was on a boat outside the landing, too far away to hear.

Tahir had climbed back onto his grounded hull. He was taking off the last of the rice. The youngest man was still bailing.

"Leave it," Tahir told him. "The water can have what we cannot carry."

The boy hesitated, then put the bowl down and climbed out. Tahir handed me the wrapped message.

"Give this to Hasan's clerk," he said. "Tell him I have not sailed."

"I will."

"And tell him my nephew's hand was hurt because I moved the boat."

"Because I asked you to."

"He can hear both."

I carried the message up the landing and found the clerk beneath the eaves of a storehouse. He took it and checked the binding. The outer cloth was damp but the inner wrapping held. He made a mark on his board and asked whether Tahir's boat could still leave.

"Not this tide," I said.

He did not ask whether I was ashamed. He had another message to protect.

Jamil was speaking to the harbor men near the storehouse. They had reports from three landings, each describing a different part of the attack. One said the strangers had taken a junk. Another said a junk had driven itself ashore. A third said the armed boats coming across the harbor had turned back.

"Which report is first-hand?" Jamil asked.

The harbor man could not tell him.

Safi came up the path with a wet sleeve. He had been helping turn a watch boat away from the moving ships. His rowers were waiting below.

"There are men in the water beside the nearer junk," he said. "I saw two. There may be more behind the hull."

"Can you reach them?" Jamil asked.

"From the shore side, perhaps. The current is running toward the outer passage."

"Can your boat carry them?"

"Three if they can sit. Fewer if they cannot."

Jamil looked at the men around him. Some were already being sent to keep the inner landings clear. Others were needed to carry messages to the ruler. He could not send every boat to the same place.

I knew the junk Safi meant. It had arrived after our return from Laoe with people and goods still waiting to be accounted for. Its sail was furled, and a patch of red cloth hung from its stern. I had never asked the master's name. I could not stop looking at it.

"I can go with Safi," I said.

"You are responsible for the message boats," Jamil said.

"Tahir's message is back with Hasan's clerk. Ruma will not sail until the harbor clears. Banu has moved his goods by land."

"That tells me where the cargo is. Who will answer for the people?"

"Ruma can answer for her crew. Tahir for his. I can ask them to keep the accounts until I return."

"Ask them, then."

I went back down. Ruma had already moved her people under the houses, away from the water. She would take wounded men from the watch boat if it came back, but she would not put her repaired hull in the line of fire. Tahir agreed to hold the list of carriers and to tell Hasan's clerk if any owner left.

"You will not be able to order me from out there," he said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

I had no answer he would have believed. He took the board from my hands.

When I returned, Jamil gave me permission to help the rescue, not to command an attack. He made me repeat the distinction. Safi's watch boat owner, a narrow-faced man who had been listening from the steps, said he would take us along the sheltered side of the junks. He would turn back if the strangers came between him and shore.

"It is my boat," he said. "I decide when it has gone far enough."

Safi agreed. So did I.

We pushed off with four rowers. The water close to shore was full of floating wood and small pieces of woven cover. Farther out, a broken pole rose and fell with the current. One rower tried to hook it aside. The owner told him to leave it. He needed both hands on the oar.

The gun sounded again. The report came from beyond the junk, hidden from us by its high side. A man on the junk's deck stood up, looked toward the foreign ship, and disappeared below the rail.

Safi told the rowers to keep close to the shoreward side. The current caught us as we passed the stern. We turned hard enough that water came over the gunwale. I grabbed a bowl and began bailing.

"Not yet," the owner said. "Hold the side."

I put the bowl down and caught the gunwale instead. The rower nearest me leaned back. His oar struck something beneath the water and shuddered in his hands.

A man appeared beside the junk. He was holding a piece of timber and kicking with one leg. The other hung below him. He shouted when he saw us.

We came alongside, but the junk's hull moved toward us with the swell. The owner shouted to keep our hands clear. Safi threw the man a line. He caught it once, lost it, then caught it again.

I leaned over to pull him in. Safi seized the back of my clothing and held me.

"Wait until the boat rises," he said.

The man came close enough for me to see blood on his shoulder. He had a thin scar across one cheek. I did not know him. I had wanted him to be someone whose name I could carry back to Laoe.

We lifted him aboard when the hulls parted. He cried out and tried to stand. Safi told him to lie down. The owner counted the people aboard and looked at the distance to shore.

"One more," he said. "Then we go."

There was another man in the water, face down. A rower reached him with the end of an oar. He did not move. We pulled him close and turned him over. His eyes were open. I put a hand under his jaw to keep his face above water while the others brought him in.

He did not breathe.

I had carried dead men after the fighting at Laoe. I knew how a body changed when the person inside it was gone. I still waited for this man's chest to rise.

The owner turned the boat toward shore.

"Go," Safi said. "I will come with you."

I looked up at the junk. Men were calling from its deck. A rope ladder hung over the side. Someone was trying to lower a third injured man toward the water, but the ladder was twisting and he could not hold it.

"We can take him," I said.

"The boat is full," the owner answered.

"I can climb up and help them lower him. You can bring these two ashore."

Safi looked at me.

"You will be on that junk when we leave."

"I know."

"Do you know who commands it?"

"No."

"Then ask before you climb."

I shouted to the men above. One answered in a speech I knew only in pieces. I called again in the trade language used at Burné. He pointed at the ladder and made a gesture for me to come. I could not tell whether he was inviting help or asking me to take the injured man away.

The owner brought us close once more. I caught the ladder when the hulls parted, put my foot on the lowest rung, and climbed.

The ladder twisted beneath me. For a moment I hung with my back against the junk's side and water beneath my feet. A man above caught my wrist and pulled until I could reach the rail.

I landed on a deck slick with spilled water and broken pottery. The man who had helped me up had a cut above his eye. He pointed to the injured sailor, who lay against a basket with one leg twisted beneath him.

I knelt to help. From the far side of the junk came another gunshot, followed by a crack that ran through the boards beneath us.

The men on deck began shouting toward the stern. I could see Safi's boat turning for shore with the injured man and the one who had not breathed. The owner had kept his word.

Between the junk and the landing, another vessel moved across the water. It was low and crowded, its crew trying to turn against the current. For a few moments it hid the watch boat from me.

When it passed, I saw the open water where Safi had been. The junk had begun to drift, and the gap between us and the shore was growing.

I had climbed aboard to help lower one man. I was now on the wrong side of a vessel I did not command, while the strangers' guns were still sounding beyond it.

---

The injured man tried to push my hand away.

I had been holding a folded cloth against his leg. Each time the junk rolled, his foot moved in a direction that made him cry out. He wanted me to straighten it.

"If I pull, it may break more," I said.

He did not understand. Or he understood and wanted me to pull anyway.

The man who had helped me over the rail knelt beside us. He spoke quickly in a language I could follow only when he slowed down. He pointed to the injured man's leg and asked whether I knew how to bind it.

"I can hold it still," I said. "I am not a healer."

He tore a strip from a cover and passed it to me. We tied the leg against a length of smooth wood. The injured man bit the edge of his sleeve until the binding was finished. When he let it go, his teeth had left dark marks in the cloth.

The junk gave a heavy lurch.

Someone near the stern shouted that the anchor line was dragging. The man beside me stood and ran to the rail. I followed.

The water between us and the shore had widened. We were no longer lying with our bow toward the landing. The current had taken us broadside, and a broken spar hung over the water where a line had once held it.

The foreign ship was nearer.

Its high side blocked part of the horizon. Men moved along the deck above us. I could hear their voices, but their words came as one sound. A smaller boat was passing between the two vessels, its rowers pulling hard to get clear.

The junk's master was at the stern, shouting to his crew. He was an older man with a broad scar across one shoulder. I asked him what had happened to the anchor.

"It holds in mud," he said. "The rope does not hold in the ship."

He showed me the frayed end of a line. It had been cut or torn near the bow. The remaining strands were black with water.

"Can we put another down?"

"Not while we are drifting."

"Can we use the sail?"

He looked up at the damaged rigging. A length of cloth had come loose and was beating against a spar. One of his men was trying to reach it.

"With which mast?" he asked.

I had been aboard less than a quarter of an hour. I had already offered him two ways to save a vessel whose damage I had not counted.

The master turned away to order his crew. I went back to the injured man.

A boy was crouched beside him, holding the wooden splint in place. He looked up when I came.

"Will he live?"

"I do not know."

"Will they take him?"

"Who?"

The boy pointed toward the foreign ship.

I looked at the injured man. He was awake, but his face had gone pale. If we tried to lower him into the water, he might die before another boat reached him. If we kept him aboard, we would have to trust what the strangers chose to do.

"Help me move him out of the open," I said.

The boy took his shoulders. I lifted his legs. We carried him behind a low cover near the center of the deck. The boy stayed with him, holding the cup of water that someone had brought.

I heard a new sound from the stern: wood striking wood, then a series of blows. A man had taken a mallet to the damaged steering gear. He stopped to listen, struck twice more, and tried the tiller. It moved a little.

The master called for the men to take their places. Two went to the bow with poles. Another began cutting loose a tangle of rigging.

I went to the master.

"Can you turn toward shore?"

"Not with that ship there."

"Then away from it?"

He pointed to the water beyond our stern. Another junk was moving toward the shallows. Its crew had put up a small sail. The vessel was listing, but it still answered its helm.

"If I turn there, I strike them," he said.

"Could they make room?"

"They are trying not to sink."

He had answered me without anger. That made it harder to ask again.

The foreign ship moved closer. A man on its deck raised something bright and pointed it toward us. Another leaned over the rail. I could not tell whether they were preparing to fire or to throw a line.

The master ordered his men to lie low.

I crouched beside the cover. The boy with the injured man had not moved. I pulled him down by the shoulder. A sharp report came from the far side of the foreign ship, and a cloud of smoke rose above its rail. The sound struck the harbor and returned from the houses.

Nothing hit us that I could see.

The master lifted his head. He shouted to the men at the bow to keep their poles clear. One of them had already jumped into the water. He was swimming toward a piece of floating timber.

"Come back!" the master called.

The swimmer did not turn. He caught the timber and began kicking toward shore.

A second man moved to follow him. The master caught his arm.

"You have a mother on this boat," he said.

The man looked toward the cover where an older woman was sitting with a basket pressed against her knees. She had been quiet since I climbed aboard. Now she rose and walked to the rail.

"Let him go," she told the master.

"He can help us turn."

"He cannot turn the sea."

The master let go. The man climbed over the side and dropped into the water. His mother watched until his head appeared beyond the stern. Then she sat down again.

I wanted to tell her that the current would carry him toward the outer passage. She could see the current. I had nothing to give her but another fear.

A boat came out from the shore, heading toward us. I recognized Safi at its stern. He was standing beside the owner, pointing toward the side of the junk that was still clear. The rowers were pulling hard, but the current pushed them away each time they drew close.

I climbed onto a low beam and shouted his name.

He looked up.

"Can you take the injured man?" I called.

He answered, but the wind carried the words away.

I pointed toward the cover. Safi shook his head and pointed back to shore. He made a cutting motion with his hand. I could not tell whether he meant we should cut a line or that his boat could not cross the water between us.

The owner of his boat turned its bow away from the foreign hull. He had promised to withdraw if the foreign hull closed the passage to land. It had.

Safi stayed standing until the boat had begun to move away. He raised his arm once. I raised mine.

I had wanted him to come nearer. I had no right to order his rowers to die for my choice.

The master of the junk came to stand beside me.

"Do you know those men?"

"Yes."

"Then tell them not to return."

I watched Safi's boat reach the clearer water near the landing. It was still moving. I could not see whether anyone aboard had been hurt.

"Who are you?" the master asked.

I told him my name.

He looked at me more carefully. "The man from Laoe?"

"I was there."

"My brother took goods there before the fighting. He has not come home."

I could have told him that I had kept disputed bundles under watch, that I had helped carry wounded people, that I had asked for names. He had not asked what I had done to make myself bearable.

"Do you know whether he was taken?" he asked.

"No."

"Did you see the place burn?"

"Yes."

He looked past me toward the foreign ship.

"Then you know how little it helps when a man tells you he was only doing what he was ordered."

I could not deny it. I had used those words in my own head.

The junk shuddered as its stern touched something beneath the water. The master went back to his crew. I followed him, but he did not ask for my help.

We had drifted close to a shoal. A man sounded the bottom with a pole and shouted the depth. The master ordered the bow turned toward the deeper channel. The steering gear moved a little. The hull answered slowly.

For a moment there was a way out.

Then the foreign ship crossed ahead of us. Its shadow moved over the water, and the open channel disappeared behind its high side. Men on its deck were looking down at us. I could see their faces now, pale and dark with sweat, some with cloth bound around their heads. One held a blade. Another carried a coil of rope.

The master ordered the crew away from the rail. The injured man was still behind the cover, and the boy was still holding the cup. The older woman stood with her basket, looking at the place where her son had gone into the water.

I could not keep the junk from being taken. I could not reach Safi. I could not return to Tahir and the messages I had left behind.

I went to the injured man and moved the wooden splint farther from the edge of the cover so it would not be crushed if someone fell against it. It was the only thing I could still do that I knew how to do.

A rope fell across the rail.

Someone above shouted. The master shouted back. I could not understand either of them.

A second rope followed, this one ending in an iron hook. It caught the wood beside the place where I had been standing. The line drew tight.

I put my hand on the injured man's shoulder. He looked at me, waiting to know what would happen next.

I had no answer.

Above us, feet struck the foreign ship's rail.

---

### Historical note

The two foreign ships and their confrontation with vessels near Burné are drawn from accounts of the 1521 voyage. The surviving witnesses differ in consequential details. [Antonio Pigafetta's account](https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/29th-of-july-1521/) describes more than a hundred approaching praus and four junks captured. [Francisco Albo's log](https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/21-june-29-july-1521/) reports 260 armed canoes and one captured junk; its published date range is editorial. Neither account establishes the intentions of all the local boats or the fictional conversations, signals, rescue and injuries narrated here. The chapter ends before the capture described in the historical accounts.
