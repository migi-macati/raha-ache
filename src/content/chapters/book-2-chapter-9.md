Nasir's sister was weighing fish when I found her.

She had put a flat stone on one side of the balance and three small fish on the other. The buyer held out his basket. She removed the smallest fish, watched the balance rise, and put it back. Only then did she tip the fish into the basket.

I stood beyond the stall's shade until the buyer had paid. She saw me before he left. She finished counting his coins against the edge of her board and did not look up again until he had gone.

"You came back," she said.

"Today."

"I heard you were taken by the foreign ships."

"I was."

She covered the remaining fish with a damp cloth. "Have you come to tell me that you saw Nasir?"

"No."

"Then what have you come to tell me?"

I had rehearsed the answer on the path. I would tell her what had happened aboard the foreign ship, how the men there asked for my name, and how little I could learn about the people they kept. I would explain that I had returned as soon as I was allowed. It had sounded like an account of what I had done.

"I have no new word of him," I said.

She folded the cloth's corner beneath the board. "I knew that when you walked up."

"I wanted you to hear it from me."

"Did Jamil send you?"

"He told me to come myself."

She moved the stone from the balance and put it beneath the stall where the other weights were kept.

"Nasir used to say a man could make a bad bargain sound good by telling you all the trouble he had taken to reach it. What I want is the name of the person who last saw my brother."

"Dali saw him at the cut, before the men came aboard. Iman was in the roots. Neither saw him reach the bank."

"I have spoken to Dali."

I looked at her.

"He has been here twice. The second time I made him show me where he stood, with the fish baskets for the boat and the water jar for the bank. He moved the jar farther away when he remembered how the current took him."

"Did he tell you anything he did not tell Jamil?"

"He told me he was afraid."

I had not asked Dali that.

She lifted the cloth to look at the fish. The sun had fallen behind the roof, but the air beneath it was still warm.

"Have you spoken to the man at the traps?" she asked.

"Not since I returned."

"Then there is something you can do. You can go to him and ask whether the voice he heard came before the attack or after it. He has told three people three different times. Perhaps he does not know. Perhaps the men carrying the question keep changing it."

"I can go tomorrow."

"On whose boat?"

I looked toward the landing. The boats nearest the steps belonged to people who had been answering questions all day.

"I have not asked an owner."

"Then you cannot go tomorrow yet."

She said it without anger. That made it harder to reply.

A boy approached the stall with an empty carrying basket. He was the same boy she had hired earlier to take questions toward the fish traps. He had grown used to entering her shade without asking. She told him to fetch the little board from beneath the rice jar. He brought it out wrapped in a leaf.

There were marks on both faces. She held the board where I could see them.

"These are the people who have taken my questions," she said. "Here is the boy. Here is his mother, who can say when he left. Here is the trap keeper's cousin, who says the man was upriver. Here is the price of a second journey. I have paid the first."

The boy watched my face. He had heard his own work counted and was waiting to see whether I would call it useful.

"You have done more than I knew," I said.

"I have done what I could pay for."

She put the board down.

"Jamil's clerk says the missing men are being counted at the landings. I went there. One man wrote Nasir with those who did not return from Hamzah's boat. Another put him among those who might have been carried away. The first said that was the same thing."

"It is not."

"I know. I made him keep both boards."

The boy picked up the empty basket. She sent him to a woman two stalls away to collect a length of clean binding fiber. When he had gone, she looked at me again.

"Who can correct the lists?"

"Hasan's clerk can take another account. Tahir is counting the boats that come in. Jamil ordered the names to stay open until there is evidence."

"That is three men. Which one will listen to the people who went into the trees?"

"Hasan will."

"Hasan heard the survivors before. Did he send anyone back?"

"He sent questions with the boats."

"Then we will ask him which boat and to whom."

She lifted the remaining fish into a shallow tray. I offered to carry it. She told me to take the water jar instead. The jar was heavy, and its handle had been repaired with cord. I followed her along the landing while she carried the fish herself.

At the lower steps men had laid three boards beneath the eaves. One recorded boats expected in the harbor. One held the names of people who had come back. The third was smaller, with a column of names and short spaces after them. Tahir stood over it with his injured nephew. The nephew's wrapped hand was tucked against his chest; with the other he held a cord tied to a post.

Tahir looked up at me and then at Nasir's sister.

"I have the name," he said before she could ask.

"Which name?" she said.

"Nasir. On the list of men still unaccounted for."

"Read me the names above his."

Tahir did. Two belonged to men from a lighter that had gone out during the firing. A third was the son of a trader who had gone to the far bank to bring in his father's baskets. There was no word of him.

"Who saw the lighter leave?" she asked.

"A steersman from the inner landing."

"Who saw it fail to return?"

"No one. It has not been counted back."

"Then why is it beside my brother?"

Tahir rubbed his thumb along the board's edge. "Because neither has returned."

"One may be hiding from the foreign ship. The other may be dead. Nasir may be in the trees. If you put them together, someone will say the board proves something."

"It proves we have not counted them," Tahir said.

"Then write that above the names."

Tahir looked at his nephew. The young man had been holding the board steady with his good hand. He shifted it closer to his uncle. Tahir made a fresh mark and asked Hasan's clerk, who had come from the storehouse, to copy the heading onto his own board.

I set down the jar. The sister had not asked me to speak. I stood beside her while the two men argued over the order in which the names should be read.

The clerk had written the name of the woman in blue from the foreign ship among those who had been seen aboard it. He had also written the younger woman's injury, because I had described it. Beside the older woman from the junk he had left a space.

"She was being taken toward the junk," I told him. "I did not see her reach shore."

"That is what I wrote."

He showed me. I had been about to correct a mark I could not read without his help.

Nasir's sister listened while he read the other names. When he reached a man from the far bank, she stopped him.

"His mother came to my stall. She said he returned before the firing."

"She said he returned to the bank," the clerk replied. "His father says he did not come home."

"Then you should speak to both."

"We are trying."

"Write which one you have heard."

The clerk turned the board over and made room for the names of the two witnesses. I watched him do it. I had spent the last days asking men to distinguish what they saw from what they heard. She had been making the same demand without anyone giving her a place at the board.

A rower called from the steps. A narrow boat had come in with its sail bundled along the gunwale. Its steersman was wet to the waist. Two men sat beneath a torn mat, holding a coil of rope between them. Their faces were gray with dried salt and mud.

Tahir went down before the bow touched. His nephew moved to catch the line, but the steersman shook his head and tossed it to a man with two sound hands.

"Count us," he said.

Tahir did. The two men beneath the mat gave their names. One was the owner of the missing lighter. The other was the son of the man who had supplied its water jars. They had gone ashore on a low bank when the foreign vessel crossed the outer channel and had waited for enough water to float their boat clear. They had not known anyone was looking for them.

"Did you see other men?" I asked.

The owner looked at me. "Which men?"

I said Nasir's name, and described Hamzah's boat and the shorter cut. He shook his head.

"We were not there. We were on the outer bank."

"Did you hear fighting?"

"Gunfire from the ships. Not from the cut."

Nasir's sister stepped forward.

"What day did you put ashore?"

He told her. She asked how many nights they had waited and whether they had seen a boat moving without a sail. He answered each question and then asked why she wanted to know.

"My brother is missing," she said.

He looked toward the other rower as if the man might have an answer. Neither did.

Tahir took the board from his nephew and struck through the two names, then stopped.

"Do not strike them out," Nasir's sister said.

"They have returned."

"Write that they have returned. Tomorrow someone will ask where they were."

Tahir made the correction on a fresh line. He asked the owner to name the bank and the man who had helped him push off. The owner was tired and wanted water. Ruma's crew brought a cup. Only after he had drunk did he answer.

I carried the jar back to the stall with Nasir's sister. The fish had begun to soften in their tray. She covered them and put the unsold ones into a basket for drying. The boy returned with the binding fiber and held it out to her.

"Tomorrow," she said to him, "we will ask the trap keeper again. We will give him the question in my words. You will bring back his words, even if they are that he remembers nothing."

The boy nodded.

I said I would find an owner willing to take us.

"Do not say us," she said. "I have not decided to go."

"I thought you would want to hear him yourself."

"I want someone who knows the water to hear him. If I leave my stall, who will pay for the next search?"

She measured the binding fiber and cut off the length she needed. She gave the boy a small portion of the fish for his mother. He thanked her and went.

I remained until she had covered the last basket.

"You have come back," she said again. "That is one answer I did not have this morning."

I waited.

"It does not change Nasir's place."

"No."

"Then go to Hasan. Find out which man can take the question and whether he will be paid. Do not send a boy to make a promise for a boat owner."

I took up the empty water jar.

Behind us the men at the steps were reading the returned rowers' names aloud so the clerk could amend his board. The two men were tired, wet and alive. Their return had corrected a count, and had told us nothing about Nasir.

I went to find Hasan before the storehouse closed.

---

Hasan had agreed to pay a boatman for one visit to the traps, provided the boatman named the water he would take and returned with the witness's words. He had not agreed to send me.

I went to the landing at first light to find the man he had named. The tide was still below the lowest sound step. Men were lifting a boat from its supports so they could scrape the growth from its bottom. They worked with pieces of shell and a broad knife whose handle had been wrapped twice with cord. The owner stood by the bow and pointed out a split seam that needed binding before the boat went back into the water.

The man Hasan had named was among them. He listened to my question while he held the hull steady with his shoulder.

"I can go when this is finished," he said.

"When will that be?"

"When the owner says the seam holds."

"Nasir's sister has paid for an inquiry already."

"Then she knows what a boat costs. I will take the question. I will not take you as another passenger without asking."

I said I understood. He went back to the hull. I watched him for a time, wanting to find a way to make his work faster. Every suggestion I thought of had been made by someone already holding the boat.

A shout came from the farther posts.

Tahir was standing with his nephew at the water's edge. Beyond the outer shoal a junk had appeared, its sail set low and patched near one corner. It moved slowly, turning against the current in small corrections. A boat with two rowers followed beside it, close enough to catch a line if it lost its way.

I recognized the patched corner before I recognized the men. The junk's master had shown me the tear when I climbed aboard to help his wounded sailor. I had watched the foreign sailors draw the junk alongside their high ship. I had last seen it from above, with the boy at the injured man's head and the older woman carrying her basket.

Now it was coming in.

I went to Tahir.

"Have you counted it?"

"Not until it reaches the post."

"That is the junk."

"I can see that it is a junk."

"The one I was taken from."

He looked at me. "Then stay where the master can see you. If he wants to speak, he will."

The junk touched the outer post with a dull knock. The crew had put folded cloth between the rail and the wood. Two men held the bow off while a third carried the line to shore. No one moved quickly. The master stood near the stern with his headcloth tied loosely, giving short instructions to the steersman.

He saw me. His mouth tightened, but he did not call my name.

Tahir counted the crew first. The master answered for the men at the stern and made the others answer for themselves. The boy was there, thinner than I remembered, still holding the cup from which he had given the wounded sailor water. The injured man lay under a covering beside the mast. His splinted leg rested on a roll of sailcloth.

The older woman sat near him with her basket between her feet.

When the count was finished, Tahir asked who had left the junk after I was taken.

The master named two men who had been moved to the foreign ship and returned before the junk sailed. He named a third who had been carried away and had not returned. He would not call that man dead.

Tahir made a separate mark.

"Where did you come from?" he asked.

"From the high ship's side, then the outer anchorage. They held us while they searched the cargo. We waited for the tide and for permission to leave."

"Who gave permission?"

The master looked out toward the foreign vessels. "A man who spoke to the one with the writing board. I do not know his name."

Tahir did not write a name for him.

Ruma came down from the storehouse with two of her crew. She looked at the wounded sailor before she spoke to the master.

"I have cloth and water ashore," she said. "If he can be moved, we can lay him beneath the eaves."

"His leg was bound again aboard the foreign ship," the master said. "I do not know whether they tightened it correctly."

"Then do not pull him by it."

She inspected the sailcloth beneath him and asked two men to bring a second length of cloth from the storehouse. The master told his crew to wait until she was ready. The boy had begun to lift the wounded man's shoulder, but Ruma put her hand on the sailcloth.

"You have done enough lifting for now," she told him.

"He will want me there."

"Then come beside him. Let the men carry."

The boy hesitated. The wounded man said something I could not hear. The boy moved to his head.

I took a step toward the junk. The master held out his hand.

"Stay there."

I stopped on the lower step.

"I came to see whether he was alive."

"You have seen."

"Can I help?"

"You helped when he was hurt. Then they took you. My men kept him alive after that."

I looked at the boy, who had carried the cup from the foreign deck to this landing. The master was right.

Ruma's men spread the second cloth. The junk's crew shifted the injured sailor onto it, keeping his leg supported. The boy walked beside them as they brought him down the plank. He did not ask me to carry anything.

The older woman waited until the wounded man was clear, then stood and lifted her basket. She climbed ashore without assistance. I went toward her.

"Your son," I began.

She stopped. Her face was tired and deeply lined around the mouth.

"Have you found him?"

"No."

"Did you give his description?"

"To Hasan's clerk. I told him about the scar on his left shoulder and the place where you last saw him swim."

"Who has looked?"

"I do not know who has gone to that bank yet."

She stared at me. I had answered the question I wanted her to ask, whether I had remembered. She had asked who had looked.

"I can take you to the clerk," I said.

"I can find a clerk."

She stepped around me and went up toward the eaves. She asked Tahir where people who had come from the foreign ships could give their names. He showed her the board. She spoke to him herself, setting her basket on the dry stone beside his feet.

The master had come ashore. He walked along the side of his junk, testing each patch with his fingers. A plank near the stern had been scraped raw where the vessels struck together. He pressed it and looked at the seam below.

"Did the foreigners do that?" I asked.

"Some of it. Some came when we grounded in the outer water. Some was there before."

"Can you sail?"

"We sailed here."

"Can you take another passage?"

He straightened and turned toward me.

"That is what you came to ask."

"No. I came because I saw the sail."

"You were looking for a boat before we arrived. Men have told me what the court wants from those who can still move."

"Jamil has not ordered you to go anywhere."

"Then why are you asking?"

I had been thinking about the boats that might carry questions to the outer banks, the boats that might follow a missing captive, the boats that might eventually take men toward Luzon. I had begun to count the junk among them before its master had stepped ashore.

"I should not have asked," I said.

He looked at the raw plank again.

"My brother was at Laoe," he said. "He went there because he believed there would be work when the fighting ended. We have had no word of him. Yesterday I watched men on the high ship count my crew as though they were jars. I do not know whether one of my men will come home. And now I have a hull to mend."

"I remember your brother."

"You remember that I told you about him. You do not know him."

"No."

He bent to the seam. I crouched a little way from him and watched as he put a thin sliver of wood into the gap to judge its depth. The scrape ran farther than I had seen from the steps. It would need a dry landing, a place to work, fiber, resin and men who knew how to fit the plank.

"Who can repair it?" I asked.

"My people can. If I can pay them to stop carrying other men's goods."

"How much?"

He named a quantity of rice, a share of resin, and two days' labor if the plank held when dried. If it had split beneath the waterline, he would need a new piece of timber. He had not inspected that far.

"I have some rice," I said.

"Keep it. I did not ask you to buy my forgiveness."

"I wasn't offering that."

"What were you offering?"

I could not tell him without making the rice into a promise he had not asked for.

"Food for the men who would have to work," I said.

"Then ask the men what they need. Do not put it in my account."

He returned to the hull.

I went to the storehouse. Hasan's clerk was reading a board with the names of the returned junk's crew. I asked whether the storehouse had rice set aside for the injured.

"It has water and cloth for the people brought ashore," he said. "Food has been offered by two households. One has already sent it. We have not promised repairs."

"Would Jamil approve the resin?"

"That depends on whose goods were aboard and who has a claim against the master."

"He was taken by the foreigners."

"That does not tell me who owns his cargo."

I had heard that answer from other men before. I had begun to hate it because it did not make the work go away.

I took the rice I had been keeping for my own meals and carried it to the men lifting the junk onto the mud. I told them it was mine and that they could use it for their midday food if they wanted. One man accepted it. Another asked whether I expected a place on their boat in return.

"No," I said.

He waited, then took the rice from the first man and divided it into two baskets so it could be cooked with the food they had brought.

By midday the tide had fallen enough for them to brace the junk on its side. I held a pole where the master told me to hold it. He would not let me work on the seam, and I did not ask twice. The scraped plank was worse beneath the waterline. One of the workers ran his nail along a dark split and shook his head.

"Not two days," he said.

The master asked him to show the others. They gathered around the split. One argued for a narrow patch. Another said it would open again as soon as the hull took a load. The master listened to both and chose to replace the plank. He asked who could fetch timber before the next market day.

No one offered.

The man who had taken my rice said he could go if another worker covered his share of a cargo delivery. The master asked who would pay that worker. No one answered.

I had imagined that the junk's return would give us another vessel. Its return had given us a damaged hull, an injured man who needed care, a missing sailor, and people who had to decide which work they could afford to leave undone.

I walked back to the storehouse for the question Hasan had agreed to send to the traps. The boatman I had spoken to at dawn was still scraping his owner's hull. The seam had not been bound.

"Will you go today?" I asked.

"Look at the water."

It had fallen away from the steps. His boat was high on its supports, and the resin pot had not yet been opened.

"Tomorrow?" I asked.

"If the owner says the seam holds."

I took the question back to Nasir's sister's stall. She was hanging strips of fish beneath the roof, high enough to keep them from the cats. She listened while I told her that the junk had returned and that the inquiry boat had not gone.

"Did the junk bring Nasir?"

"No."

"Did it bring anyone who saw the cut?"

"No."

"Then we still need the man at the traps."

"Yes."

She looked at the board on which she had marked the cost of the first two inquiries. There was space for another line. She did not fill it yet.

"You have eaten?" she asked.

I shook my head.

"Why?"

"I gave my rice to the men repairing the junk."

She went back to hanging the fish.

"Then you will have to find work for your own meal," she said. "Do not spend tomorrow's boat fare to make today feel useful."

I looked toward the water. Beyond the landing the junk rested on its braces, its exposed plank marked for removal. The master and his men had chosen to repair it. They had made no promise about what it would carry afterward.

Nasir's sister handed me the empty basket from which she had taken the fish.

"Bring water," she said. "The drying boards need washing before dark."

I took the basket and went to the well.

---

The merchant had brought his scales into the open yard.

He had placed them beneath the storehouse roof where everyone passing from the landing could see. A small weight sat in one pan. In the other lay a folded square of cloth. He was not weighing gold. He was showing how much weight his household had kept in the chest before the foreign ships came.

A man from the court stood beside him with two clerks. Hasan watched from the doorway. Three boat owners had come to ask whether their cargo would be held while the payment was investigated. One had brought a jar of oil he meant to sell that morning. He had put it on the ground between his feet and refused to let anyone carry it inside.

I came with the question for the man at the traps. I had hoped Hasan would be free to tell me which boat could take it.

"Here is the man they released," the merchant said when he saw me.

The people in the yard turned.

"I have already given my account," I said.

"Then you can tell it again before the men whose goods paid for you."

"I do not know whose goods paid."

The merchant picked up the cloth from the scale and opened it. It was empty.

"That is the trouble," he said. "The men who took my gold know where it went. The man for whom it was taken does not."

The court representative told him to put the cloth down. He had a narrow board on which the names of witnesses were written, and he kept turning it in his hands as though he expected to find a clean side.

"You have claimed that your store was entered without your consent," he said. "The clerk has recorded it. We are here to hear the men who carried the gold."

"Then bring them."

"Two are on the outer landing. One has not returned."

The merchant pointed at me. "And this one?"

"He did not carry the gold from your store."

"He received its benefit."

I wanted to say I had received my freedom. It was the answer everyone expected, and it would have made me sound grateful for something I could not name.

"I saw pieces of gold on the foreign ship," I said. "I did not see them taken from this yard. I did not see the person who first gave them to the men who brought them aboard."

The merchant looked at the court representative.

"Do you hear? He saw them."

"That is what he has said," the man replied.

The merchant took the small weight from the pan and set it beside three others. He had chosen each one to represent a measure of his loss. I could see the marks on them, but I did not know whether they matched any piece of gold I had seen.

"How much did you see?" he asked.

"I did not count it."

"Did you see a chest?"

"I saw gold handled in cloth."

"Was it this cloth?"

"No. I do not know."

He held the empty square up to my face. The cloth was plain and could have been cut from any larger piece. He had wanted me to recognize it. I could not.

One of the boat owners shifted his jar of oil away from the doorway.

"My men were told to carry a package from the storehouse to a boat," he said. "They were paid for carrying. If someone now says the package was stolen, are they thieves?"

"Who told them?" asked the court representative.

"A man who said he spoke for the harbor."

"Name him."

The owner gave a name. The clerk checked the board and shook his head.

"That man says he was sent by the merchant."

The merchant struck the scale's wooden beam with his palm. The pan swung and knocked against its support.

"I sent no man to empty my chest."

"You sent a man to the landing," the owner said. "He asked for a boat."

"To protect my goods."

"Then your man should say what he carried."

"He has not come back."

They began speaking over one another. The oil jar stood between the owner's feet, and every time he moved his sandals he made a small circle in the dust around it.

Hasan came out of the doorway.

"One question at a time," he said. "The man from the court will ask. The clerk will write who answers. If two accounts disagree, they will remain separate."

The merchant folded the cloth and placed it on the scale again. He had not been told to agree, only to wait.

The court representative turned to me.

"Did the foreigners tell you what they had received in exchange for your release?"

"They spoke through a sailor who knew some of the harbor speech. I understood that they wanted men returned to them. I could not tell what the sailor repeated to the others."

"Did you promise those men?"

"No."

"Did you hear a man from our harbor promise them?"

"No."

"Did you hear anyone say the gold would replace those men?"

"I heard talk of an exchange. I did not understand all of it."

The clerk wrote. I asked him to read the last answer back.

He said, "Ache heard that the gold was exchanged for men."

"No. I heard talk of an exchange. I do not know whether the gold was part of that bargain or a separate payment."

He looked at me, then scraped away the last mark and wrote again.

The merchant watched.

"If you take away the gold," he said, "the foreigners released you for nothing."

"I have not said that."

"Then say what you think they took."

"I saw gold. I saw the men from our harbor taken to the ship. I saw people still held there when I left. I cannot say which thing was offered for which."

A rower at the edge of the yard spoke up.

"My cousin is one of the men who has not come back. If the gold bought the prince, did it buy him too?"

The court representative asked his name. He gave it and pointed toward the outer water.

"His wife has been at the steps since morning," he said. "Do you have a board for her?"

Hasan's clerk brought the list of missing people. The rower refused to put his cousin's name beside men whose boats had not been seen. He wanted it written that the man had been taken aboard a foreign vessel. The clerk asked who had seen it. The rower named a boatman from the watch boat. Hasan sent someone to find him.

I recognized the difference. It was the one Nasir's sister had forced Tahir to make the evening before. The rower had come to argue about gold and had found a question about a person that no one could answer with money.

The merchant called to his assistant, who brought a sealed jar from inside. He broke the seal and poured out small pieces of metal onto a mat. They were not gold. They were weights and counterweights, the measures by which his household had counted payments.

"I will show you what was missing," he said.

He arranged them in a row, then called the clerk to inspect the marks. The court representative asked why he had not brought the actual record from before the attack.

"My son carried it to the river house when the foreign guns sounded."

"Where is your son?"

"With his uncle. He will return."

"Then we will hear him."

The merchant looked offended that anyone would need his son's testimony. He pushed the weights toward the clerk.

"These have been in my household longer than he has."

"The weights may be yours," the court representative said. "We still need the count."

One of the boat owners laughed under his breath. The merchant turned on him.

"You laugh because your cargo is still on your boat. If the court says my gold was a gift, they will come for your oil next."

The owner put a hand on the jar.

"Then I will ask who has the right to give it."

The court representative asked whether the merchant would sign a statement that he had made no voluntary contribution. The merchant agreed at once. He then asked whether the court would promise repayment.

"No," the man said. "We have not established what was taken."

"Will you at least stop calling it a gift?"

"I will record that you deny giving it."

The merchant stared at the scales. His claim had been heard, and he had not been paid. I knew the feeling. I also knew it was a smaller injury than losing a son or a boat, and that knowledge did nothing to make his missing gold reappear.

Hasan called me aside beneath the eaves.

"Nasir's sister sent a question," he said. "I have agreed to pay the boatman for one journey to the traps. He will go when his owner's seam is sound."

"She knows."

"Good. I need you to take this board back to her. The boatman must hear the question from her or from someone she chooses. I do not want him returning with an answer to words he never heard."

He handed me a thin board, still clean.

"Am I to go with him?" I asked.

"Ask the owner. I have paid for a question, not a passenger."

I took the board. Across the yard the merchant was gathering his weights. He saw me leave Hasan and called me back.

"Are you going to the river?"

"To a stall by the landing."

"Then you will pass the grain sellers. Tell them I will not extend credit to men whose accounts the court has not settled."

"Which men?"

He named me.

I stood holding Hasan's board.

"I have no account with you."

"Not yet. Men have begun asking whether I will supply the boats that take you home. I will not. Not while my chest stands open and no one will say who ordered it emptied."

"I have asked no one to supply a boat for me."

"Then you lose nothing by my refusal."

He turned away. His assistant lifted the scales and carried them into the storehouse.

The yard emptied slowly. The boat owner took his oil jar to market. The rower whose cousin was missing stayed to wait for the witness. The court representative left with two accounts that could not both be true. Hasan went back inside to find a clerk who could copy the questions.

I had survived the foreign ship. The merchant had survived the loss of his gold. Neither of us knew the full bargain, but he could keep his goods from me and I could not give him an answer. Men who had never been aboard the foreign vessel were already deciding what my release would cost them.

On the way to Nasir's sister's stall I passed the receiving house. Laila stood beside a cook and two men from the returned junk. A measure of rice sat between them. The cook was holding a board with more marks on it than I could read.

"The wounded man needs food he can swallow," one of the boatmen said. "His master has nothing left to sell until the cargo is counted."

"Then do not put it against the cook's evening rice," Laila said. "Take it from my store. Write who receives it, and ask the master for his account when he can stand here."

"Will he pay?" the cook asked.

"I don't know. I will still know where the rice went."

She saw me waiting by the passage. I had not spoken to her since the day I had asked her to find a boat for my message to Maynila.

"They said you were taken," she said.

"I was."

"I sent someone to ask which ship held you. He came back with three different answers. I stopped asking him to guess."

"I did not know you had asked."

"You were not here."

The cook lifted the measure. Laila checked that the board named the injured man rather than the man collecting his food. Only then did she tell them to go.

"I hear you may leave again," she said.

"I have asked about passage. No owner has agreed."

"Then I hope you have learned to wait for an answer."

She went inside with the board. I had come past her store with no provisions and no right to ask her to supply my journey. The wounded sailor would eat that evening. I did not know whether his master could repay her.

At the stall, Nasir's sister took the clean board and turned it over.

"Nothing written?" she asked.

"The boatman must hear the question from you."

"Then he shall."

I told her the merchant would not extend me credit. She asked whether I had borrowed from him.

"No."

"Then why did you go there?"

"I went to Hasan. They were hearing the payment claims."

She set the board beside her basket.

"That was their work," she said. "What have you brought for ours?"

I looked at the clean wood. There was room on it for a question that might be answered, and no space at all for the gold I had seen.

---

The watch boat lay on two rows of stones above the wet sand. Its owner had pulled the drain plug and left the hull tilted so the water could run out. Safi knelt inside with a strip of cloth wound around his fingers. Each time he pressed it into a seam, the cloth came away dark.

I found them after the morning market had begun. I had carried two jars of water for Nasir's sister before coming, and my shoulders still hurt where the cords had cut them. I had no food with me. The merchant's refusal of credit had reached the grain sellers before I did.

Safi saw me first.

"Do not climb in," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You have already put one foot on the stone."

I stepped down. The owner looked over the rail. He was a narrow-faced man whose name I knew from the landing, though I had never before had reason to use it. He had agreed to the rescue on the day of the foreign attack and had brought his men back when the high ship closed the passage. The agreement had saved them. I had been angry with it while I stood on the junk.

He lifted a little wooden peg from the bottom of the boat and showed it to Safi.

"This one has swollen," he said. "It will hold if the seam dries. The next one is loose."

Safi put his finger beside the peg. "That was where the hull struck the junk."

"Or where you put too much weight on the side."

"I was there," Safi said. "It struck."

The owner grunted. He did not argue further. He set the peg beside the others and asked his youngest rower to bring a piece of dry fiber.

"Did the injured man live?" I asked.

Safi looked up. "The one we brought ashore? He is alive. Ruma says his breathing is easier. The man who died has been named by his brother."

"Has the brother taken him home?"

"He has taken his body. Ruma sent cloth with him."

I had seen the dead man hauled aboard, his head knocking against the gunwale. I had not asked his name before I climbed onto the junk. I asked now. Safi told me, and I repeated it until I was sure I could remember.

The owner heard.

"Are you going to write him on a board?" he asked.

"If his family wants his name there."

"His family knows his name."

"I know."

"Then ask what they need before you count him."

He took the dry fiber from the rower and worked it into the gap. Safi held the hull steady with his palm. They had been at it since dawn. I could not make them finish sooner.

"Will this boat go out again?" I asked.

The owner did not lift his head. "It will go out when I have finished making it fit to carry my sons."

"Hasan has agreed to pay a boatman to ask after Nasir."

"That is not my boatman."

"I know. I came to ask Safi about the outer water."

Safi set down the strip of cloth. "Ask."

"Could the foreigners still be holding people beyond the shoals?"

"Yes."

"Could they have put people ashore on the far bank?"

"Yes."

"Would you take a boat to look?"

"No."

I waited for him to explain. He reached for another peg.

"Not with this hull," he said. "Not with men who have already carried one wounded stranger and one dead man through gunfire. Not because you have heard that someone might be somewhere."

"How would you know enough to go?"

"Someone would have to see where the ship lay. Someone would have to know which channel was clear. The owner would have to agree. We would need water for the rowers, a place to turn, and a way to tell the shore whether we were coming back."

"I can arrange a signal."

"You can propose one. Who will watch it?"

I looked toward the landing. The people who had watched the harbor during the attack were repairing nets, unloading jars, searching for missing kin and carrying water to the wounded.

"I could ask Tahir."

"Tahir has a nephew who cannot use one hand. Ask him what work he can leave."

The owner leaned over the rail. "And do not count my boat."

"I have not counted it."

"You asked whether it would go. Men who ask in the court's name sometimes go away saying the boat was offered."

"I am not asking in the court's name."

"Then say that when you speak to other men."

I promised I would.

Safi climbed out and washed his hands with a little water from a bowl. The water ran brown over the stones.

"Come with me," he said. "Ruma has something to show you."

We went along the bank to the place where Ruma had hung clean cloth beneath the eaves. The injured sailor from the junk lay on a mat with his leg supported by two folded rolls of sailcloth. The boy who had carried his cup was asleep beside him. The older woman sat a little way off, repairing the handle of her basket with a narrow strip of fiber.

Ruma was mixing warm water with crushed leaves. She tested it on her own wrist before bringing it to the injured man's leg. She looked at us without interrupting her work.

"He has a fever?" I asked.

"He had one in the night. It has fallen. His leg must stay still."

"Will he walk?"

"Ask me when he can stand."

I watched her loosen the outer binding. The sailor closed his eyes but did not cry out. Ruma made the boy move his sleeping arm away from the mat before she worked.

Safi spoke quietly. "Ache wants to know whether we can take another boat to the outer water."

Ruma looked at me. "Who is hurt there?"

"We don't know."

"Who has seen them?"

"No one we can name."

"Then bring me names when you have them. I have two people here who need clean water now."

She held out the bowl. I took it and went to the jar to refill it. When I returned, the older woman had stood. She asked whether I had spoken to anyone who might know her son.

"I gave his description to Hasan's clerk," I said. "I have not yet found a witness who saw him after he went into the water."

"Will someone ask along the bank?"

"Nasir's sister has a paid inquiry going toward the traps. I can ask whether the boatman could carry your question too."

"Would that delay hers?"

"I don't know."

"Then do not join them without asking her."

She returned to her basket. Her son was still missing, and she had not asked me to make his search part of my plan.

Jamil came from the upper path with Hasan. They had been inspecting the storehouse roof, where men had lifted a mat to find a leak. Jamil stopped when he saw us.

"Hasan says you have been asking for boats," he said.

"I asked the watch boat owner. He refused."

"Did you ask him as my messenger?"

"No."

"Good. Keep it that way."

Hasan handed me a small board. On one face were the names of owners who had given permission for specific journeys. On the other were marks for the cargo they had agreed to carry. Beside two names the clerk had left the space for a witness blank.

"These men offered to carry questions," Hasan said. "One will take Nasir's sister's inquiry when his seam is sound. The other will bring a witness from the far bank if his brother agrees to row. Neither has agreed to go after the foreigners."

"Why show me?"

"Because you keep asking how to go," Jamil said. "First show me how you would keep from taking what has not been offered."

I turned the board over. The names were familiar; the terms were not. One owner would accept rice for the crew but would not carry cargo. The other would carry a bundle of messages only if the receiving household named a man to meet him. Neither had offered his boat beyond the river mouth.

"The ruler has not returned your authority," Jamil said. "If you want work on the water, make an account that an owner can read back to you. Who pays the rowers? What is carried? Where may the boat turn? Who remains to hear from those who do not come back?"

"I could prepare that."

"Prepare it for a journey you can actually make. Do not draw the whole sea on a board and call it an agreement."

He went toward the storehouse. Hasan stayed.

"The merchant says he will not supply you," Hasan said.

"He told me."

"Then leave his goods off the account. If you need rice, earn it or find an owner who offers it. I will not turn an unresolved claim into your allowance."

I gave him back the board. He told me I could copy the terms after the owners had confirmed them.

When he had gone, Safi took me to the lower landing. A trading boat was being loaded with jars of oil and bundles of cloth. Its master had sailed to the river mouth often enough to know the shallow banks by the color of the water. I had spoken to him before the attack, when I still carried Jamil's messages.

"Ask him," Safi said. "But listen to the answer."

The master stood beside the plank while his crew rolled a jar toward the hold. He waited until it was secure before looking at us.

"I can pay for my own food," I said. "I can carry messages, count cargo and work the sail. I want passage toward the outer settlements when you next go."

"Who sends you?"

"No one. I am asking for myself."

"Where would you leave my boat?"

"Where you agree."

"What would you bring?"

"Questions about people taken during the attack. Perhaps a message for a household beyond the mouth."

"Perhaps?" He turned to his steersman. "Did you hear? He wants us to carry perhaps."

The steersman did not laugh. He was securing the jar against the side of the hold.

"I can bring the names before you sail," I said.

"How many names?"

"I don't know yet."

"How many people are waiting for answers?"

"More than I can count."

"Then you have not brought a cargo or a destination. You have brought grief. I have enough of my own."

He bent to check the binding on the jar. I waited. When he stood again, I asked whether he would consider a shorter journey, only to the far bank.

"No."

"Because of the foreign ships?"

"Because my crew is already hired, my cargo is already promised, and my brother will take this boat after I return. He has not agreed to your errand."

"I could ask him."

"You may. But not through me, and not as if I owe you a favor because you were taken."

He turned away to call for the next jar.

Safi and I walked back along the landing. He did not tell me that the master had been unfair. I was grateful for that, though I could not have said so without sounding foolish.

"Do you think I should stop asking?" I said.

"No. I think you should stop asking for the journey before you know the work."

"What work?"

"The questions Nasir's sister has already paid to send. The people Ruma has already taken in. The damaged boats whose owners have to decide whether they can sail. Begin there."

The rower nearest me lifted a coil of rope from the sand. A strand had frayed halfway through. He cut the damaged length, tied the sound ends together, and tried the knot against a post. When he pulled it free, the severed piece stayed on the sand.

Safi had gone back to the watch boat. I remained until the trading master loaded his last jar. His steersman shouted for room at the post; I moved the cut rope out of the way.

The boat pushed off on the tide it had been waiting for. It carried no message from me.

---

Ruma's sail was spread across the floor of her house.

The room was open to the evening air on one side. From the landing I could see the pale cloth rising and falling as the wind came through. A lamp stood beyond its edge, protected from the moving fabric by a ring of stones. Ruma sat with one knee bent, drawing a needle through a patch where a corner had torn loose.

I had come to return a jar. She had lent it to the people tending the injured sailor when the junk arrived. They had washed it and sent it back with me. I set it beside the door.

"Put it outside," she said. "The floor is full."

I moved it.

"Did the boy eat?" she asked.

"He ate with the men repairing the junk. He would not leave the wounded man until the master told him someone else could sit there."

"Did he sleep?"

"For a little while."

"Then someone should let him sleep longer."

She pulled the thread tight with her teeth. I stood by the doorway, looking at the patch. The new cloth was a darker color than the old sail.

"That will hold?" I asked.

"Until I can replace the corner."

"When will you sail?"

"When the corner holds, the seams are dry, and I have cargo worth carrying."

"I thought you were still helping with the wounded."

"I am. I have two hands. They cannot be in every place at once."

She set down the needle and rose to turn the sail. I caught the nearest edge without being asked. The cloth lifted between us, hiding her face for a moment. We lowered it on the clean side of the floor.

"Your boat was repaired before the foreign ships came," I said.

"It was made fit to sail. It was not made new. We have since carried water, cloth and men who cannot stand. Every trip leaves something to mend."

"Has Jamil asked you to stay?"

"He asked whether I could take wounded people. I told him what I could do. He has not asked me to remain until all of Burné is well."

She sat again. I knelt on the other side of the sail and held the patch flat while she stitched.

"Where will you go?"

"Upriver first. A household there owes me baskets from the last delivery. If I do not collect them, they will give them to someone who comes sooner. Then I may carry rice down to the outer settlements. The people who hired me before the fighting have not stopped needing food."

"Would you go while the foreign ships are still outside?"

"Not beyond the mouth until I know the water. I can work upriver without crossing their path."

I watched the needle move. She had stitched the edge of the patch twice where the old fabric was thinnest. The thread made a little ridge under her fingers.

"I had thought you might take a boat toward Luzon," I said.

"You had thought I might take you."

I looked up.

"I have been asking people for passage," I said.

"I know. A man came here today to ask whether I had promised you my boat. He said you had been speaking to the trading master."

"I did not tell him you had."

"He did not say you had. He wanted to know whether I would be the next owner asked."

"Would you?"

"Asked? Yes. I have been asked many things. Carrying you is another matter."

She drew the needle through and knotted the thread. The sail lay still between us.

"Where do you want to go, Ache?"

"Home."

"Which home?"

I could have said Luzon. I could have said the house where my mother once measured grain and men brought messages to my father. I could have said Burné, where Jamil had given me work and then taken it away. I had used both words at different times as though the people in each place would be waiting in the same positions when I returned.

"Luzon," I said at last.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Then you have no cargo, no return date, and no answer for the people who ask whether you will come back."

"I am not asking you to take me there."

"You are asking me whether I would. That is close enough for tonight."

She picked up the sail again. I held the edge while she checked the stitching against the lamp. The patched corner did not pull away.

"Why do you want to leave now?" she asked.

"Because every day I stay, another man asks me to explain what happened. The names do not grow fewer."

"Would they grow fewer if you left?"

"No."

"Then why?"

I let the cloth fall. She looked at me across it.

"Because I am afraid that if I stay, I will be made to answer for every person who did not return."

She waited.

"And if I go," I said, "I will have left them here."

"That is true either way. You cannot stand at every landing."

"I know."

"You say it as though knowing should make it easy."

Outside, a child called to someone on the path. The voice came closer, then moved away. Ruma listened until it was gone.

"My mother's brother sent word yesterday," she said. "He wants me to bring rice before the next market. His household has been feeding people who came down from the outer bank. Some are kin. Some are not. He will pay for the rice. He will not pay for me to remain here."

"Do you want to go?"

"I want to be paid for work I have already done. I want my boat to carry something other than fear. I want to see whether the baskets are ready. None of that requires you to come."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to offer to help with the sail."

"I am helping."

"Yes. You are."

She smiled then, briefly. I had not seen her smile since the ships came. I looked away because I did not know whether the smile was for me or for the small absurdity of my hands holding a piece of cloth she could have secured with a stone.

She returned to her stitching.

"When I first brought you aboard," she said, "you counted the men before you counted the water. I thought you would learn. Later you began counting the boats that belonged to other people. You have learned some things. I still do not know whether you can leave a boat alone when its owner says no."

"I can."

"You did today?"

"The trading master refused me. I left."

"Good. He told me you asked once more about a shorter journey."

I felt my face grow warm. "I did."

"Then you are still learning."

We worked in silence for a time. The sail had a smell of salt, smoke and the oil used to keep the needle moving. Ruma's hands were rough along the fingers. Mine had softened during captivity and hardened again carrying water. Once, when she passed the edge toward me, our hands touched. She did not draw away, but she did not hold on. She was reaching for the seam.

I wanted to tell her how frightened I had been when the foreign men took me below deck. I had told the court where I was held and what I saw. I had not told anyone how I had listened for footsteps and tried to decide whether they were coming for me.

"I thought I would die on that ship," I said.

She stopped stitching.

"I know."

"How?"

"Because when you came ashore you looked at every boat as though it could carry you away again."

I laughed once, without meaning to.

"I was trying to see who had returned."

"You were doing that too."

She put the needle down. For a moment she seemed about to say more. Instead she lifted the patched corner and folded it over.

"I was afraid for my crew," she said. "I was afraid the foreigners would take my boat. I was afraid that if I carried one more injured man, the seam would open and I would lose the people already aboard. I have not finished being afraid. Tomorrow I will still sail when the boat is fit."

I sat back on my heels.

"Will you be angry if I leave Burné?"

"Why should my anger decide where you live?"

"Because I have asked you to trust me."

"I can trust you with one thing and refuse you another."

"What can you trust me with?"

She pointed to the patched sail.

"Hold that corner while I tie it."

I did. She finished the knot, tested it, then took the cloth from my hands.

When the sail was folded, she put the needle into a small wooden case and carried the case to a shelf. The lamp was burning low. She did not ask me to stay for food, and I had none to offer her.

At the doorway she said, "There is a boat coming downriver after the next market. Its owner trades near the mouth. He has a sister in Luzon, or so he says. I have carried his baskets before."

"Would he take me?"

"I have not asked him. I can give you his name and tell him you carried water for the wounded. He will decide what that is worth."

"Would you speak for me?"

"I will tell him what I saw. I will also tell him about the day you asked a crew to follow a shorter passage without its captain's leave."

I looked at her. "He may refuse."

"He may."

"And you would still tell him?"

"Yes."

She lifted the jar I had left outside and checked that it was clean.

"If you want someone to take you across the water," she said, "let him know the man he is taking. I cannot give him a better one with my words."

I asked her for the boat owner's name. She told me, but I did not put it on Hasan's board. It was a lead for my own passage, not a commitment from anyone.

As I went down the path, Ruma called after me.

"Tell Nasir's sister I can carry a question upriver if it does not delay the rice. Ask her first. Do not promise it for her."

I said I would.

The house behind me grew quiet. Ruma had work in the morning, a sail to hang, baskets to collect, and a boat she could take away without waiting for my answer. I walked toward the landing with the name of another owner in my head. He might carry me toward Luzon. He had not yet said yes.

At Nasir's sister's stall, the shutters were already tied down. I left no message. Her inquiry had a boatman and a price of its own, and she would hear Ruma's offer from me when the market opened.

---

Three mornings passed before the boat to the traps was ready.

On the first, its owner found another wet seam after the hull had been lowered. On the second, the man who was to row with him was called to help carry a sick child to his mother's house. On the third, the resin held when they pushed the boat into the shallows and let it rest there until the sun stood above the palms.

I had gone to the landing each morning. I had learned to ask whether the boat was ready before asking whether it would go.

Nasir's sister came on the third day with a small board wrapped in cloth. She had written no words herself. The marks were those she had made with the boy and his mother to distinguish the first inquiry from the second. She carried a little packet of dried fish for the rowers and a separate portion for the man at the traps if he agreed to speak.

"Where is Hasan's man?" she asked.

The boatman stood beside his owner, testing the line at the bow. He came when he heard her voice.

"You have been paid for one question," she said.

"One journey," he corrected. "I will ask what you have given me to ask. If the man is away, I will not go farther without another agreement."

"That is fair."

She took a thin strip of wood from the cloth and held it before him. The boy who had carried her earlier questions stood at her side. He had come because he knew the path to the traps and could name the households along it, though the owner had not agreed to carry him.

"Tell me what you will ask," she said.

The boatman repeated the question he had heard from Hasan: whether the trap keeper had heard a voice at the cut before the attack or after it. He added that he would ask whether the man saw anyone there.

"Do not add the second question as though he has already seen someone," she said. "Ask what he saw, if anything. Then ask what he heard and when."

The boatman nodded. She made him repeat it. He did, this time without putting a person into the place where only a voice had been reported.

"Who will hear the answer?" she asked.

"My cousin. He is rowing."

"Will you bring the answer to Hasan?"

"To you first, if you are here when we return. Then to Hasan, with the same words."

She looked at the boy. "Listen. If he returns when I am at market, you will bring him to me. Do not tell me the answer yourself before he does."

The boy agreed.

The owner came forward and asked for the packet of food. He opened it and divided the fish into two portions, one for each rower. Nasir's sister told him the smaller packet was for the witness. He wrapped it again and set it beneath the seat.

I had brought a second board. On it Hasan's clerk had marked, at my request, the names of people whose families still sought an answer from the outer banks. The older woman's son was among them. I had asked her whether she wanted the question carried on this journey. She had said she wanted to know first whether the boat would pass the place where he had entered the water.

It would not.

I left her board wrapped in my cloth.

"Is there anything else?" the owner asked.

"No," I said.

He looked surprised. "You have been here three mornings. I thought you would ask to come."

"I would like to."

"That is not an answer."

"I will not come unless you agree."

He glanced at his rowers. The cousin was already sitting at the stern, one hand on the steering paddle. The boatman had stowed a water jar beneath the forward seat.

"I do not agree," the owner said. "Two men can carry the question. A third would use food and space we did not count."

"Then I will stay."

He nodded once and turned to the bow line. I had hoped that saying it would cost me less than it did. I wanted to see the cut. I wanted to ask the trap keeper myself. I wanted to be present when someone finally spoke a word that changed Nasir's place on the board. The boat did not belong to that wanting.

Tahir came down with his nephew. The young man's injured hand was still bound, but he could now hold the board against his chest without wincing. Tahir read the names of the two rowers aloud. He marked the boat's intended bank and the time of departure.

"What if they come back after dark?" the nephew asked.

"Then we will hear them when they come," Tahir said. "We will not mark them missing at sunset."

Nasir's sister looked at him. "Good."

The owner pushed the boat into the water. The rowers stepped in one at a time, keeping their weight low while the hull rocked. The boatman set the packet for the witness under a mat. The cousin tested the paddle against the current.

"One journey," the owner called. "No cargo on the return unless you have room and I have agreed."

The boatman raised his hand. They moved out from the landing, first sideways, then forward when the current took the bow. The cousin corrected with two strokes. They passed the first post and turned toward the channel that led to the traps.

Nasir's sister watched until the boat was beyond the bend.

"Now we wait," I said.

"We work," she replied. "Waiting does not dry the fish."

She went back to her stall. The boy followed with the empty wrapping cloth. I remained until the boat had disappeared.

Jamil found me beside Tahir's boards.

"Hasan says you prepared an account," he said.

"I prepared two. One for the inquiry that has gone. One for the people who may still be on the outer water."

"Show me the second."

I unwrapped it. I had kept the names apart according to the last place each person had been seen. The woman in blue and the younger woman with the injured ear were listed among those last seen aboard the foreign ship. The older woman's son was recorded as having entered the water from the junk. The junk's missing sailor had been taken away and not returned when the master left. Nasir's name stood alone beneath the cut, with two witnesses who had not seen him reach the bank.

Jamil read the headings. He did not ask why I had made them.

"Which of these people have you promised to recover?" he asked.

"None. I have told their families what I know."

"Which families have agreed to let you carry their questions?"

"Nasir's sister has given hers to the boatman. The older woman asked about the bank where her son swam. I have not asked the other families for permission to send anything in their names."

"Then do not send it yet."

"I won't."

He turned the board over. On the back Hasan's clerk had marked the two owners' terms for journeys they might make later, leaving blank the places they had not agreed to visit. One was willing to carry messages upriver. Another might go to the outer settlements after his cargo had been delivered. Neither would cross to the foreign ships. I had not written Ruma's name beside either offer.

"Who pays?" Jamil asked.

"Hasan has paid for the inquiry already under way. The other journeys have no payment yet. I have no claim on the merchant's store."

"Who would hear the answers?"

"The households that sent the questions. Hasan's clerk can keep copies if they agree."

"Who would decide when a boat turns back?"

"The owner and the man he puts in charge of it."

"Not you?"

"Not unless an owner chooses me for that work."

Jamil looked toward the bend where the inquiry boat had gone.

"You could have made this account before the foreign ships came," he said.

"I did not think I needed to."

"No."

He held the board out to me.

"Hasan may use you to carry questions between the families and his clerk. He will tell you which ones. You will be paid for work done, not for a title. If an owner asks you to sail, you will bring his terms to Hasan before claiming any court authority. That is what I can offer."

"Will the ruler agree?"

"He has agreed that the inquiry must continue. He has not restored your command. Do not make my words into his."

"I understand."

"I hope you do."

He started to leave, then stopped.

"The merchant's son returned yesterday. His account of the gold is different from his father's. Hasan will hear him. You are not to settle the difference by repeating what you saw aboard the ship."

"I couldn't."

"You have tried to settle other things you could not know."

I had no answer. He went up the path toward the storehouse.

The work he had offered was smaller than the work I had once held. It would not put a sail under my hand or a captain's place beneath my feet. It would let me carry questions from people who wanted them carried. I could do that without taking a boat from anyone.

At midday I found the older woman beside the repaired junk. The master and his workers had lifted the damaged plank free. The gap in the hull was wider than it had looked from the water, and the new timber lay beside it, still rough along one edge. The boy had been sent to bring resin from a household beyond the market. The injured sailor remained under Ruma's care.

I told the older woman that the inquiry boat would not pass the place where her son had swum.

"Then it will bring me no news," she said.

"Not today."

"Will another boat go there?"

"I have no agreement for one."

She ran her thumb along the handle of her basket. "The master says he may have to leave before the plank is finished if the men cannot be paid. He wants to find another place to work. If he goes, I must decide whether to stay and look for my son or travel with the people I know."

"What will you do?"

"I will decide when he tells me where he can take me."

She looked at the open hull. "You have been telling people you may go to Luzon."

"I have asked about passage."

"Then you may leave before I know whether my son is alive."

"Yes."

"Do not say you will remember him as though that is the same as looking."

"I won't."

She took her basket and walked toward the eaves. I watched her go. I could not promise to remain until her question was answered. I could promise to leave the question where another person could find it, with the name of the one who had asked.

Ruma returned late in the afternoon with a bundle of baskets balanced across her boat. She had gone upriver and back on the same tide, collecting part of the payment she was owed. Her crew unloaded the baskets before she stepped ashore. She saw me beside the posts.

"The owner I told you about has sent word," she said.

"Is he coming?"

"After the next market, if the water is high enough. He may have room for one passenger bound toward the mouth. He has not said where he will go after that."

"Will he speak to me?"

"If you are here. I told him you wanted to ask."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me for a place he has not given you."

She handed me a small bundle of cord to carry up to the storehouse. I took it. She went back to her crew to count the baskets.

When the sun dropped behind the roofs, the inquiry boat had not returned. Tahir left its name on the board beneath the heading for boats out on agreed journeys. He had not moved it to the missing list. Nasir's sister brought him a little food and asked whether anyone had seen the boat beyond the bend. No one had. She did not ask him to change the mark.

I carried Hasan's questions between the landing and the storehouse until there was no light left to read them. The board with the names of the missing stayed wrapped beside me. I had made no promise that the woman in blue would come home, or that Nasir had reached the bank, or that the older woman's son was dead. The names remained.

The owner Ruma knew might take me toward Luzon. Jamil would let me work in Burné without giving me back the authority I had lost. Neither path was closed. Neither was ready.

Below the last steps, the water turned and began to rise again. I watched for the small boat from the traps. I could not see it yet.
