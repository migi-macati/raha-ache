The boat I had pointed to was already full.

Its owner let me look. A bundle lay beneath the crossbeam where I had imagined sitting, and the water touched the hull close below the rim. Two men were still trying to find a place for a covered basket. The owner stood with his foot on the bow and watched them lift it out again.

“Could you take two more?” I asked.

“Look at her.”

“I could work.”

“You could work from the shore.”

He took the basket from his men and carried it back to the landing. I stayed until he had put it down. Then Sulad touched my shoulder, and we moved aside so the men could push off.

The fish sheds smelled of salt, smoke and wet rope. A woman was pulling small bones from a piece of dried fish. She worked beneath a roof that shaded several parcels wrapped in leaves. The fish was for sale. I knew that before I asked, but I watched her hands long enough that she looked up.

“You have water,” she said.

“We need food.”

“Then you have brought the wrong thing to buy it with.”

Our jar stood between Sulad's feet. Its chipped rim had been washed clean, but I could still smell the creek in the cloth around its neck. I had paid for that water with my food. I could not pay for food by pouring it away.

The woman took another bone from the fish and set it with the others.

“Do you have a place to sleep?”

“No.”

“Then you should find one before the light goes.”

I looked toward the boats. “We are trying to reach Brunei.”

She stopped picking at the fish. She had not stopped when I said we were hungry.

“Which part?”

I had never been asked that. In Maynila I had spoken of Brunei as if it were one house, with my grandfather waiting somewhere inside.

“The court,” I said.

“Whose court?”

“Siripada's.”

The woman looked at Sulad. He said nothing. She turned the fish over and found a bone she had missed.

“Many people would like a man at court to know their names,” she said. “What would you ask him to do?”

“Receive me.”

“Why?”

I had meant to save the answer for a man who could take us there. I had imagined saying it from the deck of a boat, already on the way, with the sound of the water carrying some of the words away.

“He is my mother's father.”

The woman laid the fish down.

A man behind us was dragging a coil of rope along the boards. He slowed when I spoke. I watched him stop to free the rope from a splinter. He did not turn his head.

“What is your mother's name?” the woman asked.

I told her who my mother was in Maynila and which house she had governed. She listened without nodding. When I finished, she asked who had sent me.

“No one.”

“Then who will say this is true when someone asks?”

“I will.”

“That is what you have just done.”

I felt the heat rise in my face. I could hear my mother correcting a messenger who had brought a report without the name of the person who saw it. I had never liked her making him begin again.

“Sulad came with me,” I said.

She turned toward him. “Can you tell me whose grandson he is?”

“I can tell you where we left from,” he said. “I know his mother's household. I have never seen the man he calls his grandfather.”

“He knows who I am,” I said.

“I know what you have told me, too.”

The woman waited. I had brought Sulad to the landing as if his presence were a second witness to everything I said. He had offered only what he could know.

“Would you take word to Brunei?” I asked her.

“I am not going there.”

“Do you know someone who is?”

“Perhaps. A man who comes for cloth has spoken of going upriver where Brunei traders receive goods. He has not come today. I do not know where he will go after that.”

“Could you tell him?”

“I could tell him that a young man from Maynila claims Siripada is his grandfather. I cannot tell him that I know it.”

It sounded small. It also sounded like the first thing anyone at the anchorage had offered to do that might travel farther than our jar.

“Tell him my name,” I said.

She held out her hand, palm up. I thought she wanted payment and looked at the empty cloth at my waist.

“No,” she said. “Say it once, slowly, so I do not give him another man's name.”

“Ache.”

She repeated it. I corrected the sound she had missed. She repeated it again, more carefully.

“And if the man does not come?” I asked.

“Then I will have no one to tell.”

“If he comes but will not take us?”

“Then he will not take you.”

The man with the rope had freed it from the boards. He stood beside a mooring post, gathering the loose end into his arms. I saw his mouth move. A boy carrying a basket beside him looked toward us.

“Could we speak somewhere else?” I asked the woman.

“We could have. You chose to speak here.”

She was right. I wanted to ask her to forget what I had said, but the man by the post was already telling the boy something, and the boy was looking at me as if I had changed shape.

Sulad lifted our jar.

“I am going to find somewhere we can sit without blocking the landing,” he said.

“Wait.”

He stopped. I had not meant to give an order, though it had sounded like one.

“Will you stay?”

“For tonight,” he said. “You still have to find out what that man will carry, if he comes.”

He went toward the high ground beside the sheds. I remained with the woman and her fish. She picked up the piece again, inspected it against the light, and pulled free the last bone.

“You should eat when you can,” she said.

“I have nothing to buy it with.”

“I heard you.”

She set the fish on a leaf, beyond my reach, and turned to a customer who had been waiting behind me. I moved out of his way.

At the mooring post the boy had begun asking the rope man questions. He pointed at me once. The man did not answer until he had finished coiling the rope.

I had spent the day asking strangers to take me somewhere. Now strangers had a name they could carry without me.

* * *

We slept on the hard ground above the fish sheds. Sulad kept our jar between us. Neither of us slept much.

The cloth buyer came the next morning. The fish woman told him my name in front of me, just as she had promised.

He did not bow. He looked at the salt dried on my sleeves and asked whether I could keep rain off a bundle without cutting the cord that held it.

“I have done that work,” I said.

“Did the bundle arrive dry?”

“One did. The other was counted separately.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It arrived.”

He turned to Sulad. “And you?”

“I have carried water and emptied it from a boat. I have carried goods ashore.”

“Were you paid?”

“Yes.”

The man looked between us as if the answers were more useful than my grandfather's name. He had cloth to send with a carrier bound for Brunei. The carrier needed hands to shift the loads at the landing and watch them aboard. We would eat with the crew. There was no silver, no promise of an introduction, and no place reserved for us after the goods were delivered.

Sulad asked where we would sleep during the crossing. The man answered him. I listened. When the master came to inspect the cloth, he repeated the terms to each of us and heard each of us agree.

I wanted to tell the fish woman that I had found a way forward. She was cleaning a knife when we left. She looked up, saw our jar, and said, “Keep that upright.”

That was all.

For the first part of the voyage I was glad to be among people who had no reason to speak of my mother. The master gave me a place beside the cloth, where I could call if water reached the outer wrapping. The bundles had been tied in pairs, their knots left visible so that the receiver could count them without cutting them open. When spray struck the side, I watched it run down the covering and away from the cargo.

I had learned to watch a fold. I had not learned to make the wind stop.

Sulad carried water to the men at work and kept our drinking jar clear of their feet. At night, when the men had finished eating, he sat where the hull made a shallow shelter from the breeze. I sat beside him. He did not ask whether the cloth buyer believed me.

On another day a crewman did.

“Your grandfather is a king?” he asked.

“That is what I came to find out how to say to him.”

The man laughed. “You should know how to say it before you leave home.”

“I know who he is.”

“Then why are you working beside his cloth?”

“It is not his cloth.”

The crewman pulled a loose strand from the mat beneath him and rolled it between his fingers.

“Someone at the anchorage said a prince was going aboard,” he said. “I thought we had missed him.”

I wanted to answer as I would have answered a man in my mother's hall. There was no hall here, and the master was listening from the stern.

“Then you have found him,” I said.

The crewman looked at the cord I had been told to watch.

“Keep an eye on that, prince.”

I did. The next time the covering shifted I called the man responsible for it. He put it back without looking at me. The crewman who had laughed said nothing more.

We passed banks where the trees grew close to the water, and stretches where I could see nothing but a low line of land. I learned the sounds that meant a man was taking in a line, and the sounds that meant I should move out of his way. When I tried to help with a heavy bundle without being asked, the master told me to put it down.

“You know where it belongs,” he said. “You do not know what is beneath it.”

I waited until the man assigned to it lifted the other end. We moved it together. My palms burned afterward. I kept the skin from tearing by doing less with my hands and more with my eyes.

One evening Sulad told me he had spoken to a man who had been to Brunei before.

“What did he say?”

“That goods go to one landing and people to another. It depends on whom you know.”

“Does he know anyone near Siripada?”

“He knows a man who receives jars.”

“That is not near enough.”

“It is nearer than anyone we knew yesterday.”

I thought of the fish woman saying my name slowly so she would not give it to the wrong man. It had reached a crewman before it reached anyone who could help me. I had been afraid that no one would hear it. Now I wondered how many people had.

When the master first pointed ahead and called the place Brunei, I was crouching over the cloth. I had expected to stand for that moment.

Houses rose above the water on posts. Between them lay narrow spaces where boats moved in and out. Farther along the bank, roofs stood among trees on higher ground. I could hear voices from a landing before I could distinguish any face. A small boat crossed in front of us carrying baskets covered with leaves. Its paddler shouted at our master, who answered and changed our course a little.

There was no single gate. I had imagined one because I had imagined a single house.

The master brought us alongside a platform where men were already waiting for the cloth. One of them held a board on his knees. He counted the pairs as they came ashore and had the knots shown to him before each bundle was carried under cover. The master counted with him. Neither accepted my count until the last bundle lay on the planks.

Sulad helped carry the empty water vessels. When the men were paid in food, he received his share and I received mine. We had completed the work that brought us here. The master was finished with us.

“Who can take me to Siripada?” I asked him.

He pointed beyond the landing. “Ask the man who keeps the arrivals. I deliver cloth. I do not bring petitioners to the ruler.”

The man he indicated sat beneath an awning with two others. People approached him, spoke, and sometimes waited while he sent a boy along the bank. He was examining a piece of cord when I came forward. Sulad stood at my side with our jar.

“I have come from Maynila,” I said.

The man did not look up. “For trade?”

“To see Siripada.”

“Who sends you?”

I gave him my mother's name and the house she governed. He set down the cord.

“Who carries her word?”

“I do.”

“That is your word.”

“I am her son.”

“Who here can say so?”

I turned toward Sulad. He met my eyes and then faced the man.

“I came with him from her household,” he said. “I cannot speak for his grandfather.”

The man listened to that more carefully than he had listened to me.

“Has your mother sent a message ahead?” he asked.

“I do not know.”

“Have you brought one?”

“No.”

He spoke quietly to one of the boys beside him. The boy went toward the houses on the higher bank.

“You will wait,” the man told me. “I can send your name. I cannot decide whether the house will receive you.”

“And my companion?”

“Is he part of your household?”

I opened my mouth to say yes. Sulad had carried my jar, slept beside me, and stayed when a boat could have taken him back. But he had never agreed to become mine.

“He came by his own choice,” I said.

“Then he must say what he wants.”

Sulad shifted the jar into his other hand. “I want to remain here until he knows whether he is received. I can work for my food if there is work.”

The man pointed to the shade at the far end of the platform. “Wait there. Do not enter the houses beyond the landing without being called.”

We went where he pointed. I could see boats coming in beneath the posts, people passing goods through openings in the floors, and children calling to one another across the water. No one stopped to look at us. I had come far enough to see the place my mother had named, but the people in it still had their own business.

Sulad set our jar between his feet.

“Did you mean to call me your man?” he asked.

“For a moment.”

“Why didn't you?”

“Because you would have told him otherwise.”

“I would.”

I watched the boy who had carried my name disappear among the houses. I did not know whether he would find anyone willing to hear it. The water rose against the posts beneath us, touching each one in turn.

For the first time since leaving Maynila, there was no boat left for me to ask.

* * *

The boy returned while Sulad was scraping dried mud from the bottom of our jar.

He had brought a man with him. The man wore a plain cloth wound twice at his waist and carried a narrow strip of wood. He looked first at the boy, then at me.

“Which one gave the name?”

“I did.”

“Say your mother's name.”

I said it. He made me repeat the part that belonged to her house. His lips moved as if he were measuring it against something he had heard elsewhere.

“Who traveled with you?”

I indicated Sulad.

The man looked at the jar between his feet. “He may remain here. You will come.”

“May he come too?”

“I have been told to bring the person who gave the name.”

Sulad stood. “I can wait. Ask where.”

The man pointed toward a roof farther along the landing, where a woman was dividing rice into small bowls. “There. If you work, speak to her first.”

Sulad lifted the jar.

“You needn't carry it everywhere,” I said.

“Someone must know where it is.”

He went toward the woman without waiting for me. I followed the man and the boy between the houses.

The planks changed under my feet. Some had been worn smooth by passing loads; others were new enough to leave a pale line where a knife had cut them. Below, the water moved against posts dark with growth. We crossed a place where two narrow boats had been drawn beneath the floors. A man stood in one of them holding a basket above his head while someone inside a house decided where to receive it.

The boy went ahead and told us to stop before a door.

The man with the strip of wood entered. I heard him speaking inside, then another voice answer. He came back without the strip.

“Wait here.”

I could see a room beyond the doorway. Mats covered part of its floor. A low tray stood near the far wall, and two men sat beside it, arguing over something I could not see. One of them raised his hand, and the other stopped speaking. Neither looked toward me.

I waited until my feet began to ache.

A woman carrying a covered bowl came out. She stopped beside me.

“Have you eaten?”

“Not today.”

“Then you should have said so at the landing.”

“I was waiting to be received.”

“You can do both.”

She called to the boy. He went away and returned with a smaller bowl. The woman placed it on the boards beside the doorway.

“Eat there. The bowl comes back to this house.”

I crouched. The food was rice with a little fish pressed into it. I ate slowly at first, then more quickly when I realized no one was watching. I kept a piece of fish for Sulad and folded it into a clean corner of my cloth. When I finished, I set the bowl where the woman had told me.

The man with the wooden strip returned. He saw the folded cloth in my hand.

“Put that away.”

“It is food.”

“I can see that. Put it away before you enter.”

I tucked it at my waist.

He led me into the room. The two men by the tray had moved to one side. A third man sat beyond them with his knees drawn up beneath his cloth. His hair had begun to grey at the temples. He was examining a cord from which several small knots hung. He looked at me only after the man who had brought me spoke.

I had imagined I would know him before anyone told me.

The man with the cord asked, “You are Ache?”

“Yes.”

“Whose son?”

I gave my mother's name. He asked me to say it again. This time he did not make me repeat the house.

“Who sent you?”

“My mother allowed me to leave.”

“That was not my question.”

“No one sent me with a message.”

“Did she know where you intended to go?”

“Yes.”

“Did she agree?”

“She said I might cross if I found a way.”

He put the cord on the tray. “Then she did not give you a boat.”

“No.”

“Or men.”

“No.”

“Or a letter.”

I looked at the floor. “No.”

The man beside him shifted as if he had heard enough. The older man did not dismiss me.

“What happened at Maynila?”

I began with the part I knew best: the demands made on my mother's house, the men who had come to speak for another ruler, the way I had been told to wait while older people decided what was safe to say. I named the man who had carried the demands. I did not name the ruler as if I had seen him give the order.

The older man asked where I had stood during one meeting.

“Outside.”

“Then who told you what was said?”

“My mother told me part. I heard part through the wall.”

“Which part did she tell you?”

I had to stop and sort the two. What I remembered hearing had become mixed with what I had been told afterward. I began again, leaving out a sentence I could no longer place.

He listened without helping me.

“And you left before you knew what your mother had agreed?”

“Yes.”

“Did you think she had no reason to remain?”

“I thought she could not leave.”

“That may be true. It is not the same answer.”

I wanted him to ask whether she was safe. I wanted to tell him that I did not know. Instead he asked how many days we had traveled and who had carried us.

I could not count the days without stopping. I described the boats, the work, the place where the jars had been received, and the woman whose wax had been lost. At the wax he interrupted.

“Who paid for it?”

“The receiver paid her for seven. She had loaded eight.”

“Did she ask you for the missing one?”

“No.”

“Did she say it was not your fault?”

“She said what she saw. I held the basket when she told me to. I moved when she told me to.”

He looked toward the two men beside the tray.

“Do you hear? He can tell us what a woman lost. He cannot tell us what became of it.”

My face grew hot. “I searched beneath the boards.”

“I did not say you stole it.”

The room was quiet enough that I could hear water striking the posts below.

He took up the cord again. “Your mother used to count knots with her fingers when she was angry. She would pull them tight until they would not move.”

I looked at his hands.

“She still does,” I said.

He turned the cord once, and for a moment I thought he would ask me another question about her. Instead he handed it to the man nearest him.

“Tell the household that the boy may remain while we learn what has happened. Feed him. Find him a place to sleep.”

I knelt more deeply. “Grandfather.”

He did not correct me.

“I came because Maynila was taken from us.”

“You have told me it is threatened. You have not told me everything that was taken.”

“Then let me tell you.”

“You have been telling me.”

“I need help to return.”

The man with the wooden strip looked at the older man, waiting.

“You have arrived with no message from your mother,” the older man said. “You do not know what terms she has accepted. If I send men now, whom do they help? Her, or a son who left before she finished speaking?”

I had no answer that would not make my mother smaller.

“May I send word to her?”

“You may ask for it to be carried. You will not choose the carrier by pointing at the first boat.”

I bowed my head. The older man told the attendant to bring me back after I had slept.

At the door I turned.

“My companion is waiting by the landing.”

“What is his name?”

“Sulad.”

“Does he serve you?”

“He traveled with me. He works for himself.”

“Then he must be asked where he wishes to stay.”

I had thought the question settled. Here, with a place promised to me and none yet promised to him, it had begun again.

Outside, the woman who had given me the bowl was collecting the empty dishes. I took the fish from my cloth. Its warmth was gone, but it was still clean.

“Where is the place you told my companion to wait?” I asked.

She pointed along the planks. I started toward it, carrying the food in my hand.

* * *

I found Sulad beside the woman who had been dividing rice. He was holding a stack of empty bowls while she scraped the last grains from a pot.

“She told me to wait here,” he said.

“I know.”

“You were gone a long time.”

I held out the fish I had saved. He looked at it, then at the woman. She was counting bowls and did not appear to hear us.

“Did they feed you?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Then eat it.”

“I kept it for you.”

“You have carried it through half the houses on the river.”

“It was wrapped.”

He took it. The fish had left a dark patch on my cloth. He ate a small piece and offered the rest to me. I refused, and he did not offer again.

“Did you see him?” he asked.

I nodded.

“Is he your grandfather?”

“He did not say I wasn't.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

“He knew something about my mother.”

Sulad chewed. The woman beside us put one bowl inside another, hard enough to make me look at her.

“He says I can stay while he finds out what happened,” I said.

“Here?”

“In his household.”

“And me?”

“He asked what you wanted.”

Sulad swallowed before answering. “I told this woman I would wash the bowls and carry water if she would feed me tonight. She said I could sleep beneath the dry side of the roof.”

“You don't have to do that.”

“Someone has to wash them.”

“I could ask him to take you in.”

“You could ask me first.”

The woman lifted the pot from the hearth. Sulad set down the empty bowls and helped her carry it to a place where she could rinse it. I stood with my hands free.

When he came back, I asked whether he would remain near the landing.

“For now. If the work is bad, I can leave it.”

“And if they tell you to go?”

“Then I will have to find somewhere else. So will you, if your grandfather changes his mind.”

He said it without bitterness. That made it harder to answer.

A boy from the receiving house came to fetch me before dark. He led me to a room behind the place where I had met Siripada. Two other young men were already there, unrolling mats. One shifted his mat to make space without asking who I was. The other pointed to a jar of water and told me not to spill it on the sleeping floor.

I thought of Sulad carrying our chipped jar along the landing. I had almost asked him to give it to me before we parted. I had no use for it here.

The room had a shutter that did not close fully. Through the gap I could see a narrow strip of the river. Men were still moving goods after the last light had gone. Someone below us called twice for a missing cord, and a voice farther along the planks answered that it had been put beneath the baskets.

I slept badly. When I woke, the two men had gone. My feet were clean, and for a moment I could not remember why.

The boy brought me to Siripada's room after the household had eaten. There were more people than before. A man stood beside a tray with two covered jars. Another held the end of a cord with knots at uneven distances. They were disputing whether a receiver had taken both jars in good condition.

“Open them,” Siripada said.

The man by the tray did so. One jar smelled of oil. The other smelled of the same oil and something sour beneath it. Siripada bent toward the second mouth, then sat back.

“Who kept it after it left the boat?”

Neither man answered at once.

“You have brought me the jars,” he said. “Bring me the men who touched them.”

The jars were carried out. Siripada rubbed his fingers against the edge of his cloth. Only then did he look at me.

“Did you sleep?”

“Some.”

“You will sleep better tonight.”

“I would rather know what you intend to do.”

He looked toward the doorway where the men had gone. “So would they.”

I had been preparing words while I lay awake. Now I could remember only the part that made me sound like a child.

“My mother is still there,” I said.

“I know where she is.”

“You do not know whether she is safe.”

“Neither do you.”

“I came to ask you to help her.”

“You came without her request.”

“She may not be able to send one.”

“That is possible.”

He said it as if possibility were something that could be placed on the tray and counted. I gripped my knees.

“Then why do nothing?”

He turned toward me. “You have been in my house for one night. You do not know what I have done, or what I have sent, or what word has reached me.”

“Has word reached you?”

“Not enough.”

“Then tell me what you know.”

“Some men speak of trouble at Maynila. Others speak of agreements. A man can call an agreement a surrender when he dislikes it.”

“Someone made demands on my mother's house. I heard them.”

“I believe you heard demands.”

“You believe only the part that changes nothing.”

He studied me. One of the men beside the tray shifted his weight. I had forgotten he was still in the room.

“I believe your mother may have chosen terms you have not heard,” Siripada said. “If I send men because you are frightened, I may break what she has kept standing.”

“And if she had no choice?”

“Then we must learn that before we make her choices worse.”

I wanted him to be cruel enough that I could hate him. Instead he reached for the cord on the tray and found a knot with his thumb.

“I can give you food and a place here,” he said. “I can ask for reliable word. I will not promise you a force to take home.”

“Because you do not believe I am her son?”

“Because being her son does not tell me whom my men would fight, or what your mother would ask of them.”

The answer struck harder than I expected. In Maynila my name had opened doors. It had never been asked to carry an account of every man behind them.

“What am I to do here?” I asked.

“For a start, learn the work you claim to understand.”

“I worked on the boats.”

“I heard.”

He motioned to the man who had stood by the jars.

“The receiving house needs another pair of eyes. Goods come in, men say what they brought, and men say what they received. You will stand with the receivers and learn to tell one account from another.”

“Like a servant?”

“Like someone who can say which jars arrived sound and which did not.”

I stared at the floorboards. The offer was small enough that I could have walked out and called it an insult. There was no boat waiting to carry that insult anywhere.

“Will people there know who I am?”

“They will know your name. They need not hear your claim from you each time a jar arrives.”

“You would have me hide it.”

“I would have you stop using it to settle questions that are not settled.”

I looked up. “You used my mother's name to bring me into this room.”

“Yes,” he said. “And I am responsible for what happens to you in it.”

I had not thought of him as responsible for anything smaller than a city. He had been shown a spoiled jar that morning, and he had not yet decided who owed for it.

“May I send word to her?” I asked.

“Tell me what you would send.”

“That I am alive. That I reached you.”

“Anything else?”

I hesitated. I wanted to tell her I had found help. I had not.

“That I left without knowing what she had agreed. That I am waiting to learn.”

Siripada laid down the cord.

“I will have the message repeated before witnesses. A carrier may take it when we know where it can safely be delivered. I will not say it has gone before it has.”

“Will you tell me when it goes?”

“Yes.”

“And if it never does?”

“Then I will tell you that, too.”

I thought of the fish woman who had promised to speak my name only if the cloth buyer came. Her promise had been narrow. It had carried me farther than the larger ones I had imagined.

“I will work at the receiving house,” I said.

“Until you have learned something useful there.”

“And Sulad?”

“What has he chosen?”

“He is working for his food at the landing.”

“Then let him do it. If he wishes to enter my service, he may ask. Do not ask for him.”

I was dismissed with the same movement of Siripada's hand that had sent the damaged jars away. Outside, the boy who had brought me waited to show me where the receiving men worked.

“Do they know I am coming?” I asked.

“They know someone is coming.”

“Do they know whose grandson?”

He gave me a puzzled look. “They asked whether you can count.”

At the landing I could see Sulad bending over the water with a bowl in his hand. He tipped it, rinsed it, and set it beside the others. Our jar stood upright behind his feet. He saw me watching and raised one wet hand, then returned to the bowl before it could slip.

The boy showed me where I should report at first light. Then I went back to the sleeping room.

* * *

At first light, the boy led me past the room where I had slept and down to a platform covered by a roof of overlapping leaves. The roof ended short of the water. Beneath it, a woman stood with her bare foot on a coil of cord, pulling a knot tight. Three men were unloading bundles from a boat. One carried each bundle up the steps while another caught it at the top. The third stayed in the boat to keep the load from shifting.

“Here is the one they sent,” the boy said.

The woman looked at me without letting go of the cord.

“Can you count?”

“Yes.”

“Then count those.”

She pointed at the bundles already lying beneath the roof. They were wrapped in woven matting and tied at both ends. I counted nine. She made me count them again while she watched the men bring up another two.

“Eleven,” I said.

“How many did you see come off the boat?”

“Two.”

“How many were here when you began?”

“Nine.”

“Who told you there were nine?”

“I counted them.”

“Then that is what you can tell me.”

She drew a knot into the cord and passed it to a man sitting beside a low pile of empty baskets. He had another cord, older and darker, stretched across his knee. The two of them compared the knots without speaking. I had expected someone to tell me what the cargo was worth. No one did.

“Where do I stand?” I asked.

“Where you can see the boat and the roof without standing in the way.”

I moved to the edge of the platform. The boy, satisfied that he had delivered me, went back toward the house.

The boatman called for the next bundle. A man in the boat lifted it and pushed it toward the steps. The man above caught one end, but his heel slipped on the wet plank. He set the bundle down hard. The woman made him lift it again.

“Not by the tie,” she said. “That is what keeps it shut.”

The bundle came up safely. I watched its path and counted aloud. Another followed. Then the boatman said there were no more.

“Thirteen,” I said.

The man with the dark cord looked at me. “You said eleven before the last two.”

“I did.”

He counted his knots and held them up to the woman. She counted the bundles beneath the roof. I counted them too. There were twelve.

“One is missing,” I said.

The man who had carried them up the steps straightened. Sweat had made a dark band across his chest.

“From where?”

“From the boat.”

He looked at the boatman. “He says I lost one.”

“I said there were thirteen,” I answered.

“That is not the same thing.”

I pointed to the bundles. “There are twelve.”

The woman told everyone to stop moving. The boat rocked against the steps. The men waited, and the man in the boat put his hand against the post to keep it from striking the planks.

“Which bundle is missing?” the woman asked me.

I looked at the matting. Each one had been tied in the same way. One had a patch where the weave had broken. Another had a broad dark stain.

“I don't know.”

“Which did you see leave the boat?”

“All of them.”

“Did you watch every one reach this roof?”

I had watched the man's foot slip. I had watched the bundle hit the plank. For a moment after that I had watched the boatman, wondering whether he would blame the man above him. I could not remember where the next bundle had gone.

“Most,” I said.

The woman turned to the carrier. “Show me where you set them.”

He went to the far side of the platform. I followed, feeling the other men watch us. Under the eaves, beyond a post that divided the roof, lay a bundle I had not included. It was covered with an old mat. The carrier pulled the mat aside.

“That one came up while you were watching the boat,” he said. “You told me to keep it dry.”

The woman nodded. “I did.”

I had counted nine before the last four came up. My total of thirteen was right. I had lost sight of one bundle after it reached the platform and accused the man who had carried it.

The carrier put the mat back over the bundle.

“You said I lost it,” he told me.

“I thought—”

“I heard what you thought.”

He returned to the boat. The woman began untying the cord beneath her foot. She did not speak to me until the men had started unloading a second load from a smaller craft.

“When you cannot see a bundle, you have not seen it stolen,” she said.

“I know.”

“You knew that before you came here.”

I had heard my mother say much the same thing to men who brought her reports. Hearing it from someone who had just lost time because of me was different. The woman gave me the loose end of a fresh cord.

“Make a knot when a bundle comes off the boat. Do not make one when you think a bundle ought to be here. I will count what reaches the roof.”

For the rest of the morning I stood at the edge. My feet grew sore. I counted the bundles as they passed and made a knot for each. The cord scraped the tender skin of my thumb. When I reached ten I ran out of space between my hands and had to begin a second length. The woman showed me how to leave a gap so that the lengths would not be confused.

A cloud moved over the river. The light went flat. From the landing came the sound of men calling for mats before rain reached the cargo. The carrier who had spoken to me dragged the covered bundle farther beneath the eaves. I went to help him.

“Take that end,” he said, indicating the side away from the tie.

We moved it together. The mat caught on a rough board. I freed it without pulling the bundle open.

“Here?” I asked.

He looked at the space under the roof.

“Farther. If the wind turns, the water comes in.”

We moved it again. When the rain struck, the outer planks went dark almost at once. The bundle stayed dry. The carrier did not thank me. He went back to the boat and covered the remaining cargo.

I stayed beneath the eaves with the cords. The woman brought her count to the man with the dark cord, and they compared them while the rain made it hard to hear. They disagreed over one bundle. This time I told them which I had seen leave the boat and which had remained there when the men stopped.

The woman went to look for herself.

She returned wet to the knees. “That is right,” she said, and added a knot to her cord.

It was the first time anyone had used my count without asking me to repeat it.

By the time the rain eased, the men had finished. The woman paid the carriers in portions of food set aside beneath a cloth. She counted each portion before handing it over. The man I had accused took his and sat at the far end of the platform, his back against a post.

I went to him.

“I was wrong about the bundle.”

He tore a piece from his food and chewed before answering.

“You were loud about it.”

“Yes.”

“Then be loud when you tell her.”

I went back to the woman. She was drying the cords, stretching them between two posts where air could pass.

“I said he had lost a bundle,” I told her. “He had put it where you told him. I did not look beyond the post.”

She looked toward the carrier. He did not turn.

“I heard you correct yourself,” she said. “Tomorrow, begin with what is here before the first boat comes.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Did you think one morning would teach you everything?”

I had been wondering whether Siripada would call for me after the work. Instead I had been given another morning.

Below the platform I saw Sulad carrying a long pole with another man. They lowered it onto a boat and checked that its ends did not strike the gunwales. Sulad caught sight of me. He lifted his chin toward the cords in my hands.

“Is that your work now?” he called.

“For today.”

“Mine is finished.”

He looked toward the far landing, where the woman who had fed him was speaking with a boatman. He had somewhere to go. I could not leave until the receiving woman had taken back the cords.

“Will I see you tonight?” I asked.

“If you come to the landing.”

“I have a place in the house.”

“I know where it is.”

The man beside Sulad called for him. Sulad went to help push the pole farther into the boat. He did not wait for me to explain the work I had been given.

The woman held out her hand for the cords. I gave them to her one at a time. She checked the knots, then coiled them beneath the dry part of the roof.

“Come before the first load,” she said.

On my way back I passed the doorway where I had first spoken to Siripada. The tray was gone. A man was sweeping the floor, pushing bits of leaf and dust toward the gap between the boards. He moved aside for me, then went on sweeping.

I had crossed the water to find a house that would know my name. At its receiving platform, a man whose name I had not asked had reason to remember mine. I went back to find him before the light failed.

* * *

The carrier was still beneath the roof when I found him.

He had taken the wet mat from the bundle and spread it over two poles. The rain had stopped, but water continued to fall from the eaves. He worked with his back to the river, pulling apart a knot that had tightened in the wet.

I stood far enough away that he could finish.

“You came back,” he said.

“I told the woman I had been wrong.”

“I heard.”

“I wanted to tell you again.”

“You did that too.”

He set the cord across his knee and began working the knot loose with his thumbnail. I had expected him to make me speak until I found the right words. He seemed more concerned with the cord.

“Did they take anything from your food because we stopped?” I asked.

“No.”

“Did they blame you?”

“They asked me where I had put the bundle. I showed them.”

I looked at the old mat. A split ran from its edge almost to the middle. He would have to mend it before it could keep anything dry again.

“I don't know your name,” I said.

He looked up then.

“Daran.”

“I am Ache.”

“I heard that when you were shouting about the bundle.”

I felt my face grow warm.

He pulled the knot free. “If you are here tomorrow, don't stand where the man coming up the steps must walk around you.”

“I will be here.”

“You said that quickly.”

“I have been told to come before the first load.”

“Then you will see how early the first load comes.”

He lifted the mat, shook it once, and moved it farther beneath the roof. I took the other pole when he indicated it. We set the mat where the wind could pass over both sides.

I left him there. He had not forgiven me in any way I could carry back and show Siripada. He had told me where to stand in the morning.

At the landing, Sulad was eating from a leaf beside the woman who had given him work. Our chipped jar stood under the bench, out of the way of passing feet. He moved his leaf so I could sit.

“Did you eat?” he asked.

“At the house.”

“Then you can watch me.”

He had rice and a small piece of fish. I watched him separate the bones with his fingers. A boatman called across the landing for someone to bring a pole. Sulad looked up but did not rise.

“Will you work here tomorrow?” I asked.

“Not for the bowls. A man at the far steps needs two hands to mend a platform. He says he will give me food if the boards hold.”

“Does he know you can do it?”

“He saw me carry the pole today. I told him what I could do. He told me what he needed.”

“Do you know how long it will take?”

“No.”

I had been about to ask whether he would be finished in time to come to the house. He took another bite before I could.

“You can ask me to visit,” he said. “You don't have to ask where I will be every hour.”

“I was going to ask whether you would come.”

“Then ask.”

“Will you?”

“When the work is finished. If they still let me cross the landing.”

“You could stay in the household,” I said.

“You have asked me that already.”

“I know.”

“Has the man who receives the bundles asked you to bring me?”

“No.”

“Then don't promise me his floor.”

He tipped the last grains of rice into his mouth. I wanted to tell him that I could speak to Siripada again, but the woman came to collect the leaf and asked Sulad to move the jar so she could sweep beneath the bench. He did it before she had finished asking.

A boy from the receiving house found me there after dark. He carried a small lamp cupped against the wind.

“They are waiting to hear your words,” he said.

“Who?”

“The men Siripada told to listen.”

I stood so quickly that my knee struck the bench. Sulad steadied the jar.

“Are you going to send a message?” he asked.

“They are going to hear it.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“I don't know who will carry it.”

“Then tell them that.”

The boy waited while I wiped my hands on my cloth. I had nothing on them, but I kept wiping until he turned toward the house.

In the room where Siripada had questioned me, two men sat beside a low tray. One was the man who had carried the narrow strip of wood when I first arrived. The other I had not seen before. Siripada was absent.

“Say whom the message is for,” the man with the strip told me.

I named my mother and her household at Maynila.

“Who is sending it?”

“Ache, her son.”

“Say what you want carried.”

I had rehearsed a much longer account on the walk from the landing. I wanted her to know the names of every place where we had worked, what we had lost, how close I had come to being turned away. I wanted her to hear that Siripada had received me. I wanted her to hear it as a promise that he would do more.

“I am alive,” I began. “I reached Brunei. Siripada has given me shelter.”

The man repeated each sentence. The other listened with his head bent. When I said Siripada's name, neither corrected me.

“I came without knowing what terms my mother had accepted,” I continued. “I have asked for help. No force has been promised.”

The man with the strip looked up.

“Do you want those last words carried?”

“Yes.”

“You can say less.”

“I know.”

He repeated the words, and I heard how they might sound in Maynila. I had crossed so much water to ask for an army. I was sending back an account of a room and a place to sleep.

“Anything else?” he asked.

“Tell her Sulad came with me. He is alive. He has found work.”

“Does he ask you to send that?”

I stopped.

“No. I haven't asked him.”

The man waited. He did not offer to decide for me.

“Leave that part out,” I said. “I will ask him.”

The other witness repeated the message from the beginning. He got my mother's household wrong. I corrected it. He began again, and this time the names and the words were as I had given them.

“Who will carry it?” I asked.

“We have no carrier named for it.”

“Then it has not gone.”

“No.”

“Will you tell Siripada that I came?”

“He ordered us to hear you. We will tell him what we heard.”

I could have asked to see him. I had asked him enough for one day. The two men remained at the tray while I left.

Sulad was still awake at the landing, lying on a mat beneath the dry side of the roof. The jar stood near his head. He sat up when he heard me.

“Did they send it?”

“They heard it. They haven't found a carrier.”

“What did you say?”

I told him. He listened until I had finished.

“You left me out.”

“They asked whether you wanted me to speak for you.”

He looked toward the dark water. “I would have told your mother I was alive.”

“Do you want me to add it?”

“Yes. But say it is from me.”

“I will go back in the morning.”

“You'll be at the bundles in the morning.”

“I can speak to them afterward.”

He lay down again. I remained seated beside him. A lamp passed between two houses on the opposite bank, disappeared behind a post, and appeared once more.

“Are you staying?” he asked.

“For now.”

“Because he told you to?”

“Because I asked him for help, and I have nothing more useful to bring him yet.”

“That sounds like something you learned to say in his room.”

I turned toward him. His face was in shadow.

“I am staying,” I said. “Even if he never sends men.”

Sulad was quiet for so long that I thought he had gone to sleep.

“Then come to the landing when you can,” he said. “Don't wait until you need something.”

I went back to the sleeping room before the household closed the walkway. The two young men were already on their mats. I lay awake listening to the water against the posts. I had given the witnesses words for my mother. I could not tell her when I would return.

Before the light reached the river, I woke and went to the receiving platform. Daran was there with the woman and the older man who kept the dark cord. No boat had yet touched the steps.

“You're early,” Daran said.

“Not earlier than you.”

He handed me an empty length of cord.

“Stand there,” he said, pointing to a place where I could see the boat without blocking the steps.

I went where he pointed. Farther along the landing, Sulad and another man were lifting a loose board from a platform. Sulad saw me and raised a hand. Then he bent to the board again.

The first boat came alongside. Its owner called the number of bundles he expected us to take. I listened, but I did not make a knot.

When the first bundle left the boat, I made one.
