Ula had put the silver under a broken shell.

I knew because I could see the shell from the doorway, its pale inside turned toward the light, and the corner of my silver showing beneath it. Yesterday I had left the piece beside her things as if a payment could make a road between Maynila and a man I did not know how to find.

“You came early,” she said.

“I thought your friend might have gone.”

“She is not my friend.”

I stood with one foot on the packed earth outside. Ula was threading small shells onto a cord. She held the cord against her knee while she worked, pinching each shell between her nails before passing it along. She did not invite me in.

“The woman whose brother carries goods upriver,” I said.

“I know which woman. I told you I would ask her.”

“Have you?”

“I have asked her.”

“And?”

“She asked what I wanted.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That I had a question for a man who might know another man.”

I felt my mouth tighten. “You could have told her his name.”

“You never gave me his name.”

I had described the captain from the upriver landing: the Malay he spoke, the way he listened before answering, the men near him who had watched the boats instead of me. I had not thought to ask what he was called. I remembered how pleased I had been to understand enough of his words to answer. I remembered less clearly what I had said in return.

“Could she take a message without it?”

“She could take a message. To whom?”

“To the captain.”

“Which captain?”

“The one I met.”

Ula let the cord fall across her lap.

I heard myself. If Sulad had spoken that way about a fish seller, I would have laughed at him.

“Then ask her brother to inquire at the landing,” I said. “He might know the men who were there.”

“He might. He is not here.”

“When will he return?”

“She does not know.”

“But he carries goods.”

“He carries them when there are goods to carry. He has a wife and two children. He is not waiting upriver for you to remember a man's name.”

The words stung more because Ula said them without anger. She moved another shell along the cord and checked it against the others.

“I could pay him,” I said.

“For going?”

“For asking.”

“And if he asks three men and none knows yours?”

“I would still pay him for his time.”

“Would you?” She looked up. “You left one piece of silver and did not tell me what you expected it to buy.”

I looked at the shell on the floor.

“Take it back,” she said.

“I gave it to you.”

“You left it. There is a difference.”

I crossed the threshold and picked up the silver. It was warm where the sun had reached the shell. I wanted to set it down again and tell her she was making too much of a small thing. Instead I closed my fingers around it.

“What would be fair?” I asked.

“That depends on the errand. First find out whether the brother will ask. Then what he wants for asking. Then how you will hear his answer. I will not promise him your money.”

“Could you ask the woman to send word when he returns?”

“I can do that.”

“What will you tell her?”

Ula drew the cord taut. “That a young man in Maynila is trying to find a captain he met once and was too proud to question properly.”

“That will make him eager to help.”

“It may make him remember you.”

I laughed, though I did not want to. Ula's mouth moved at one corner.

She laid the finished cord beside the broken shell. “There is something else. If the brother finds this man, what do you want him to say?”

“That I need passage to Brunei.”

“On what boat? With what cargo? At what price?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Then you have two questions, not one.”

I sat at the threshold where I could see the river through the gap between houses. Men were lifting baskets from a boat onto the bank. One basket split at its seam, and its owner caught it before the contents fell. No one stopped to look at me.

“Tell him I want to speak with the captain,” I said at last. “Only that. If he can find him, ask where and when. If he cannot, I will pay for the asking, provided we agree on the price first.”

Ula nodded once. It was the first answer I had given that she did not have to pull apart.

“And if he says no?” she asked.

“Then I have to find another way.”

“Good. Now you have a message.”

I turned the silver over in my palm. It had been easier to imagine a voyage than to arrange the first question.

Outside, someone shouted for another basket. Ula returned to her shells. I stayed long enough to watch her tie the cord, then stood.

“Will you tell me when she answers?”

“If she answers, yes.”

I wanted to ask how long that would take. I had learned enough that morning not to ask her to know what no one had promised.

On the fourth morning, Ula sent a child to find me.

The child did not know why. He had been told to say that a man had come home and that I should bring my question, not my temper. I gave him a piece of fruit for the errand. He bit it before he was out of sight.

By the time I reached Ula's house, the man was gone again.

“He went to the landing,” she said.

“Did he find the captain?”

“He found a man who had heard of men like the one you described.”

“That could be anyone.”

“Yes.”

“Then why send for me?”

“Because he has something else to tell you, and I would rather you hear it from him.”

She returned to the work spread across her mat. She had made me wait four days without once pretending that she could make the river move faster. I had tried twice to come by. Both times she had been elsewhere. I had begun to suspect she was avoiding me, which was a convenient suspicion: it spared me the trouble of wondering why she should be available whenever I wanted her.

At the landing, men were passing baskets from a boat to a row of buyers. One of the baskets had been patched with a strip of pale reed. I watched it go from one pair of hands to another, each man shifting his grip before the next took the weight. Nothing fell.

The brother was standing by a stack of jars. He was broad across the shoulders, with a cut near one thumbnail. I knew him only because he turned when I asked for the woman who had spoken to Ula.

“You are the boy with the captain,” he said.

“I am looking for a captain.”

“That is what I said.”

“Have you seen him?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“I asked two men at the upriver landing. One had heard a Malay speaker bargaining there some days ago. The other had not. Neither could tell me where he had gone.”

He held out his hand.

I stared at it. “For what?”

“For asking.”

“I told Ula we had to agree on the price before anyone went. She said she wouldn't promise you my money.”

“She didn't. I was going upriver with goods. I asked while I was there. You can keep your silver.”

He had done the work without a bargain. I could refuse him; he could refuse the next errand. The silver lay against my skin where I had carried it from home.

“How much would you take?”

He named a smaller amount than I had feared and a larger one than I liked. We settled it without calling witnesses. He counted the payment twice, then tucked it away.

“That was an honest answer,” I said.

“It was an honest errand.”

He turned back to the jars. I could have left with the same captain I had begun with: one whose face I remembered better than his name, and whose whereabouts no one knew. Instead I stayed.

“Ula said you had something else.”

“I told her there is a trader taking goods downriver and beyond. He has spoken with men who travel farther than I do.”

“To Brunei?”

“Not that I know. If you ask him that first, he will say no and finish with you.”

“Why?”

“Because you will sound like a man asking a fisherman to bring him a mountain.”

I wanted to ask whether everyone who worked the river had agreed to speak to me as Ula did. But the brother had already given me what I paid for. He owed me nothing more.

“Where is the trader?”

The brother pointed toward the boat with the patched basket. A man near its stern was making a mark on a slat of wood while another counted jars aloud. He had a narrow face and a gray patch in his beard. His attention stayed on the cargo as I approached.

“I hear you carry goods beyond the river,” I said.

“Sometimes.”

“I want to travel.”

“Everyone wants to travel when the price is someone else's.”

“I can pay.”

“Then tell me where.”

I looked at the baskets. “South, toward the traders who go on to Brunei.”

The man put down the slat.

“Those are two different requests. I can tell you what I carry and where I intend to stop. I cannot sell you the rest of a journey I will not make.”

“Where will you stop?”

“At the next place where these goods have a buyer. Perhaps farther, if the price is poor.”

“That is not a place.”

“It is the answer I have.”

He lifted the nearest basket, judged its weight and set it beside another. I had thought the voyage would begin with a name on a shore. To him it began with what he could sell there.

“Could I come with you until you stop?” I asked.

“How many?”

“One. Perhaps two.”

“Perhaps?”

“The other has not decided.”

“Then you have not decided what you are buying.”

“I know what I want.”

“I am sure you do.” He called to a man at the bow to move a jar away from the wet edge of the planks. When the jar was secure, he looked at me again. “If I take a passenger, his food comes from somewhere. His water takes space. If he is sick, someone must tend him. If he knows nothing of boats, someone must keep him out of the way. If he can work, I need to know what he can do.”

“I can work.”

“At what?”

I remembered the patched sail, the line I had pulled when Sulad told me to let it go, and the man who had been hurt when I refused to listen.

“I can carry baskets,” I said.

“So can the men who already know where to put them.”

“I can learn.”

“You can learn on your own boat.”

The brother, who had followed me without speaking, made a sound that might have been a cough. I did not look at him.

“I have silver,” I said.

The trader named a price for one passenger to his first stop, with food and water supplied separately. It was more than I had left after paying the brother, and it bought no promise of onward passage. For a second person he named another price, not quite double. He would not hold space while I went away to think.

“I could bring provisions,” I said.

“Whose?”

“From our household.”

He looked at my clothes, then at the brother. “Ah.”

I wished he had not understood.

“Your household can spare food?” he asked.

“I would have to ask.”

“Ask, then. Do not bring it here and tell me afterward that your mother wants it back.”

“She is not likely to do that.”

“I do not know your mother.”

“Most people here do.”

He glanced at me with a new care. I had wanted my name to open a door. Now I could see him measuring what trouble it might bring onto his boat.

“I carry goods for men who expect to be paid,” he said. “I will not take a quarrel with a ruler along with their baskets.”

“I am not asking you to.”

“Then do not offer what is not yours.”

The brother moved a jar into the shade. No one had told him to. The trader checked that it stood level, then nodded his thanks.

“What if I pay you now?” I asked.

“For what?”

“To keep a place.”

“There is no place to keep until I know the cargo, the water, the people and the day I leave. Even then I may need the space for goods.”

“Then how do I find you again?”

“Ask at this landing. If I am gone, I am gone.”

He bent to his slat. I had been dismissed without being told to go.

The brother walked beside me as far as the first houses. He did not offer to carry another message.

“You should have told him who you were at the beginning,” I said.

“I told him you were looking for a captain.”

“He might have spoken differently.”

“He did speak differently when he guessed.”

I thought of the care in the trader's eyes. It had not been respect.

“Would you go on that boat?” I asked.

“With those goods? If he paid me to carry them, perhaps. Not for the price he gave you.”

“Why not?”

“Because I have somewhere to be when it comes back.”

He left me there. I watched him disappear into the traffic of baskets and jars, returning to work that had waited while he answered my question.

The silver I had left would not buy even one place beside the baskets. I counted it again anyway, then put it away and took the path home. Behind me the trader called for another jar. His men carried it aboard without looking up.

My mother was counting jars.

The steward brought them into the shade one by one. He took off each cover, waited while she inspected the grain, then put it back. One jar had a dark line down its side. When he tilted it, a few grains caught in the crack.

“Bind it,” she said, “but keep it away from the sound ones.”

He set it aside and made a mark on a board.

I had practiced what to say on the way home. It sounded less convincing beside the jars.

“I found a man who may carry me south.”

“May?”

“He has a boat and goods to sell. He offered a price for a passenger to his first buyer.”

She looked up. “To Brunei?”

“No. He knows men who trade farther on. He has not offered to introduce me.”

“Does he know the captain you met upriver?”

“No one I asked has found him.”

She motioned for the next jar. I gave her the fare the trader had named, the extra price for a second passenger, and his condition that we bring our own food and water. I had been tempted to leave out the last part. I said it while the steward was listening.

“Can we spare any grain?” I asked.

“From which jar?”

I gestured toward the row.

“That one belongs to a merchant,” she said. “Those two have been promised upriver. The jars by the door are ours, but they have already been counted for the households that depend on them.”

“You could change the count.”

“Yes,” she said. “And then someone else would discover the difference.”

The steward waited with his board raised. My mother looked at him.

“What have I forgotten?”

“The merchants you asked to bring offers for boatmen. Two have stopped trading until you approve their crews.”

“I told them to bring me prices, not ask my permission.”

“They heard both.”

She rubbed the bridge of her nose. I had watched men carry her words away as if they were baskets with the bottoms tied shut. This time something had spilled.

“Tell them they may hire whom they choose,” she said. “I wanted to know what a crew costs when its men are paid. I did not forbid their boats.”

“And the organizer's men?”

“I have not seen their debts. Do not promise they are free of them.”

The steward made another mark.

“There is a young rower outside,” he said. “He says Sima paid the organizer for the first crossing, but he still has no wages. He wants work.”

“Bring him in.”

I knew the rower by a pale mark across the back of his hand. He stood with his eyes on the floor while my mother asked what he was owed.

“The organizer says the woman paid him,” he answered.

“That is what she says too,” my mother said. “I cannot make your old wage appear by paying you for a different task.”

“I know. I need work now.”

She offered him the job of moving the jars. The steward would name the pay before he began and mark it separately under his name. The rower asked whether the organizer might claim that accepting new work settled the old debt.

“He may claim it,” she said. “Our record will say otherwise.”

“Will I be paid today?”

“Yes.”

He agreed. The steward led him outside to inspect the cracked jar before lifting it.

I had come hoping my mother would settle my journey as quickly.

She crossed to a chest and lifted a wooden box from beneath folded cloth. A bent loop of gold hung from a newer cord inside. She held it by the break.

“Your father wore this,” she said.

“I remember.”

“You tried to put your finger through it.”

“I remember you telling me not to pull.”

“He was the one who broke it. Took it off to enter the water, then stepped on it himself. He blamed the cord.”

She smiled at the loop, not at me. Beneath it lay two small pieces of gold wrapped separately. She let me see them, then placed the lid beside the box.

“These are mine,” she said. “The grain is not.”

“Would one pay the fare?”

“I do not know. Nor do you, until the trader finishes loading.”

“He may leave before I can find out.”

“Then he leaves.”

I looked toward the door. The steward and rower were testing the weight of the cracked jar. They lowered it again when the binding shifted. No one shouted at them.

“What must I bring you?” I asked.

“The first landing he intends to use. What food you need. How you will send word from there. Whether he has room when his buyers have finished. And a decision from Sulad, not one you make for him.”

“Bulkan knows I want to go.”

“He knows,” she said. “I need him here. He cannot be your answer to every danger you have not imagined.”

I had thought of Bulkan following if I sent for him. I had not asked what he would leave behind.

She wrapped the gold again. “I would rather you stayed.”

I had prepared myself for an argument. There was nothing to argue with in the way she said it.

“Then why show me?”

“Because I may still help you go. But I will know what I am giving before I give it.”

She put the box away.

Outside, the rower and steward lifted together. They took two short steps, stopped to change their grip, and continued. I waited until they had cleared the doorway before leaving. I still had no gold and no place on a boat. I had a list of questions I could no longer pretend someone else had answered.

Sulad was bringing in the net when I found him. His uncle stood on the bank with one end wrapped around his wrist, taking the weight a little at a time. The wet mesh dragged over the stones.

I caught the loose edge and pulled. Mud came up with it.

“Leave that,” Sulad said.

“I can help.”

“Then hold the basket.”

I reached for the basket as he shook the net. A fish struck its rim, slipped past my hand and flashed once in the shallows. I lunged after it and came up with a fistful of grit.

Sulad looked at the water. His uncle looked at me.

“I'll pay for it,” I said.

“With whose fish?” the uncle asked.

“With silver.”

“We cannot put silver in tonight's pot.”

He returned to the net. I set the basket farther from the water and held it still while they finished. They needed no instruction from me. Once I learned where to stand, they stopped having to look at me.

When the last weight was ashore, I told Sulad about the trader. I had found a man who might take passengers south with his cargo, though he had not promised a place and had not named his first buyer.

“Have you found the captain you wanted?” Sulad asked.

“No.”

“Then you have found another question.”

His uncle sat beside a torn section of net. “You want my nephew on a boat that may not take him.”

“I want him to decide whether he wants to ask.”

“He has spoken to me already.”

I looked at Sulad. He bent to pick a knot apart and would not meet my eyes.

“If he goes,” his uncle said, “the catch will come in with fewer hands. His cousins have their own boats. Do you know which mornings they can spare?”

“No.”

“Neither do I. That is what he must settle here.”

“I could hire someone.”

“You cannot hire a morning that has already passed.”

Sulad handed his uncle a length of fiber. “I haven't agreed to go. I said I wanted to find out what it would cost.”

“Then ask the trader yourself,” I said.

“I will. I want to know whether he charges me as a passenger and works me as crew. And whether he leaves us where he says he will.”

“I can ask those things.”

“He can answer me.”

The uncle gave the net a hard pull. A knot held; the next one opened. He set it back in Sulad's hands.

“You have time to mend that before you leave,” he said.

“Perhaps I won't leave.”

“Then it still needs mending.”

I stayed beside the basket while Sulad worked. He had tucked his cord beneath his cloth; I saw it only when he reached across the net. I wanted to touch his hand, and found myself gripping the basket harder instead.

“You said you wanted me with you,” he murmured.

“I do.”

“You were pleased before I answered.”

“I know.”

His fingers brushed mine when he took the basket. He might have meant it. He might only have needed the handle.

“Come tomorrow,” he said. “Help us bring in a fish to replace the one you lost. If you're still here.”

“I'll come.”

I meant it when I said it. By the time I reached the path, I was already calculating how early I could see the trader first.

I went to the trader before I went to the fishing bank. I knew what that meant for the fish I had promised Sulad I would help catch.

The trader had a basket turned on its side. Water was seeping through a pale repair in the reeds. He pressed it with his thumb, then told a crewman to move it into shade.

“My mother wants to know where you will put me ashore,” I said.

“She is right to ask.”

“Do you know now?”

“A buyer sent word. I mean to stop at his landing first. If he refuses the goods, I may go farther.”

He named the place and the bank where he intended to bring the boat in. I repeated both. He waited while I did, as though testing whether I had come to buy a journey or merely to say Brunei again.

“Will one of your men carry a message back to Maynila?”

“No. You will have to find someone there.”

“And the food?”

“Your own. The crew's pot is for the crew.”

He pointed to the water jars. Each had to be carried aboard; each took room that might otherwise hold cargo. If a passenger broke one, the cost was his. I asked about a second traveler who could mend nets and knew fishing boats.

“Let him ask me,” the trader said. “I have not seen his hands.”

“He has better hands than mine.”

“Then bring him. I still cannot promise space until my buyers have inspected the cargo tomorrow.”

At the fishing bank, the catch had already come in. Sulad was rinsing the weights. His uncle held out a broken cord and showed me where it passed through the net. My first knot slipped.

“You said you would come for the fish,” Sulad said.

“I came.”

“After the fish.”

I tried the knot again. It held this time, though neither of them praised me for it.

“The trader has named his first landing,” I said. “He will speak to you about work.”

“He can speak to me about passage,” Sulad answered. “Work is another bargain.”

His uncle set down the net. “Go and hear the man. You can mend the rest when you return.”

Sulad went without waiting for me to lead.

At the landing the trader was counting jars. He looked at Sulad's hands, then asked what he knew.

“Fishing boats. Nets. Lines that need replacing before they part,” Sulad said. “Not your cargo, and not your boat.”

The trader nodded at the admission. “If I take you, you will help carry water and move your own things. My men will tell you where to stand.”

“And if you ask me to carry goods for a buyer?” Sulad asked.

“Then we will discuss the work.”

“Before I do it. I won't pay as a passenger and then be called crew whenever you need another pair of hands.”

A crewman holding a jar stopped to listen. The trader made him set it down.

“You take up room whether you work or not,” the trader said.

“Then charge me for the room. Don't sell my labor twice.”

I wanted to speak for Sulad. I knew the trader could decide there was room for only one of us. Sulad had not looked toward me once.

“The fare is to my first landing,” the trader said. “Your own food. No onward promise. Come after the buyers have seen the cargo tomorrow, if you still want to ask.”

“And if there's room for only one?” I asked.

Sulad turned to me. “You can decide whether to go without me.”

“Would you stay?”

“I haven't decided to leave.”

On the walk back, I asked if he thought the trader fair.

“I think he told us what he wants,” Sulad said. “That is more than some men do.”

“You could have told him I would pay the difference.”

“I have money of my own. You don't know the difference yet.”

At the fishing bank his uncle was waiting beside the net. Sulad went to him, not to me. I stayed long enough to pull one knot tight, then left for my mother's house with a landing name, a price I could not pay, and no right to tell her Sulad was coming.

Bulkan was at the door when I returned.

He had a length of cord in his hands. He was tying it into loops, then pulling them apart and starting again. I thought he was mending something until I saw there was nothing at either end.

“Your mother is with the steward,” he said.

“I have news.”

“Then give it to her. She has had enough of mine.”

He put the cord away. I followed him inside.

The cracked jar was gone. In its place stood two smaller jars with fresh bindings around their necks. My mother was speaking to the steward about which men would carry them upriver. She stopped when she saw me, but did not send him away.

“The trader knows his first landing,” I said.

“Does he have room?”

“Not yet. His buyers must see the cargo tomorrow.”

“Then what have you brought me?”

I gave her the name the trader had told me, which bank he intended to approach, and the terms for food and water. I told her he would not send a crewman back with a message. If I needed word carried home, I would have to arrange it at the landing.

The steward listened without lifting his eyes from the jars.

“And Sulad?” my mother asked.

“He spoke to the trader himself.”

“What did he say?”

“That he would pay to travel, if he chose to go. He would help with what passengers must do, but he would not be called crew when the trader needed hands and a passenger when he wanted silver.”

Bulkan looked up.

“He said that to the trader?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What did the man say?”

“He counted his jars.”

Bulkan laughed once. My mother did not.

“You have been speaking for Sulad since you were both small,” she said. “It seems he has found a way to answer without you.”

“I did not tell him what to say.”

“I know. You would have offered the man more.”

I had no defense against that, because I had nearly done it.

The steward cleared his throat. “The merchants sent their boats this morning. They say they are free to hire whom they choose.”

“Good,” my mother said.

“One of them asks whether that means the organizer must release the men who owe him work.”

“Did you tell him it does?”

“No. I told him you had spoken about hiring, not about the debts.”

She nodded. I saw how tired she was by the care she took before speaking.

“Tell him that again,” she said. “And bring me the organizer's account if he claims the men cannot leave. I will not settle a debt I have not seen by declaring it gone.”

The steward bowed and left. I had come to ask for a piece of gold. I watched a question about men's freedom go out the door ahead of mine.

Bulkan stayed.

“You have the landing,” my mother said. “You have the food you must carry. You do not yet have a boat that will take you. How much silver remains?”

I showed her.

She looked at the silver without touching it. “And how much did you spend finding the captain?”

“I did not find him.”

“That was not my question.”

I told her about the man who had asked at the upriver landing and been paid for an honest failure. She asked what he had promised before taking the money. I said he had promised to ask two men, and had asked them.

“Then that was a bargain kept,” she said.

“It brought me no captain.”

“It brought you an answer.”

She went to the chest and took out the wooden box. Bulkan turned toward the doorway, but she called him back.

“You should hear this,” she said.

She unwrapped the two small pieces of gold. The broken loop remained on its cord beside them.

“This belongs to me,” she told Bulkan. “I will give it to Ache. No grain from the merchants, no boatmen ordered from their work, no guards taken from the landing.”

Bulkan folded his arms. “And if he does not come back?”

“Then he will have spent it.”

“I meant him.”

My mother set the gold down. Neither answered at once.

“I know what you meant,” she said.

Bulkan looked at me. “I cannot go. There are men on the river who listen to me because I have stood beside them. Your mother needs that now. If you send word that you are in trouble, I may not be able to leave even then.”

“I have not asked you to.”

“You were going to.”

He was right. I had kept his name in my mind as a man who could be sent after me if the journey went badly. I had not considered what he would have to abandon to come.

My mother pushed one wrapped piece across the floor toward me. She kept the other in the box.

“Take this to learn whether the trader will carry you,” she said. “Do not hand it over to hold a place he has not offered. If he agrees to a fare and you know how many will travel, return with the amount. Then we will see what else I can spare.”

“Will it be enough?”

“I do not know.”

I wanted her to say it would. I put the wrapped piece in my palm and felt how little it weighed.

“If Sulad stays,” she said, “you may still go. But do not say afterward that he failed you. And if he goes, do not spend his consent as though it were yours.”

Bulkan picked up the cord again. “You will need to ask who takes the watch when the others sleep,” he said. “And who decides where the passengers sit when the cargo gets wet.”

“Will you come with me to ask?”

“No. You will ask.”

He resumed working the loops, pulling one through another until they held. I thought he might show me the knot. He did not.

I left with a piece of my mother's gold and no claim on anyone else's hands. I had imagined that receiving it would make me ready. Outside, the landing was still full of people whose work could not be hurried because I wanted to leave.

The next morning, Sulad's uncle had the repaired net laid out on the bank. A cousin was helping him check the weights. Sulad stood apart with a cloth bundle in his hand.

“You came back,” he said.

“I said I would.”

“Yesterday. For the fish.”

“I came after the catch. I know.”

“And today?”

“I came to speak to you first.”

“I remember.”

His uncle called for the cousin to pull the far edge of the net. It lifted evenly, with no new tear. He inspected it once more and nodded.

“Come here,” he said to Sulad.

Sulad went. I stayed where I was.

“You've shown your cousin where the weak part is?” the uncle asked.

“Yes.”

“And told him which mornings you usually go out?”

“He knows.”

“That doesn't mean he'll come every morning.”

“I know.”

The uncle looked at the bundle. “You have money saved.”

“From carrying fish for the sellers. My share.”

“I know whose money it is. I want to know whether you understand what it won't buy.”

Sulad glanced at me, then answered his uncle. “It won't buy you another pair of hands. It may not buy me a place on the boat either.”

“Then why go?”

“Because I want to.”

He said it without raising his voice. I had waited days to hear him choose. When he did, he was looking at someone else.

His uncle lifted the net onto his shoulder. “I won't say I sent you. If you come home, you'll find work here. If you don't, we'll learn to fish without you.”

Sulad stepped forward to take the other end, but his uncle shook his head. The cousin lifted it instead. The two of them carried the net down to the boat.

For a moment Sulad looked as though he might follow. Then he turned to me.

“I want to go,” he said. “I haven't agreed to go wherever you decide after the first landing.”

“I know.”

“And if I want to find a boat home?”

“I won't stop you looking.”

“You cannot promise there will be one.”

“No.”

He tucked the bundle beneath his cloth. I wanted to tell him I would make the journey worth what he was leaving. I could not say what that meant, much less how to do it.

At the landing, the trader was counting the jars that remained. A local buyer had already taken several jars. The basket with the pale patch stood beside the cargo bound for the trader's buyer at the first landing, still waiting to be loaded.

“Two asking?” the trader said when he saw us.

“Two, if there's room,” Sulad answered.

“The buyer has finished with the jars. I may have space when the rest is aboard.”

“You said we could ask about fares today.”

“You can ask.”

Sulad opened his bundle. He asked the price to the first landing, repeated the food and water terms, and made the trader say aloud that a passenger's ordinary help did not make him an unpaid crewman. The trader agreed, impatient to return to his cargo.

Sulad counted his silver once, then folded the cloth.

“Not enough?” I asked.

“Not enough.”

I reached toward the gold my mother had given me. Sulad caught the movement.

“Don't spend it before we know what it will buy.”

“I could cover the difference.”

“Then say what you expect back.”

“Nothing.”

“You don't know what you expect yet. Ask your mother what she can spare, and tell her why. I'll wait here.”

“And if the trader fills the space?”

“Then we find another boat.”

I left him beside the baskets, holding his own money and his own decision. On the path home I passed the fishing bank. Sulad's uncle and cousin were pushing the boat out together. They did not look toward me.

My mother had put away the counting board.

I found her sitting on the floor with a strip of cloth across her knees. One edge had been torn and she was turning it under with her thumb. She had made three stitches that did not lie straight. I could see where she had taken one out and tried again.

“The trader has room?” she asked.

“He may. A buyer here in Maynila has taken some of the jars. The rest are still bound for the landing he named.”

“May.”

“He will take payment when the loading is finished.”

“And Sulad?”

“He wants to go. He spoke to his uncle. He has his own money, but it is short of the fare.”

She set down the cloth. “What did you promise him?”

“Nothing.”

“You have promised him nothing at all?”

“I told him I would not prevent him finding a boat home. I did not promise there would be one.”

My mother looked at the crooked stitches. “That is less than you used to promise.”

“He knows what the trader charges. He knows it is only to the first landing.”

“And he knows what he will do there?”

“He will decide when we arrive.”

She put the needle down carefully, though the point had already caught in the cloth.

“You are asking me for the rest of the gold,” she said.

“Yes.”

“To pay for another man's journey.”

“To pay for two passages. Sulad will put in what he has. I will put in what I have. We cannot say what the trader will take until we offer it.”

She stood, went to the chest and brought out the box. The broken loop lay where she had left it. She touched the cord but did not lift it.

“When your father went away, he said he would return before the rains had done their worst,” she said. “I told everyone he had promised me that.”

“Hadn't he?”

“He said he would try.”

I did not know what to do with the difference.

“I made him sound more certain than he was,” she said. “It was easier to hear myself say it.”

She took the second piece of gold from the box and held it beside the first, which I had unwrapped from my cloth.

“This is what I can spare from what is mine,” she said. “If it buys the first leg, you will still need food and a way onward. If it does not, you will have to come back.”

“I know.”

“You know what I have told you. You do not yet know what it feels like to have spent it.”

She put the gold in my hand. For a moment her fingers closed over mine. Then she released it.

“Does Sulad know you intend to use this for him?”

“He knows I came to ask.”

“Then tell him exactly what you offer. He can refuse. And do not let the trader count his work twice.”

I waited for another condition. Instead she picked up the torn cloth.

“Will you come to the landing?” I asked.

“I have men waiting to speak about the organizer's debts. If I go to see you off, they will think I have decided those matters can wait.”

“Have you?”

“No.”

I wanted to be angry that she would stay with her accounts. I had asked her not to make the city pay for my departure. Now I was discovering that I wanted her to leave the city for an hour to watch me go.

She drew the needle through the cloth. The stitch was still crooked.

At the landing, Sulad was standing where I had left him. He had kept his bundle closed. The trader's crew had finished moving the jars and were testing the baskets that remained. The patched one stood beside the other baskets, its pale binding still visible. I felt an unreasonable pang at the thought of watching it leave without us, as though the basket itself had been waiting for me.

“I have enough to offer for two,” I told Sulad.

“From your mother?”

“From what belongs to her. She said you must know before I spend it.”

“Does she want me to watch you?”

“She wants me to listen when you say no.”

Sulad gave a short laugh. “That will be a difficult journey for you.”

“I know.”

He opened his bundle and showed me the silver. I laid the gold beside it on the cloth, keeping both pieces separate.

“You can keep your money,” I said.

“No. I said I would pay what I can. If you give the rest, I will know what it cost you. I will not owe you my answer when we reach the first landing.”

“You never did.”

“Then say it where the trader can hear.”

We approached together. The trader looked at the gold, then at Sulad's silver. He took his time, rubbing a mark from one piece with his thumb. He asked whether the gold was ours to spend. I said it was my mother's, given to me. He asked whether she would demand it back. I said no, and wished he had not asked.

He named the fare again. Sulad repeated the terms: two passengers, their own food, no claim on the crew's stores, no work beyond what passengers were expected to do, and no promise beyond the first landing. The trader agreed to those terms in front of the men carrying his cargo.

“And if there is no room after you finish?” I asked.

“There is room now,” he said. “If you are paying, pay.”

He took the agreed gold and silver. Sulad counted what remained in his cloth. I had very little left in mine.

“We leave after the last goods are secured,” the trader said. “At first light, if the water and the men allow it. If we cannot leave then, you will wait. You will not order my crew to hurry.”

“I won't.”

He pointed to a place on the planks away from the baskets. “Put your food there before dawn. Keep it dry. And if one of my men tells you to move, move.”

Sulad nodded. I nodded a moment later.

We had paid for two places. I had imagined I would feel taller. Instead I felt the space where the gold had been, and heard my mother's needle pulling through the cloth long after I could no longer see her.

Before dawn I could hear the landing from the path.

Someone was dragging a basket across planks. A man called for a light, then cursed because the light had been put where he could not reach it. The boat was still tied to shore. I was relieved to see it and ashamed of the relief.

I had brought food wrapped against the damp and a jar small enough to lift myself. The food had come from my mother's personal share, not from the merchants' stores. I had asked twice before taking it. The second time she told me that if I asked again she would begin to think I wanted her to refuse.

She had not come to the landing. I had left while she was still speaking with the steward.

Sulad stood beside the place the trader had shown us. His bundle was open. He was moving its contents into a different order, the heavier things beneath the cloth he would need first.

“You're early,” I said.

“I'm here.”

“Did you sleep?”

“A little. You?”

“I kept thinking of the gold.”

“You gave it to the trader yesterday.”

“I know where it is.”

“No, you don't.”

I put my bundle beside his. It was larger than I had expected it to look. I had thought leaving would make me feel light. Instead I was carrying food, spare cloth and a jar that knocked against my knee each time I moved.

The trader came down the planks with a man carrying a water jar. He pointed toward the middle of the boat. The man set it down, and the trader crouched to examine the binding around its neck.

“Not that one,” he said.

“It held yesterday.”

“It leaks today.”

The man lifted it again without arguing. He was not pleased. I could tell by the way he kept his mouth closed.

“We have water,” I offered, lifting my jar.

“For yourself,” the trader said. “Keep it. We need enough for the men who move this boat.”

Sulad glanced at me but said nothing. I lowered the jar.

A crewman took our bundles and placed them behind a row of baskets. Sulad went after him to make sure the food would not be crushed. I followed, though there was nowhere for a third person to stand. The crewman pointed me back toward the landing.

“Stay there until we're ready.”

“I can help.”

“Then help by staying there.”

I went back.

On the bank, men were arriving with the last goods. One carried a basket alone and would not let another touch it. Two others lifted a jar between them, stopped when one lost his grip, and set it down before trying again. The trader watched the jar until it was secure. He had not looked once toward the road to see whether I had come.

Sulad returned with wet fingers. “Your cloth was touching the boards. I moved it.”

“Thank you.”

“Don't thank me yet. If the boards get wet again, we'll move it again.”

He had tucked his cord beneath his cloth. I could see the shape of the knot when he bent to lift his own jar.

“Did you see your uncle?” I asked.

“He was at the net before I left.”

“Did he say anything?”

“He asked whether I had eaten. Then he told me not to leave my knife where someone could step on it.”

“That's all?”

“He knows I'm going.”

Across the landing a crewman called for the last line to be checked. I turned at once. The boat shifted against its mooring, and the rope drew tight with a sound I felt in my teeth.

I started toward it. Sulad caught my sleeve.

“They haven't asked for you.”

“I thought it was slipping.”

“Then watch the man holding it.”

The crewman had planted his feet and was waiting while another moved a basket away from the edge. When the weight had been shifted, he eased the rope through his hands. The boat settled back against the planks.

I had once pulled at a line because I was frightened of what would happen if I did nothing. Someone had been hurt before I listened. This time I stood where Sulad had put me and watched until the crewman nodded to his companion.

The trader looked over his cargo, then at us.

“Both of you,” he said. “Bring your jars. If you are coming, come now.”

Sulad took up his jar. I took up mine. We stepped toward the boat together, though he reached the planks first.

The plank bent beneath Sulad's foot. I waited for him to move before I followed, carrying the jar away from my hip so it would not strike the side. A crewman reached for it. I held on too long.

“Do you want it aboard or in the water?” he asked.

I let go. He set it behind the baskets, where it could not roll.

There was less room than the trader had promised, though he had never promised comfort. Sulad found our food beneath a fold of dry cloth. He nested his own jar beside it and tied the bundle closed, then moved his knees to make space for me. I sat facing the landing. From there I could see the path by which I had come, and no farther.

“Keep clear of that rope,” Sulad said.

“I am clear.”

“You're sitting on its end.”

I stood. A man hauled the coil from beneath my foot without looking at me. The trader was checking the cargo once more. He pressed the patched basket with his palm, listened to the man beside him, and ordered it moved a hand's width toward the middle. Nobody asked whether I was ready.

At the stern, a crewman loosened the line around the post. The boat drew away and came back with the movement of the water. Another man held a pole against the landing while the last jar was lowered aboard. I thought the pole would break. He shifted his hands, let the wood bend, and waited.

“Now,” the trader called.

The line slid free of the post. The crewman gathered it aboard, wet fibers dragging across the gunwale. The man with the pole pushed once, hard enough that his heel slipped on the plank. Sulad caught my shoulder before I lurched into him. Then the gap between the boat and landing widened, and the pole came in after us.

I had expected someone to call my name. I had expected, perhaps, to see my mother at the end of the path despite what she had said. The landing was already smaller. A man carrying a basket walked across it and never looked toward the water.

The crew settled into their work. One man showed me where to place my feet when he needed to pass. Another told me to stop watching the bank and hold the jar steady. I obeyed him, though by then the jar was scarcely moving.

We traveled with the cargo, stopping when the men needed to attend to it and waiting when the water did not suit them. I learned how often a passenger could be in the way without taking a single step. Sulad asked questions when the crew had breath to answer. I asked one at the wrong moment and received no answer at all.

Each time the boat changed its angle, the patched basket leaned toward my knee. I steadied it once with my palm. A crewman shifted it back into place and told me to keep my hands clear of cargo that was not mine.

When the trader finally pointed out the landing where his buyer waited, I did not recognize it from the name he had given me. Men stood among jars and baskets. Someone raised an arm to guide us toward a place beside the planks.

“That's where our fare ends,” Sulad said.

I had known it yesterday. I knew it again now, and the second knowing cost more.

The first thing I recognized at the new landing was the basket with the pale binding I had watched them load in Maynila.

It had been lifted out of our boat before either of us was allowed to move. A buyer I had not seen in Maynila pressed his thumb against the seam, then shook his head.

I had spent the journey watching the basket whenever the boat shifted. I had thought that if it reached shore whole, the trader would be pleased. He was not.

“It was sound when you took it,” the trader said.

“It is wet now,” the buyer answered.

“The bottom is dry.”

“The bottom is where the water will go.”

The buyer turned the basket on its side. A thin line of water ran from the binding and darkened the boards. The trader crouched beside it, pushed the reed with his thumb and said nothing.

The crew had finally cleared the baskets where our bundles were stowed. Sulad fetched both and stood behind me with them. When he tried to follow the cargo ashore, a man carrying a pole told him to wait.

“We paid to come here,” I said.

“Then you have come,” the man answered. “Wait until the cargo is out.”

I felt Sulad's hand settle briefly against my back. It was not a warning to be quiet. He was holding me out of the path of two men carrying a jar.

When the planks were clear, the trader nodded to us. I reached behind the baskets for my jar, where the crewman had wedged it for the crossing. Sulad stepped ashore first. I followed with the jar against my hip. The boards were rougher than those at home, and the jar knocked against a raised edge. I stopped to lift it higher. Behind us the boat moved against its rope, lighter now that the baskets were coming off.

We had reached the place whose name I had carried home from the trader. I had repeated it so often to my mother that I could still hear myself saying it in her room. But standing there, I could not have told anyone which house would shelter us or where we might buy another passage. The name had bought me the right to find the place. It had not made the place mine.

Sulad pointed to a strip of shade beyond the baskets. “Put the food there before someone steps on it.”

We moved our bundles. A woman carrying a shallow tray came past, looked at the two of us and asked whose boat we belonged to.

“The trader's,” I said.

She looked at the cargo coming off it. “His boat is leaving again?”

“I don't know.”

“Then you do not belong to it for very long.”

She walked on before I could ask her name.

The buyer had called two more men to inspect the basket with the new binding. They pulled at the reeds and argued about how much of the contents might have taken water. The trader's voice rose. I had heard him refuse my silver when he could not promise space. I had not expected to see him pleading for a price.

“What happens if the buyer will not take it?” I asked Sulad.

“He takes it somewhere else.”

“And if no one wants it?”

“Then he has carried it here for nothing.”

The trader heard us. “Not for nothing. I have carried two passengers.”

I touched the cloth where my money had been. Sulad did not smile.

“Do you know someone who sends boats back to Maynila?” I asked.

The trader looked at the baskets. “I know men who say they will. I do not know which of them will be here when you have written your message.”

“I brought no written message.”

“Then you will need someone who remembers your words. That costs more than a mark on a board.”

“Will you ask for me?”

“I am asking this man to pay for goods he ordered.”

He turned back to the buyer. It was a refusal, but I could see what it cost him to stand there and keep bargaining. I had wanted to be offended. Instead I went to the basket that had been set aside and held it steady while a crewman loosened the wet binding.

“Who told you to do that?” he asked.

“No one.”

“Then put your hand there instead. If you hold the broken side, I cannot lift the bottom.”

I moved my hand. He lifted the basket, and together we set it where the air could reach the patch. The buyer watched us. He did not offer a better price.

Sulad came over and examined the binding. “That fiber will not hold once it dries,” he told the crewman.

“You have some better?”

“Not with me.”

“Then we have the same problem.”

Sulad looked at me. “We should find water before we find a captain.”

“We have a jar.”

“We have what is left in the jar. There is a difference.”

I wanted to ask whether he regretted coming. But the question would make my fear his responsibility, and he had given me no reason to think he wanted that.

“Where do we find it?” I asked instead.

He nodded toward the woman with the tray, who had stopped to speak to someone near the houses. “We can ask.”

We picked up our bundles. Behind us the trader was still bargaining, and the basket with the new binding stood open to the air. I had been counting the journey by how far we had come from Maynila. Sulad was counting how much water remained.

I went with him to ask.

The woman with the tray was selling cooked fish.

She had laid them in two rows, the larger ones nearest her, and was covering the smaller ones with a cloth when Sulad asked where we might fill our jar. She looked at the jar first, then at our bundles.

“Is it yours?” she asked.

“Yes,” I said.

“Then take it with you. The water is beyond those houses. Don't leave it beside my tray and expect me to watch it.”

Sulad thanked her. I followed him along the path. The woman called after us.

“And bring back the jar you borrowed if you borrow one.”

“We haven't borrowed one,” I said.

“Good. Keep it that way.”

There was a shallow place where people drew water. Two children stood beside a row of jars, waiting for an older man to finish filling his own. Sulad put our jar at the end of the row.

“You could have asked her about a boat,” I said.

“She was selling fish.”

“She might know.”

“She might. She also knew where the water was.”

The man ahead of us lifted his jar, tested its weight, and walked away. The children moved their jars forward. I watched Sulad set ours beside theirs. We had paid to cross the water, and now we were waiting behind children to fill a jar.

When we returned to the landing, the trader was no longer arguing. The buyer had accepted part of the cargo and refused the damp baskets. The crew had moved the rejected goods into the shade. Their boat was being readied for another errand, though no one would tell me where it might go.

“Can we stay aboard until you leave?” I asked the trader.

“No. The space is for goods again.”

A crewman was clearing the place where Sulad and I had sat. He shook out a damp rope and coiled it there.

“Where do we sleep?”

“Ask the people who own the ground.”

He bent to inspect the binding on a jar. Sulad took our jar from me before I could put it down too hard.

“Thank you for the passage,” he said.

The trader looked up. “You listened to my men. That is more than some passengers do.”

Sulad nodded. I waited for him to say the same of me. He did not.

At the edge of the landing, a man was loading a smaller boat. He had room between two bundles, and he was tying them down without hurry. I watched until he saw me.

“Where do you go?” I asked.

“To the place where these belong.”

“Is it farther along the coast?”

“It is not here.”

“Could you take two people?”

“No.”

“Even if we pay?”

“Not if you pay me twice.”

He pulled the knot tight and began another. I had asked badly. I tried again.

“I need to reach people who trade with Brunei. If you know someone—”

“I know people who say they do. I don't sell their promises.”

“Could you carry a message back to Maynila?”

He looked at my jar and our bundles. “If I say yes, and another man carries it from my next landing, and he loses it, will your family come looking for me?”

“I would tell them you had only agreed to the first part.”

“Would they hear that?”

I thought of the trader in Maynila, measuring the trouble that might come with my name. I had told him I was not asking him to carry a quarrel. Now I understood that I could bring one without meaning to.

“I don't know,” I said.

“Then I won't take it.”

He finished his knot. I stood there long enough for him to look at me again.

“I have no more to ask,” I said.

“Then let me work.”

I went back to Sulad. He was crouched by the rejected baskets, talking with the buyer. The buyer had pulled one apart to see which goods were damaged and which might still be sold. Sulad was helping him separate them. He had not waited for my permission.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Finding out whether he needs hands.”

The buyer looked at me. “Can you carry a basket without dropping it?”

“Yes.”

“Can you carry one after someone tells you where?”

I glanced at Sulad. “Yes.”

“I'll pay you for the work when it's done. Food, if you want food. No place to sleep.”

“That is enough for now,” Sulad said.

“It may not be enough for me,” I said.

The buyer picked up an empty basket. “Then find someone offering more.”

He turned away. Sulad watched me, waiting. I could have gone after the boatman and asked again. I could have told the buyer who my mother was. I could have stood there until someone grew tired of me and offered a little more simply to make me leave.

Instead I took the basket.

The work was not difficult, though I had to be shown twice how to stack the damp ones so that air could pass between them. When we finished, the buyer gave us food and directed us to a household that sometimes let travelers sleep under its roof for payment. He did not promise that they would have room.

We ate sitting beside our jar. The trader's boat was still at the landing. The smaller boat had gone, leaving a wet mark where its rope had rubbed the post.

“Do you want to go back?” I asked Sulad.

“I want to eat before the fish goes cold.”

“I mean to Maynila.”

“I know what you mean.”

He took another bite. I let him finish.

“I may want to go back,” he said. “Not today. You?”

I looked toward the path leading to the houses. We had a place to ask for shelter, enough food for the evening, and no one willing to carry my words home. Brunei was no closer in any way I could name.

“Not today,” I said.

Sulad pushed the last piece of fish toward me. I took it. When we stood, he lifted the jar and I carried the bundles. Neither of us knew whether the household would let us stay. We went to ask.
