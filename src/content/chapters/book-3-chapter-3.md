The first man to come to the hall after sunrise had mud up to his knees.

He stood outside the hall with a broken carrying pole across his shoulders. One end was splintered. The other held a basket of dried fish that had been pulled out of the river. Water ran from the weave and made a dark patch on the floor.

"Are you the man who hears complaints?" he asked.

"I am."

"Then come and hear mine where it happened."

Amaya was sorting cord with two women beside the cooking place. She looked up when he set the basket down.

"Take that outside," she said. "We have food drying here."

The man lifted it again without answering. He was from the far bank, judging by the way he had come in, but I had learned how little a man's landing told me about whom he served.

The keeper came through the door carrying her measuring cord. She saw the basket and frowned.

"Was it in a boat?"

"It was," the man said.

"Whose?"

"Mine to carry. Another man's to steer."

"Then we should hear the steersman as well."

"He is still trying to get his boat free."

I followed him to the steps. The keeper came with us. Behind her I heard Amaya tell the women to move the clean mats away from the wet place.

A long narrow boat lay across the lower approach, its bow pointing toward the shallows. Three men were standing in the mud beside it. One pushed a pole under the hull while the others rocked the boat from the rail. Each time they moved it, the bow rose a little and settled again.

Beyond them the nearer of our two channel stakes leaned toward the bank. I remembered it standing almost upright when Sulad had sounded the passage. Now the cut at its top faced the wrong way.

The man with the basket pointed at it.

"That was not where it stood yesterday."

"Who moved it?"

"Ask your people."

The keeper went down the steps without waiting for me. She called to the men to stop rocking the hull until she had seen how it rested. The steersman answered that the tide was falling and the boat would be left on its side if they waited.

"Then take the baskets out first," she said. "You are shaking your cargo into the water."

He looked toward me. "Is she giving the orders?"

"She knows this landing," I said.

"Then tell her to send us another pole."

I carried one down from the pile beside the shelter. The man who had brought the complaint took it from me and tested its end against the mud. He pushed it under the bow, but the ground gave way before the hull moved.

There had been two passages since the trial. The inner one was narrow and deep enough for small boats at the measured tide. The outer one took heavier loads when the water rose. We had marked the edge of the shoal with stakes cut from the same bundle. A man coming in from the bay could see where the bank began to rise without knowing every bend beneath the surface.

Yesterday the outer stake had stood beyond the bow of this boat. Today it stood beside the stern.

The keeper bent over the leaning wood. The water had fallen enough to show a fresh band of wet grain beneath the darker part of the stake. It might have been pulled up and driven in again. It might have been loosened by a passing hull. I could see no mark of a hand.

"Do you know which?" she asked the steersman.

"I know where I steered. I came by the stake. The bottom caught me."

"Did you strike the stake?"

"Not with the boat."

"Did you see anyone move it?"

"No. I saw where it was."

The man with the broken pole made a sound in his throat.

"That is what I have been telling him."

The keeper looked at the baskets still in the hull. Two were wet on their lower sides. One had lost its lid. The others had been raised onto the gunwale.

"Who bought this load?" she asked.

"A woman above the hall," the steersman said. "She was to send it onward before the market."

"Has anyone told her it is here?"

The men looked at one another. No one had gone.

I sent a boy from the steps to find her. I offered him a piece of dried fish from my own meal. He said he would rather be paid a small coin when he returned. I agreed.

The man with the broken pole followed me as I climbed back to the shelter.

"You can see what happened," he said.

"I can see your boat on the mud."

"Because your stake moved."

"The stake is out of place."

"Then you admit it."

"I do not know who moved it."

He stopped beside the steps. "You heard a whole hall of people yesterday. Did they give you a place to say that nothing is known?"

His basket was dripping onto his foot. He shifted it to the other shoulder and winced where the broken pole pressed against him.

"I will hear the steersman and the buyer," I said. "We will look at the mark Sulad kept from the sounding."

"While my fish sits wet?"

"No. Bring it above the tide."

"Into your hall?"

"Ask Amaya. She keeps the stores."

He stared at me as if I had sent him to a stranger's house.

"Is it yours or hers?"

"It is where she works."

"That was not my question."

"No," I said. "It was not."

He went toward the dry ground above the steps. I did not follow him into the hall to make Amaya take the baskets.

Sulad arrived from the net sheds with a coil of thin cord over one shoulder. He had been called to help set a new weight on a fishing line. When he saw the boat, he put the cord down where it would not trail in the mud.

"That stake is wrong," I said.

He looked at it, then at the boat.

"Who says it was right yesterday?"

"We measured it together."

"We measured a passage. I marked where the bottom rose. I did not tie the ground to the wood."

He took off his sandals and went into the water. The keeper gave him her cord. He held one end at the old post on the bank and asked the steersman to stand where he had first felt the hull touch. The steersman moved twice before choosing a place.

"Which of those is the first?" Sulad asked.

"I was in the boat."

"Then say you do not know. We can still measure."

The steersman settled on the nearer place. Sulad stretched the cord from the post and called the distance to the keeper. She made a cut in a thin strip of wood.

"Will you swear the stake was moved?" I asked.

"I will say it is not where I drove it," Sulad answered. "I did not see it move."

He pressed his foot into the mud beside the leaning stake and felt below the surface with his toes. Then he withdrew his leg and washed it in deeper water.

"There is another hole," he said. "A little beyond this one."

The man with the broken pole came running down.

"Then it was pulled out."

"Perhaps," Sulad said. "Or a pole was driven there before. There are many holes in this bank."

The man turned to me. "He will not say what you want him to say."

"He has said what he found."

"That is not enough for my loss."

I could not argue with that. It was not enough for mine either. A landing whose markers changed between one tide and the next would soon lose the owners who had agreed to use it.

Dala came down with Pawi carrying a net frame between them. They had meant to set it in the shallower water beyond the boat. The grounded hull blocked their way.

"How long?" she asked the keeper.

"Until we can lift it."

"Then the tide will be gone."

Pawi looked toward the men in the mud. He had helped rebuild their rack after the rope pulled it down. He started to set the frame aside and step into the water.

Dala caught his arm.

"You will not go under that hull," she said.

"I can reach the pole."

"You can reach it when the boat is standing. I will not have you pinned beneath someone else's load."

The steersman heard her. "If everyone keeps talking, we will be here until dark."

"Then listen while you work," Dala said. "You have taken the place where we set our frame. If we lose this tide, who will hear that?"

The keeper looked at me. Yesterday I had promised to hear complaints concerning the landing. I had not imagined that the first would come from a boat we could not move and a net we could not set.

"We will keep the inner passage open," I said. "The boat is not across it. No one is to pull the outer stake until we have two people from each bank to see where it stood."

The steersman shook his head.

"You will leave us stuck beside it?"

"We will lift your boat. We will not move the evidence while we do."

"Evidence of what?" the man with the broken pole demanded.

"Of a disagreement."

He laughed once, without pleasure.

The keeper asked Dala whether the frame could be carried farther upstream. Dala said the footing there was too soft for Pawi to hold it. She would lose the morning's catch if she moved. The keeper offered a place to hang the frame until the next tide. Dala said she would take it, but she wanted the missed work named when the landing counted the day's losses.

I nodded. She did not thank me.

The boy I had sent returned with the buyer, a woman who walked so quickly that he had to trot to keep beside her. I paid him the promised coin. She stopped at the wet baskets, opened one, and pressed the fish between her fingers. She smelled it and looked at the steersman.

"I paid for dry fish," she said.

"It was dry when we left."

"Then you should have brought it dry."

"The passage was marked."

She turned to the keeper.

"Was it?"

"At the last sounding."

"Who is responsible for the mark now?"

The keeper looked toward the river. Sulad was helping the men lift the stern enough to slide a pole beneath it.

"We have not agreed on that," she said.

The buyer closed the basket. "You have agreed on a fee."

"For landing," the keeper answered. "You have not landed."

The buyer set her hands on her hips. "That is the trouble."

I asked her whether she would take the undamaged baskets before the next tide. She said she would, but the wet fish could not be sent onward with the rest. She wanted the loss witnessed before anyone sorted or dried it. Amaya came out to see whether the baskets could be laid on the old rack outside the hall. She agreed to lend the rack until evening, provided the buyer and the carrier both counted what was placed there. She would not mix the fish with the household stores.

The man with the broken pole followed them, carrying the wet basket with both hands. His shoulder had begun to swell beneath his tunic.

I stayed at the steps with the keeper.

"Yesterday they gave me the right to hear this," I said.

"They gave you the work," she answered.

"Who will pay?"

"First find out what was lost. Then find out who promised what. Then ask the people who can pay."

She looked at the leaning stake.

"And do not let a man carry that away because he thinks it proves his side."

A small boat came through the inner passage while we were speaking. Its steersman watched the grounded hull, took the deeper line and passed without touching the bank. A second boat followed him. The landing still worked, though no one on the outer passage could pretend it was safe.

Near midday Banu arrived with a man I had not seen before. The stranger wore a plain cloth around his head and carried no goods. He stopped short of the wet ground, studying the stake and the men lifting the boat.

Banu came to me alone.

"He has come from the other bank," he said.

"From the house that sent the man to our hearing?"

"No."

"Then who sent him?"

"That is what he wants to tell you."

The stranger called from above the steps.

"I heard that you have ordered a marker to remain where it is."

"I have asked that it not be moved until both banks see it."

"Both banks?" he said. "You have heard one house speak for ours. Did you think that was everyone?"

I looked toward the far shore. Smoke was rising from the houses beyond the boats. I could not tell which roof belonged to the man who had come yesterday or to the one standing before me now.

The stranger came down one step.

"If you mean to make a rule for our passage," he said, "you will have to learn who may answer for it."

He waited. I had a boat on the mud, wet fish on Amaya's rack, and a marker whose old hole could no longer be distinguished from the others. I had no answer that would send any of them away.

"Then tell me who sent you," I said.

He looked at Banu first, as if deciding whether the messenger had repeated his words correctly.

"That," he said, "will take longer than you think."

* * *

The stranger would not come beneath the shelter.

He stood where the path met the dry bank, far enough from the broken boat that no one could accuse him of helping to free it. His headcloth was faded blue. He had tied it twice at the back so the ends would not fall over his eyes.

Banu waited beside him.

"Will you speak here?" I asked.

"Not while that man says his loss belongs to my house."

The carrier with the broken pole had set his wet fish on Amaya's rack. He turned at the sound of the stranger's voice.

"Your people use this water."

"So do yours."

"My boat was put on the mud."

"I can see that."

"Then you can see why I came."

The stranger looked at the keeper instead of me.

"Who set that stake?"

"People from both sides watched the sounding," she said. "Sulad drove the wood. The owners agreed to try two passages."

"Which owners?"

She named the boat-owning house that had sent a representative to yesterday's hearing, and the Maynila owners who had taken part. The stranger waited for another name. When she had finished, he asked again.

"Which owners?"

"Those who were there," she said.

"My people were not."

The carrier lifted his hands.

"Your people have a boat on this river."

"We have boats farther up. We come down to sell. We were not asked to put our name on a marker."

The keeper asked whether the stranger owned the boats himself. He said his sister's household kept two, and that he was sent to learn why one had been turned away from the outer passage before sunrise. He had not come to accept a toll, settle the fish claim or promise another house's cooperation.

"Then why did you say you spoke for your bank?" I asked.

"I did not."

"You said I must learn who may answer for it."

"Yes. You have already called a few owners the whole bank. I came because that is what men say before they begin collecting from people who never agreed."

I felt the carrier watching me. I had asked for a hearing because I wanted to know who moved a stake. Now I was being asked who had given me the right to ask.

Banu rubbed the dust from his feet with the edge of his cloth.

"I told him there was a quarrel," he said. "I did not tell him you had ordered his boats to pay."

The stranger turned to him.

"You said a man at Maynila was hearing claims."

"That is what I said."

"And you said the man had been given a place in the old hall."

"That too."

"You did not say he was given our passage."

"No."

I looked at Banu. He had carried my words twice now, each time returning with less certainty than I had hoped for.

"Will you carry another question?" I asked.

"After you decide what it is."

The stranger folded his arms.

"I can answer a question about my sister's boats. I cannot give you the people who live above our landing."

He had come to deny an authority I had not claimed aloud. Yet I had already spoken of witnesses from both banks, as if there were only two households to summon.

The steersman called from the river. The boat had risen enough for them to slide a round pole beneath the bow. They were trying to turn it toward the inner passage. Sulad told them to wait for the tide to cover the outer edge of the mud. One man wanted to pull now, while they could still stand beside the hull. The others wanted to spare the bottom planks.

The keeper went to them. She left me with the stranger and the carrier.

I asked the stranger how his sister's boats usually passed the lower landing.

"With the tide. We use the outside water when the loads are heavy. When it is low, we wait or take the inner way if no one is coming out."

"Who tells you which is open?"

"We look."

"Then you know the stake."

"I know there was a stake. I do not know who told you to move it."

"I did not move it."

"Then who is keeping it there?"

I had ordered that no one touch it until witnesses came. To the stranger, the stake was still mine because I had spoken about it as if it could be kept by my word.

"We are keeping it for people to see," I said.

"Which people?"

"Those whose boats use the passage."

"That is better."

He said it without approval. He was measuring whether I would make the next claim larger.

The man from the first hearing arrived while we were speaking. He had come in a small canoe, without cargo. When he saw the stranger, his face tightened.

"Who sent you?"

"My sister's house."

"I was here yesterday."

"I heard."

"Then you know what we agreed."

"I know what you agreed. We did not."

The first representative looked at me.

"Did you send for him?"

"No. Banu told him of the dispute."

Banu shook his head.

"I told him what happened. He decided to come."

The first representative pointed toward the fish boat.

"That carrier works for a man who uses our landing. We can hear his claim. We did not ask another house to come and undo yesterday."

"Your agreement was to try two passages," the stranger said. "That is not a right to mark all the water."

"It was not all the water."

"Then why does your stake turn our boats?"

The first representative began to answer, but the carrier interrupted.

"Your stake turned my boat."

The first representative looked at the grounded hull and then at the man carrying its loss. He had not seen the morning's approach. His mouth closed.

I knew how he felt. Yesterday he had been the voice from the other shore, the man whose acceptance had helped bring me into the hall. Today another man had arrived to tell him his voice was smaller than it sounded.

The keeper returned and told us the boat could be freed at the next rise. She had agreed to lend the men two poles and a line, to be returned before dusk. The carrier asked whether the fish buyer would still be waiting. The keeper said she was on Amaya's rack counting baskets and would not leave until she had a witness to the wet ones.

The first representative said he would take the carrier's account to his house.

"And will your house pay?" the stranger asked.

"I have not heard what happened."

"Then do not say you will settle it."

The first representative took a step toward him. I moved between them before either had raised a hand.

"Neither of you saw the stake move," I said.

"Neither did you," the stranger answered.

"I know. We have Sulad's sounding and the hole he found. We have the steersman's account. We can ask who worked on this passage after the last tide."

The first representative nodded. The stranger did not.

"And if you learn one of your people moved it?"

"Then that will be part of what we hear."

"Part of it?"

"The loss, too."

He looked toward the rack. Dala had come to speak with the fish buyer. Pawi stood beside her with the net frame they had been unable to set. The boy was watching the men in the water as if he might be allowed to help when the hull rose.

The stranger pointed at them.

"Do they have to wait for the houses to finish arguing?"

"No," I said.

"Then let them speak first."

I brought the men down to the rack. Amaya had made the buyer lay the dry baskets on one side and the wet ones on another. She had put a folded mat between the fish and the ground. The carrier had counted each basket aloud while she watched. The buyer had opened two and set aside the fish that smelled of mud.

Dala stood with her hands on her hips.

"I lost this tide," she said before I asked her anything. "My boy was ready to set the frame. The boat was in the way."

The first representative asked whether she meant to charge the carrier for the missed catch.

"I do not know yet," she said. "I know where the boat was. I know what I could not do. I will not put a price on fish I never caught because someone wants the account finished today."

The stranger looked at her with interest. He had come to challenge the authority of one house. Dala was challenging the ease with which all of us could turn her morning into a number.

The buyer named the baskets that had arrived wet. The carrier repeated where the boat grounded. The steersman, called from the river, described his approach and said he had followed the visible stake. The keeper confirmed the last agreed sounding but would not say when the stake moved.

Isan came from the market carrying a strip of cloth he had been asked to deliver. He stood beside me while the accounts were repeated. The first representative used a trade word for a charge that Isan had heard used for unloading goods. The stranger used it for a payment demanded from a passing boat.

"Are they speaking of the same thing?" I asked him quietly.

"No," Isan said. "One means a share when goods are brought to the steps. The other means a charge for going through the water."

"Tell them."

"You can tell them," he said. "They understand you. I will say which word each man used if someone later claims he heard another."

I asked the keeper to state the agreement made yesterday. She did so: a share for using the Maynila landing, collected with witnesses, and no charge merely for passing. The first representative said his house had accepted that trial. The stranger said his sister's house had accepted nothing, but had no reason to object to a passage that remained free.

"Then we have no quarrel over a passing fee," I said.

"We have no new fee yet," the stranger answered. "That is a different thing."

He had been watching my words as carefully as the keeper watched her cord.

The first representative wanted us to move the leaning stake back to Sulad's old measure before the next tide. The stranger objected that the old measure had never been witnessed by his sister's people. The carrier wanted the passage made safe immediately. Dala wanted the outer boat cleared so she could work. Each demand was reasonable until I tried to satisfy all of them at once.

I asked the keeper whether we could mark the dangerous place with a floating branch for one tide while the stake remained untouched.

"We can warn boats from the steps," she said. "I will not promise that a branch will stay where we put it."

The first representative offered to send a man from his house to stand watch. The stranger said his sister would send one too, but only if the warning did not become another way of claiming the passage. They agreed to stand on opposite sides of the steps, where each could see the other.

The keeper made them repeat what they would do. They would call to approaching boats, show the deeper line and leave the disputed stake in place until the next sounding. They would not demand money. They would not turn a boat away merely because its owner had refused yesterday's agreement.

It was the first thing the two men had agreed upon.

I asked whether they would meet again after the tide, with the people whose boats had been delayed.

The first representative said he could bring the men who had authorized him yesterday. The stranger said he would bring his sister, or someone she chose, and would ask the other households above the bend whether they wanted to send witnesses.

"When?" I asked.

"After they have heard what you want," he said.

"I want a rule that keeps the passage open."

"You want to make the rule."

He said it as if I had asked for something I did not yet understand.

Banu offered to carry the invitation if I would give him the words in front of both men. He wanted to know whom to approach, what was being asked, and who would pay him for the crossing. The keeper said the landing could pay a messenger from the repair share only if the owners agreed the journey served the landing. The first representative said his house would pay half. The stranger refused to pledge his sister's money without asking her.

"Then I will pay the other half," I said.

Banu looked at me.

"With what?"

I had a small piece of cloth left from the gifts I brought from Burné. I told him where it was and what it might be worth. He said he would take it when he had finished, if it was still there.

He made me repeat the invitation.

I asked for a meeting about the displaced stake, the two passages and the loss to the carrier, buyer and people kept from work. I said the people who had accepted yesterday's trial could attend, and those who had not accepted it could come to state their objections. I did not ask anyone to recognize me as master of the river.

The first representative listened. The stranger listened. Neither gave me the satisfaction of saying I had spoken well.

Banu went to find a boat.

When the men had returned to their work, I found the keeper washing mud from her measuring cord. She spread it over the rail to dry.

"Will they come?" I asked.

"Some will."

"Will that be enough?"

"Enough to begin. Not enough to speak for people who stay away."

I watched the two men take their places beside the steps. One stood where the outer passage opened. The other stood farther up, near the turn toward the inner water. They were not friends. They did not need to be friends to call a warning to a boat.

A woman carrying firewood came down the path and asked which of them had the right to stop her crossing. Both men pointed toward the keeper.

The keeper told her she could pass.

The woman climbed into the waiting ferry without asking either man again.

I had spent the morning trying to discover who spoke for Tondo. I had learned how many people could refuse to be spoken for. The meeting I had asked for would be harder than the one that had given me my place in Maynila.

I went back to the hall to find the cloth I had promised Banu. Before the tide turned, I wanted it ready.

* * *

Banu came back with the cloth tied around his wrist.

I had left it beneath a bowl in the hall, where I had told him to find it. He had found it there and brought it back.

"Did the man refuse it?" I asked.

"I have not offered it. He would have thought you sent me to buy his answer."

"Then what did you tell him?"

"What you told me to tell him. A stake has moved. A boat has grounded. The people who use the passage are asked to see what was measured."

"And?"

"He said his boat is not yours to summon."

I waited.

"He said he would send a woman who knows the landing, if she can leave her work. That is all he promised."

Banu untied the cloth and placed it on the bench. A corner had darkened with sweat. I wanted to tell him he could keep it for the journey already made.

"You still have to pay me," he said.

"I know."

"Then find something I can carry home. Cloth is no use to me if every man I show it to asks whose favor I have sold."

The keeper had heard us from the doorway. She went to the store rack, came back with a small basket of dried fish, and set it on the floor.

"From my own share," she said. "Not from the landing repair count."

Banu lifted the basket and tested its weight. He said it would do for one crossing, not for another journey after dark. He took it away before I could promise him anything else.

We were to meet on a strip of bank where neither of the two trial passages reached the shore. It was a place where ferries waited when a boat was already unloading below. No one called it neutral. It was simply too narrow to hold much cargo, and too exposed to the current to make a good place for a store.

The keeper told me to bring my own drinking water.

"Will the men from the other bank bring theirs?" I asked.

"I do not know. I know whose jars are in the hall."

Amaya stood behind her, cutting a strip from a damaged mat. She had been repairing the sleeping place of a child whose family still used the hall.

"The other men may ask for food," I said.

"They may."

"Would you give it?"

"If they are hungry, I will feed them as visitors. You will not take the grain we put aside for the workers and offer it as a pledge for an agreement they have not made."

"I did not ask for a pledge."

"You were looking at the jars."

She went back to the mat. I had been looking at them.

The keeper lent me the old sounding cord on the condition that she carried it herself. Sulad agreed to come only after the man who had hired him to mend a net released him for the afternoon. I offered to pay the difference.

"You have not finished paying Banu," he said.

He went to tell the net owner where he would be. I waited on the steps, watching the two warning men call the deeper passage to a canoe with three children and a load of green leaves. The canoe passed without a charge. The man from the first hearing raised his hand to the woman at the stern; she did not raise hers back.

We crossed in the ferry owner's boat. He charged the usual fare. He would take us to the waiting bank, then continue with his passengers. If we wanted to return after the tide turned, we would have to find another passage.

"Do you expect trouble?" I asked.

"I expect to earn my fare," he said. "I have already lost a morning to your trouble."

He put us ashore where the roots held the mud firm enough for feet. The keeper stepped out first with the cord. Sulad followed, carrying no weapon and no bundle. I brought a small water jar. The ferry owner pushed away before I had set it down.

Two people were waiting. One was the first far-bank representative. The other was an older woman with a paddle worn thin near the grip. She said her household kept boats above the bend and that the stranger in the blue headcloth was her brother.

"Did he send you?" I asked.

"He told me you were asking questions. I came to hear them myself."

She looked at the cord.

"Are you going to stretch that across the river?"

"No. It marks a depth we measured yesterday."

"Then it cannot tell you what the bottom was last season."

"No."

"Good. My youngest son says a cord can settle any quarrel if you pull it tight enough. He has not yet tried pulling one against a flood."

The first representative seemed relieved to have someone else answer me. He offered her a place on the driest root. She declined it and stood where she could see the river.

A third boat came from upriver with two men and a boy. They stopped beyond the roots. The boy carried a bundle of thin sticks and a shallow basket. One man came ashore, said he had been asked to witness the sounding for a boat-owning household farther north, and refused to name who had asked him until he knew who else had arrived.

The woman with the paddle laughed.

"Then you can go back without telling us who sent you."

He looked offended, but gave the name of his elder sister, who managed the household's boats while their father was ill. He had come because she had lost a load at a different bend when a marker was moved without warning.

"Was it this marker?" I asked.

"No. That is why she sent me. She does not want it to happen here."

He set the basket down. It held a broken stake, much older than the one in our passage, with the point worn smooth.

"She told me to show you what a marker becomes when everyone says another man will replace it."

I wanted to ask who had moved that stake. The boy who carried it had already turned away to help his boatmen secure their bow. The object had come to us without the story I needed.

The woman with the paddle asked whether I had called anyone from Lakandula's household.

"I sent no message to him," I said.

"Then do not say the houses of Tondo have met."

"I have not said that."

"Your messenger said you wanted to settle a passage for both shores."

"That is what I want."

"Wanting is easy. The people who answer to Lakandula will ask what you have offered their boats and why you expect their agreement before they have heard the injury."

"Will you carry the question to them?"

"I will carry what I hear. I cannot promise an answer. Nor can I promise that a man you call Lakandula will speak for the house that sent this boy."

The boy, who had been listening, looked up.

"My aunt sent me," he said. "She did not send him."

The man from the boat opened his mouth, then closed it. He had been speaking for the household as if the boy were only there to carry wood. The woman with the paddle asked the boy to repeat his aunt's instruction. He said she wanted the old stake shown, and that no one should agree to a new payment in her name.

"Now we have heard it," she said.

The keeper unrolled the cord on the roots. She showed the marks made at the last sounding and named the two people who had witnessed each one. She did not ask anyone to accept them. Sulad pointed across the water to the place where he had driven the newer stake, still leaning in the shallows.

"The bottom rises before it," he said. "A loaded boat needs room to turn. We measured the turn at that tide."

"Could you move the stake back yourself?" the woman asked.

"Yes."

"Would that make it right?"

"It would put it where I drove it. I cannot say whether that is where it should stay."

She turned to me.

"Then why did you stop people from moving it?"

"Because I wanted the man whose fish was spoiled to see where the boat ran aground."

"And when his fish is gone?"

"He has kept the wet baskets apart. The buyer has counted them."

"Then you have saved the count. Have you saved the passage?"

"No. The two men are warning boats until we can sound it again."

The man from upriver asked how long they would be paid to stand there. I told him they had offered one tide. He asked what happened on the next. I had no answer.

A narrow boat passed the waiting bank. Its steersman saw us gathered on the roots and called out whether the outer passage was open. The keeper answered that the inner line was clear and the outer stake remained disputed. He cursed the lost time and took the longer water.

The woman with the paddle watched him go.

"That is what our people hear," she said. "Not your good reasons. They hear that they must wait because men are arguing over a piece of wood."

I felt myself reaching for a promise. I could say we would pay for a watch every day, or build a new marker before sunset. The keeper had brought a cord and no money. Sulad had given an afternoon that belonged to another man's net.

"What can we try without making a new levy?" I asked.

The woman set her paddle flat on the roots.

"Send one person from each shore to watch a sounding. Let each say where a loaded boat can pass at the next low water. Do not put a new stake in until they have both seen the bottom. If they disagree, leave the old one where everyone can see it and warn the boats again."

The first representative said his house could send a steersman.

"Your house was at the first sounding," she answered. "Send him, but let another house watch too."

The man from upriver offered the boy as his aunt's witness. The boy shook his head.

"I can show where she told me to go. I do not know the bottom."

"Then she must send someone who does," the woman said. She looked at me. "That is not an insult to the boy. He has told us what he knows."

Sulad asked who would handle the pole if the witnesses came. He could do the work for a wage, he said, but the next low water would arrive before the net owner had finished with him.

The keeper said the landing had two workers who knew how to sound the shallows. One was the man who had helped Sulad set the first stake. The other had carried the cord. She would ask them whether they could come and what work they would miss.

"Then the first sounding can be done without Sulad," the woman said.

I looked at him. He nodded. It was a relief to him, I thought, though I could not know whether it was the money or the hours he would keep.

The woman agreed to bring one steersman from her household's boats. The first representative agreed to bring one from his own house. The man from upriver said he would ask his elder sister for someone who knew the shallow turn. None of them pledged another household.

We had a trial to arrange, but the woman had not finished.

"What will you do if your own people moved the stake?" she asked.

"Ask why."

"And after you have heard why?"

"Count the loss. Hear who was warned. Decide what can be repaired."

"Who will decide?"

I opened my mouth. The keeper was looking at the water. She had not come to answer for me.

"The people whose boats use it must hear the finding," I said. "I can answer for what the Maynila landing did. I cannot order your houses to accept my answer."

"That is a smaller promise than the one your messenger brought."

"Then he should carry this one back."

Banu was not there. I would have to find him again, or carry the words myself.

The tide had begun to pull away from the roots. The woman with the paddle lifted her basket and called for her boat. The boy took the old stake from the mud and carried it aboard. He had not agreed to leave it with us.

The first representative offered to take me back to Maynila. I thanked him and refused. I had come in a ferry whose owner wanted his fare, and I would return the same way if I could find one.

The woman watched me walk toward the upper landing where boats sometimes waited.

"Do not call this an agreement for Tondo," she said.

"What shall I call it?"

"A few people who will come and look."

She pushed off before I could ask whether that would be enough.

The keeper followed me along the bank, winding her cord. Sulad had already turned toward the net sheds across the river. He would find a crossing that did not require him to wait for me.

I looked back once. No one had left a pledge on the roots. No one had accepted a fee. We had chosen a tide, a place to measure, and people who might arrive.

When I reached the upper landing, the ferry owner was still on the far side. I sat on a dry root with my jar and waited for a boat that owed me nothing.

* * *

The first witness arrived while the outer passage was still covered.

She brought her own pole. It was longer than the one the keeper had lent the landing workers, and its lower end was worn pale from touching sand and mud. She set it beside the steps and asked where she could stand without being counted as one of our people.

"Where you can see the water," the keeper said.

"I can see it from anywhere."

"Then stand where your feet will hold."

The woman looked at the broken edge of the lower plank. She chose the higher step.

Her name was not offered to me. She said she steered for the household whose boats waited above the bend. The woman with the paddle had sent her to watch the sounding and return with what she saw. She would not agree to a marker merely because it made the Maynila landing easier to reach.

The first far-bank representative came in another boat with a man who knew the shallow turn. The boy from upriver followed them. His aunt had sent a steersman, he said, but the man had stopped to bring in a load and might not arrive before the water fell.

"Then he will see what remains," the keeper said. "We will not put the stake back while he is away."

The boy nodded. He had no wish to be asked to speak for the absent man.

Dala came down from the net sheds carrying a basket with a split rim. Pawi walked beside her with the frame they had not been able to set when the fish boat grounded. The frame was dry now, but a section of cord had been cut to free it from the hull. Dala laid it on the planks where everyone could see the loose ends.

"I am not here to sound the river," she said. "I am here because that boat stood where my boy should have worked."

The carrier with the broken pole had come too. His shoulder was wrapped in cloth. He kept his arm close to his chest and used the other to hold a shallow basket of fish that the buyer had refused.

"Some of it can be dried again," he said. "Some cannot."

The buyer stood behind him. She said she would speak when the people who counted the baskets were present. Amaya had agreed to come after the hall's morning cooking. Until then she had given the keeper a strip of wood marked with the baskets placed on her rack.

I looked for Sulad among the workers. He was not there. He had told me which net he would be mending, and the owner had not released him for another sounding. The keeper had brought the two men who helped him at the first measure. One held the cord; the other carried the pole.

"Begin where we began," she said.

The man with the pole stepped into the water near the old bank post. He sounded the inner passage first, calling the depths in words the keeper repeated for both shores. The far-bank steerswoman went into the shallows beside him. She made her own marks on a narrow piece of wood. The two men did not always choose the same place to lower their poles. When they differed, the keeper asked them to try again at the same point.

A canoe waited above the turn. Its steersman wanted to pass before the tide went out. The keeper told him the inner line was open. He went through, watching the poles.

The outer passage was shallower than I remembered. The displaced stake leaned toward the mud where the fish boat had struck. The hole Sulad had found lay beyond it. Between them was a patch of bottom that had been disturbed and smoothed by the water.

"Someone worked here," the steerswoman said.

"Boats ground here," the first representative answered.

"Both can be true."

She put her pole beside the stake. The wood shifted under the pressure. She stopped.

"Do you want me to lift it?"

"No," the keeper said. "We are here to see it before anyone does."

The steerswoman withdrew her pole. The keeper asked the two landing workers whether either had handled the stake since the first sounding. The younger man shook his head. The older man looked toward the upper steps.

I had seen him there the afternoon before the fish boat grounded. He had been hauling a load of jars from a vessel whose owner complained that the mooring line scraped her hull against the landing.

"Did you move it?" I asked.

"I helped clear a boat."

"That was not my question."

He pointed to the outer bank.

"The loaded boat came in against the current. Its stern swung toward the post. We needed a place to hold the line while we moved the jars. The bank post would not take the weight. The stake was closer."

The steerswoman looked at the leaning wood.

"You tied a loaded boat to a sounding mark?"

"Only while we shifted the cargo."

"And when the line pulled it out?"

He rubbed his palms together. They were crossed with old rope burns.

"We put it back."

The younger man stared at him.

"You said it was in the same place."

"I thought it was."

"Did you measure?" the keeper asked.

"We could see the old hole."

"Which hole?"

The man looked down. There were three dark places in the mud, one beside the stake and two beyond it. He pointed to the farthest.

"That one."

"Yesterday Sulad found another," the keeper said. "He would not say which was the old one."

The man took a breath.

"The tide was rising when we finished. I drove the wood where I could stand."

The first representative made a sound through his teeth. The woman from the other bank looked at the man for a long time before she spoke.

"Who told you to move a marker without calling the people who had measured it?"

"No one told us to move it. We needed the line."

"Then who told you to put it back?"

"I did."

He said it quietly. The younger man stepped away from him, as if the confession had changed the ground beneath them.

I remembered the loaded boat and the owner demanding that the jars be moved before dusk. I had told the men to clear the landing for the next arrival. I had not seen the line fastened to the stake. I had not asked how they meant to keep the stern from striking the bank.

"Was I there?" I asked.

"You were at the hall," the older worker said. "You told us the steps must be clear."

"Did I tell you to use the stake?"

"No."

"Did you think I would approve?"

He looked at me for the first time.

"I thought you wanted the boat gone."

He had wanted the same thing I wanted. The difference between an order and a wish had not kept the wood in the right place.

The first representative turned to the far-bank steerswoman.

"Now you know it was Maynila's men."

"I know these two men handled it," she said. "I know the stake was not measured when they returned it. I still need to know where a loaded boat can pass."

She put her pole in the deeper water. The keeper called for the sounding to continue.

I wanted to stop and make the older worker explain everything before the others heard it from the bank. The keeper was already taking the marks. She had chosen to finish the work the witnesses had come to see.

The man who had carried the wet fish followed me toward the steps.

"Will you make him pay?"

"I will hear what was lost."

"You heard it yesterday."

"Then I will hear what can be done about it."

"My shoulder will not be sound because you hear it twice."

He lifted the cloth enough to show the dark swelling. I had seen him move the baskets with his good arm. I had not asked who would do the carrying he could no longer do.

"Who hired you?" I asked.

"The man whose fish I brought."

"Will he pay for the days you cannot carry?"

"He says I was paid for the journey."

The buyer came to stand beside us.

"I paid for fish that would arrive dry," she said. "I can use some of it. I cannot sell the rest at the price we agreed. The man who sent it will say I made the loss larger than it was."

She pointed to Amaya's mark on the strip.

"I asked her to keep that because the carrier and I did not agree on the spoiled baskets. I will not put a false count beside a true injury to make your decision easier."

Dala had heard her. She came with Pawi, carrying the damaged net frame.

"I lost the tide," she said. "You cannot give it back. I will not claim fish I never caught. I will claim the cord that was cut and the work needed to mend it. My boy will tell you how long we waited because he was here."

Pawi looked at me. His mother had not asked him to speak. He said he had held the frame while the boatmen tried to move the hull. He had been told to get away when the pole broke. He knew how many loops were cut because he had tied them himself.

The older worker was still standing in the shallows. The keeper had finished the outer sounding. She called him up and asked him to repeat his account in front of the carrier, buyer, Dala and the two far-bank witnesses. He did, without looking at me.

The younger worker said he had seen the older man drive the stake back, but had not noticed that it stood nearer the bank. He had been carrying the last jars. The keeper asked why he had not spoken when Sulad measured the hole the previous day.

"I did not know he was asking about our boat," he said.

"Did you know the stake had been lifted?"

"Yes."

"Then you knew something the people measuring did not."

He looked at the older man. No one answered for him.

The first representative said the landing should pay for the damaged fish because its workers had caused the hazard. One of the Maynila owners, who had arrived during the sounding, objected.

"The men were helping a visiting vessel," he said. "The owner of that vessel should pay."

The older worker shook his head.

"She paid us to unload. She did not ask us to tie to the stake."

"Then you pay," the owner said.

The worker held out his hands. His palms were empty.

I knew the owner wanted me to call the act an emergency. If I did, the worker would be forgiven for saving a loaded hull. The owner could say no one had done wrong. The buyer and the carrier would be left to quarrel over the loss, and Dala would be told the tide had gone without her.

I asked the keeper to bring the repair share count.

She did not move.

"That share was agreed for the landing's repair," she said. "You cannot spend it because you are ashamed of what happened."

"I was going to ask what it could cover."

"Ask the people who gave it."

The Maynila owner looked annoyed. The far-bank steerswoman looked toward the empty water, where the stake still leaned.

I turned to the people on the steps.

"The landing workers took the sounding stake out to hold a boat. They drove it back without measuring. They did not tell the keeper or the owners who had witnessed the passage. I asked them to clear the steps, and I did not ask how they would do it. The boat that grounded followed the visible mark."

The owner opened his mouth.

"I will not call the movement of the stake a necessary repair," I said. "It made the passage unsafe. The loss belongs in a claim against the Maynila landing and the people responsible for its work. The carrier, the buyer and Dala may state their losses separately. No one will charge a passing boat to pay them before those claims are heard."

The older worker lowered his head. I had not named him as a thief. I had named what he had done. The younger one watched me with a face I could not read.

The keeper said she would write the finding on a new strip, with the witnesses' marks beside it. She would keep the damaged fish count apart from the unmeasured lost catch. The owner demanded to know who would pay.

"We have not decided the shares," she said.

"You are leaving the claim open."

"Yes."

The first representative said he would bring the finding to his house. The steerswoman said she would take it to the woman who sent her. She would not call it a settlement until the people who suffered the loss had heard what was offered.

Dala took Pawi's frame and walked toward the net sheds. The carrier sat on the upper step and rested his injured arm. The buyer went with Amaya's marked strip to find the man who had sent the fish.

The older landing worker approached me when the others had gone.

"Will you send me away?" he asked.

"I do not know yet."

"I did what I thought you wanted."

"I know."

"Then why did you say it before all of them?"

"Because they were all made to use the mark you put back."

He looked past me toward the river. The new sounding was finished, but the old stake still stood in the wrong place. No one had moved it. For the first time that day, I understood why the keeper had insisted on leaving it there until everyone had seen it.

The worker turned away. I did not call him back.

A canoe came toward the outer passage. The steerswoman from the far bank raised her pole and called the deeper line. The canoe altered its course. It passed the landing without stopping, and no one asked it for money.

The passage was open for that boat. The people on the steps were still waiting to learn who would pay for the last one.

* * *

The Maynila owner came to the hall with a coil of new cord.

He dropped it beside the keeper's bench. It struck the floor hard enough to make the child sleeping behind the partition wake and cry.

"For the woman's net," he said. "There. One loss settled."

Amaya came from the cooking place and lifted the coil out of the child's reach.

"Which woman?"

"Dala."

"Then give it to Dala."

"I brought it here so everyone could see."

"She does not sleep here."

He looked at me as though I had told Amaya to shame him. I had said nothing. She carried the cord outside and laid it on the rack where Dala had left the damaged frame.

The owner followed her.

"She will say it is the wrong length."

"Then she can say so to you."

He returned to the bench without the satisfaction of having his gift accepted. He had helped pay for the first sounding and had lent two men to the landing repairs. He believed that entitled him to be heard before the people whose work had been interrupted.

"We cannot keep counting every complaint," he said. "The passage is open. I have boats waiting."

"The carrier cannot lift a basket," I said.

"Then his employer must find another man."

"His employer says the journey is finished."

"Then let him come here and say it."

The keeper was already sending for him. She had also asked the buyer to bring the basket marks, and Dala to bring the frame. The owner complained that we were holding another meeting when everyone should be working.

I wanted to tell him that his boats could work while he sat. Then I remembered that I had asked the landing workers to clear the steps quickly, and they had tied their line to a sounding stake. I had made the same demand in gentler words.

The man who had moved the stake arrived carrying a short piece of timber. He said he could set a new marker in the deeper line as soon as the witnesses agreed.

"Will you be paid?" the owner asked.

"I have work here."

"That was not my question."

The man set the timber beside the bench.

"I will do the work," he said. "I do not know who will pay me for it."

The keeper told him to leave the timber where it lay. The new stake would not be driven until the people from the far bank had seen the sounding.

The owner sat down and folded his arms.

"Then I will take my men back. I did not pay them to stand beside a piece of wood while strangers decide what it means."

"You may take them," Amaya said.

He looked up.

"Will you finish the repairs without them?"

"We will find out."

His face tightened. He had expected us to beg him to stay. I had expected it too.

By the time Dala arrived, the cord was still on the rack. She tested it between her fingers. She pulled a short length against the frame and shook her head.

"It will mend the part that was cut," she said. "It will not give us the hours we lost."

"I have not asked you to forget them," I said.

"Good. I have not asked you to buy the fish I did not catch."

She called Pawi over and asked him to show the keeper which loops needed replacing. He did so, counting each knot and then showing how the new cord would be joined to the old. He knew more about the repair than I did. The owner watched him without speaking.

The carrier came with the buyer and the man who had sent the fish. His injured arm was still wrapped. The sender had brought no goods. He said he would pay the carrier for another journey when the shoulder healed, but would not pay him for days spent sitting.

"He was hurt bringing your fish," the buyer said.

"He was hurt because the passage was wrong."

"Both things can be true."

The sender looked at the Maynila owner.

"Your men moved the mark."

"My men did not own that boat."

The older landing worker stood by the door. He had come because the keeper asked him to hear the finding again. He had brought his younger companion, who would say what he had seen. Neither man could pay the loss from the coins in his house. The owner knew that.

The keeper asked the buyer to name the baskets she could not sell. She gave the count from Amaya's rack and the price she had agreed before the journey. The sender disputed the price of two baskets, saying they had been packed with smaller fish. The buyer asked him to look at the marks made before the cargo was opened.

He looked, then said he would accept the count if the keeper repeated the difference aloud. She did. He agreed to replace the spoiled fish from his next catch, provided the buyer accepted it at the same price and did not demand coin that day.

The buyer said she would accept the fish if it arrived before the next market and was sound. She would not take a promise that could be changed when the tide was wrong.

The keeper cut a mark for the replacement and named two witnesses. The sender said he would bring the catch himself.

The carrier asked who would feed his household until he could work.

No one answered.

I had been waiting for the accounts to become small enough to settle. The shoulder was not an account that could be closed with a basket.

The older landing worker offered to carry loads for the injured man on the next two market days. He would take no wage for that work. The carrier asked whether he could lift as much as the man who had been hired.

"I can carry," the worker said.

"Can you carry for my employer without losing the work you already owe the landing?"

The worker looked at the keeper.

She said he could leave the repairs for two days if the owners found someone else to finish them. The Maynila owner made a noise of protest, but did not say no. The carrier agreed to try the arrangement for one market day and return if the work was not enough.

The hall had grown hot. The cooking fire had been banked, but the air was thick with the smell of rice and wet cloth. A child crawled beneath the bench and touched the new stake. The older worker moved it away before the child could pull it down.

Banu arrived with the woman who had brought her paddle to the waiting bank. With her was a man I had seen crossing from the far side on a narrow ferry. He carried a short staff used for walking on mud, not a weapon.

The woman said the man had been asked to carry words from a household that brought disputes to Lakandula.

"Did Lakandula send you?" I asked him.

"I was asked to hear whether a new charge was being set on the water."

"By whom?"

He named the person who managed that household's boats. I did not know the name. The woman with the paddle did. She said the man had carried messages for them before, and that he had returned with the same words each time.

"Then tell us what you were asked to say," the keeper said.

"No house has given you all the boats on the far shore. The people who follow Lakandula may hear a claim about their own passage, but they will not let another house promise their boats in their name."

He paused.

"That is what I was asked to carry. I have no order to agree to a payment."

The Maynila owner laughed once.

"Then you have come to refuse something no one offered."

"I came because people are already saying the new marker will have a price."

The keeper showed him the strip on which she had recorded the first sounding. It named a share for goods unloaded at the Maynila steps and no payment for boats that passed through.

"Who may change it?" he asked.

"The people who use the landing must hear a proposed change," she said. "Those who only pass cannot be made to pay by an agreement they did not make."

"And if a boat owner refuses?"

"Then we cannot say he agreed."

The owner who had brought the cord stood up.

"If every man can refuse, no one will pay for the passage."

Dala looked at him.

"Then do not ask us to pay for a passage that belongs to your boats."

"My boats bring goods you sell."

"And my nets feed people who do not own boats."

They faced each other across the bench. I thought he would walk out. Instead he pointed to the new cord on the rack.

"Will you take it?"

"I said it would mend the frame."

"Then take it."

"I will. I will also come back if the frame fails where the new cord joins the old."

He sat down. The keeper cut a mark for the cord received, leaving the work still to be checked.

The far-bank messenger asked what would happen when the next boat struck a marker. Would the owner of the boat have to bring his claim to Maynila, where the men who moved the wood would decide it?

The Maynila owner said the keeper knew the passage. The messenger said knowledge was not the same as an answer to a complaint.

I asked the keeper whether she would let a far-bank witness hear a claim against her own landing. She said she already had, and pointed to the woman with the paddle.

"That is one claim," the messenger said. "What of the next?"

The keeper asked him what his people would accept.

He said a boat owner should first bring a complaint to the shore whose work caused the loss. If the shore refused to hear it, the owner should be able to ask witnesses from both banks to see the mark and the cargo before it was moved. Neither shore should collect a new payment while the complaint was being heard.

The Maynila owner objected that people would use a complaint to avoid paying the landing share. The keeper answered that a boat which unloaded goods still owed the share agreed for the steps. A boat which passed through did not.

The messenger asked who would choose the witnesses.

"Each household may send someone who knows the passage," the woman with the paddle said. "But the person who made the claim must be allowed to hear what they found."

Dala raised her hand.

"And the people whose work was blocked? A boat may be gone before anyone asks what happened on the bank."

The keeper said they too could bring a claim. She asked Pawi whether he would be willing to repeat what he saw at the grounded boat when the next witnesses came. He nodded, then looked at his mother.

"He will speak for himself," Dala said. "I will be beside him."

The Maynila owner wanted a time limit. He said no one could keep a boat waiting for days while people searched for witnesses. The far-bank messenger agreed that a passage must be warned or opened at once if it was dangerous. The damaged cargo could be counted separately. The stake should not be changed secretly while the claim remained.

We had almost reached terms when the owner said the landing should collect a small payment from every boat to keep two watchers at the turn.

The woman with the paddle picked up her staff.

"Then we are finished."

He stared at her.

"Someone must pay them."

"Ask the owners whose boats need the watch. Do not take from the people who did not agree."

She started toward the doorway. I moved to let her pass, then spoke.

"We have agreed on the sounding and on how to hear the loss. We have not agreed on a new payment. Let the trial happen without one."

She stopped. The messenger waited beside her.

"Will you put that beside the other terms?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then I will stay until the keeper has marked it."

The keeper made a fresh strip. She did not call it a treaty. She marked who had agreed to witness the next sounding, who would hear complaints, and which payments remained unsettled. The first far-bank representative added his mark for his own house. The woman with the paddle added hers for her household. The messenger said he would carry the terms back to the people who sent him, without pledging their consent.

The Maynila owner refused to add his mark until the keeper recorded that he had supplied cord for Dala's frame and would lend one worker to finish the new marker. She did. He marked the strip, then said he still opposed any rule that let a passing boat refuse a charge for keeping the water safe.

"You may oppose it," the keeper said. "You may not collect it today."

He looked at me. He wanted me to overrule her. I had been given a place in the hall to hear claims, not the right to empty the keeper's words when they troubled an owner.

"I will not collect it," I said.

He left before the others. The coil of cord remained on Dala's rack. Pawi had already begun to cut it into lengths he could use.

When the meeting ended, the far-bank messenger asked me what title I intended to use when I sent the terms upriver.

"Ache," I said.

"That will not tell them which boats you speak for."

"Then say I speak for the Maynila landing and the people who have asked me to answer there."

He nodded.

"And say which people have not."

He went down to his ferry. I watched him cross without asking whether Lakandula would approve. I did not know if the man who carried our words would ever stand before him.

Behind me, the keeper hung the marked strip above the floor where the children could not reach it. It held fewer promises than the men had wanted when they entered the hall. The promises it held belonged to people who had been present to make them.

* * *

The old stake came out of the mud with a sound like a foot pulled from a deep shoe.

The landing worker had waited until the witnesses arrived. He had stood beside it with both hands empty while the keeper called the names of the people who had seen it leaning. The steerswoman from the far bank was there. So were the first representative, the boy who had carried his aunt's old marker, and two workers from Maynila.

The man who had moved it before did not touch it.

He stood on the bank and watched the younger worker loosen the mud around the wood. When the stake came free, the keeper asked him to show the place where he had tied the loaded vessel's line. He pointed to a worn band below the top. The fibers had left a pale mark in the dark wood.

"Is that where the line was?" the steerswoman asked.

"Yes."

"Did you see the line take the wood out?"

"I saw it lean. We pulled it free to keep the boat from dragging it under."

The keeper repeated his words so the people on both banks could hear. She asked the younger worker what he had seen. He said he had helped put the wood back and had not checked the old measure.

No one said the wood could tell us who first made the mistake. The workers had told us that themselves. The keeper wrapped the stake in an old cloth and laid it above the tide, beside the strips holding the soundings and the basket count.

The new stake lay on two blocks of timber. Its point had been shaped the day before. The older worker had made it, then left it where the keeper could see it until the witnesses came.

The far-bank steerswoman examined the point. She ran her hand along the grain and asked whether the wood would split when driven into the hard bottom. The older worker said it might. He had brought a second piece in case it did.

"Who pays for the second?" she asked.

"I cut both from the wood the landing gave me."

"Then the landing has paid already."

The keeper made him say which pile it had come from. It was timber set aside for repairing the lower steps, not from Amaya's hall. The owner who had supplied the cord had agreed to let one length be used. He had not agreed to pay for another after that.

We went into the shallows while the water was low enough to show the edge of the turn. The two steersmen lowered their poles in the same places. The keeper held the cord. The far-bank woman watched the cuts and asked them to repeat one depth where the bottom fell away sharply.

The first representative wanted the marker farther toward his bank, to leave room for a loaded boat to turn without scraping the Maynila posts. The steerswoman objected that the place he chose would send a narrow canoe across the deeper line used by ferries.

"Then put it between," he said.

"Between what?"

He showed her with his pole. She tried the bottom and found a hollow he had not noticed.

"That is where a boat will strike when the water drops," she said.

He tested it himself. He did not argue again.

I had come into the water ready to say where the stake should stand. I had seen the sounding once before. I could point to the old mark and the place where the fish boat grounded. When the steerswoman moved her pole a hand's breadth, the depth changed enough to make my answer useless.

The keeper asked whether the two witnesses could agree on a place. They chose a point beyond the hollow, where the bottom rose slowly enough for a loaded boat to turn. The keeper called it aloud. Each repeated it. The older worker brought the new stake and waited.

"Drive it," the first representative said.

The steerswoman lifted her hand.

"Who will tell the next man he may not pull it out to hold his line?"

The older worker looked at his palms.

"I will," he said.

"You may not be here."

The keeper said a mark was not a mooring post, and that the landing would keep a separate post for vessels needing to hold against the current. The far-bank witnesses could not order Maynila to build it. The Maynila owners had to decide where it would stand and how to pay for it.

"Then leave room for the post," the steerswoman said. "Do not make a rule today that gives you no place to tie a boat tomorrow."

The keeper measured the space from the steps to the proposed stake. She asked the older worker whether a mooring post could be driven closer to the bank without narrowing the passage. He said it could, but the bank there was soft. It would need bracing before a loaded vessel trusted it.

"That is work for another tide," she said.

We had no bracing ready. The first representative agreed that boats could use the upper post until the landing repaired the lower one. He would warn his house not to tie to the sounding stake. The steerswoman said she would carry the same warning to hers, but could not speak for every vessel that came downriver.

The older worker drove the new stake.

It went in straight at first, then twisted as the point met something hard beneath the mud. He stopped, pulled it a little, and tried again. The keeper watched the cord. The steerswoman tested the depth on both sides. When they were satisfied, the worker pressed the wood down until it stood firm.

No one cheered. The first representative wiped his hands on his cloth. The steerswoman went back to her boat to fetch the load they had agreed to use for the trial.

The load belonged to her household: four jars of oil and a basket of cloth. She had brought it from the far bank and meant to take it to a buyer above Maynila. The boat was neither empty nor as heavy as the cargo vessel that had pulled the first stake loose. She said that was enough for a first trial and no more.

The keeper asked who would steer. The steerswoman said she would. The first representative asked to stand at the bow with a pole so he could call the depth. She agreed on the condition that he did not touch the new marker.

The canoe left the upper bank and entered the turn.

At first it moved easily. The current took its stern toward the Maynila steps, and the steerswoman turned the paddle to bring the bow back toward the deeper line. The man at the bow called a depth. She answered without looking at him.

Then a boat came out of the inner passage.

It carried two women and a child, with a basket of leaves beneath the forward seat. The women had not been told there would be a trial. They were crossing before the sun reached the roof of the hall.

The man at the bow of the loaded canoe raised his pole.

"Hold there!"

The woman steering the small boat shook her head.

"Where?"

He pointed to the outer shallows. She looked at the stake and saw the mud beyond it.

"That is not deep water."

He lowered his pole again and found the bottom.

The steerswoman in the loaded canoe had already turned her bow toward the upper bank. She called to the small boat to keep the inner line. The two vessels passed close enough for the child to reach toward the oil jars. His mother pulled his hand back.

The loaded canoe swung once in the wake. The steerswoman corrected it with a long stroke. The bow passed the new stake with room to spare. The stern came close enough that the first representative reached for his pole, then stopped when she told him to leave it.

They cleared the turn.

The keeper made no mark until the canoe had reached the upper landing. Then she asked both steersmen whether the passage had been deep enough. The first representative said yes at that tide, but he wanted the turn sounded again after a heavy rain. The steerswoman said a wider boat would need more room.

"Then say that," the keeper told them.

They did. She recorded what the trial had shown and what it had not.

The woman from the small boat came ashore with her child. She asked whether she would have to wait for the next trial before using the passage again.

"No," the keeper said. "The inner way is open."

"And who will stand here to tell us when it is not?"

The keeper looked at the owners who had agreed to send warning men. One had already gone to unload cargo. The other was helping the steerswoman carry her jars.

"For now, the people working the steps will call the safe line," she said. "We still have to agree who keeps watch when they are away."

The woman nodded and went on with her child. She had asked a question the witnesses could not answer with a stake.

The owner who had brought Dala's cord arrived after the trial. He had not come to see it. He had come to ask when his worker would return.

"The passage is marked," I told him.

"Then he can work."

"The keeper needs him to help set a mooring post."

"I have already given wood for the stake."

"Yes."

"And now you want more."

"I want the landing to decide how boats will be tied without moving it again."

He looked at the new marker, then at the old one wrapped above the steps.

"How long will you keep that broken piece of wood?"

"Until the people who lost work have heard what is offered."

"They have heard it."

"Dala has taken the cord. The carrier has agreed to one day of replacement work. The buyer is waiting for fish. Those are not the same thing as every loss being paid."

The owner shook his head.

"You will keep men coming to this hall until they learn that every broken thing has a claim."

"Then bring yours when something of yours is broken."

He did not smile.

"I will bring it to the owners who pay for the landing," he said. "I do not know why I should bring it to you."

He walked away before I could answer. He was free to take his boats elsewhere. He was also free to tell other owners that I had made the work harder. The passage we had opened could lose him without closing at once. I did not know how long it could lose men like him.

Dala came down with Pawi carrying the repaired net frame. The new cord was darker than the old, and the knots sat close together where he had joined them. She set the frame beside the steps, but did not put it in the water.

"Will you use it today?" I asked.

"When the tide is right."

"Is the repair sound?"

"We will find out."

Pawi looked at the new stake.

"That one is farther out."

"It is where the witnesses measured it."

"Can I fish beside it?"

"Ask your mother."

Dala lifted the frame.

"He can fish where the water and the other boats leave room," she said. "No one here has promised him a place forever."

She went down to the bank and set the frame where she could hold it. Pawi followed, watching the cord as the current took it.

The keeper brought the marked strips to the hall. She had recorded the new stake, the witnessed sounding, the trial at that tide, the absence of a new passing fee, and the people who would hear a complaint if the marker moved again. The first representative had marked the account for his house. The far-bank steerswoman had marked what she saw. The man who carried words from the household associated with Lakandula had not returned. His people had made no promise.

Amaya read the names aloud to the workers gathered near the door. She kept the old stake in the corner beside the broken red post from my father's hall. They were different pieces of wood. One belonged to a dispute about a passage. The other belonged to a house that had changed while I was gone.

I sat on the low bench. A porter came in to ask whether the new mooring post would be ready before the next cargo boat. I told him the owners had not yet agreed on the bracing. He said he would tell the steersman to use the upper post until they had.

Then he asked who would pay him for carrying the message.

I looked toward the keeper. She was putting away her cord. The work of keeping a passage open had begun again before the mud dried on our feet.

"Ask the owner who sent you," I said.

"He told me to ask you."

I had no coin ready. I told him I would go with him and speak to the owner myself. He waited while I put on my sandals.

Outside, the tide was rising around the new stake. The old one lay dry above the steps, where anyone could ask why it had been taken out. Boats passed on either side of the turn. Some used the marked water. Others chose their own way.

I went down to meet the man who had sent the porter. Across the river, the houses kept their boats and their claims. The wood stood between us until the next tide, and the people who had put it there knew where to find one another.
