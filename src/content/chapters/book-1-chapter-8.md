The buyer finished counting the stack before he answered me.

“I need the six at the back moved out of the sun,” he said. “I need them kept in order. Can you do that without opening them?”

“Yes.”

He lifted the covering from one basket and showed me a line of dampness along its rim. “They were set there while the ground was dry. The shade is over there now.”

I looked where he pointed. A low screen of woven leaves stood beside the path. Behind it, the earth was firm and cooler beneath my feet. I could move six baskets in less time than it took him to explain.

“What will you pay?”

He glanced at me. Yesterday I had spent my last silver buying another man's help. I could still feel the place where it had lain against my skin.

“What do you need?”

“Breakfast.”

“Do you want fish or a boat?”

“Fish, if that is what you have.”

He uncovered a small leaf packet beneath his counting board. There was cooked rice pressed together and two narrow pieces of fish, dark where they had dried near a fire. I could see at once that it had been his own meal.

“For the six,” he said. “Not for standing here asking questions.”

I looked at Sulad. He was beside our jar, rubbing the red mark at the base of his thumb. He had carried water yesterday and moved baskets until the light failed. I had expected him to step forward.

“Take the work,” he said. “I will see that nobody puts the jar in the path.”

It was not what I had wanted him to say. It was useful.

The first basket was heavier than it looked. I took it by both handles and felt the woven edge press into the sore places in my palms. The buyer made me set it down again.

“That one goes last.”

“Why?”

“Because I counted it last.”

He showed me the shallow cuts on the counting board. There were six marks, with a space between the fourth and fifth. The marks told him which baskets he had checked, though they told me nothing about what lay inside.

I carried the basket he chose. Its damp bottom left a dark print on the ground. When I returned for the next, he was talking to another man and did not look up.

By the fourth basket, I had stopped trying to impress him with speed. I set each one beside the last, leaving room to lift it without dragging its neighbor. Sulad had moved our jar against the base of the screen. He sat where he could see the path and the water at once.

A boy came running from the landing. He stopped at the buyer's board, bent with his hands on his knees, and tried to speak before he had breath.

“Not here,” the buyer said. “First tell me what you saw.”

“Two poles. Jars under a mat.”

“Whose boat?”

“I could not see.”

“Then you saw a boat.”

The boy nodded, offended by the difference. The buyer gave him a little water and sent him back to watch. I took up the fifth basket.

“Is it going south?” I asked.

“I haven't seen it.”

“But you know boats.”

“I know what they buy from me. I don't know where they go after they have bought it.”

He counted the fifth basket, then pointed to the sixth. I could hear the boy calling to someone farther down the bank. The sound made my arms hurry. I lifted too soon, caught the basket's rim against the next one, and heard the dry scrape of reed on reed.

“Put it down,” the buyer said.

I did. Nothing had broken. He came over anyway and checked the binding before letting me lift it again.

“You said you could do six.”

“I have done five.”

“Then do the sixth.”

When the last basket stood in the shade, he went along the row, touching each rim and checking his board. He found the wet one and turned it so the air could reach the dark patch underneath.

Only then did he hand me the packet.

I carried it to Sulad. We sat behind the screen with the jar between us. The rice was cool and stuck to my fingers. We divided the fish without measuring it, though I noticed that he gave me the thicker piece. I gave part of it back.

The boy shouted again. This time the buyer left his board.

From where we sat, I could see the end of a vessel come around the bend, moving slowly under the pressure of two poles. Jars stood beneath a mat stretched across its middle. One man was trying to keep the mat from lifting while another watched the shallows ahead.

I swallowed the last of the rice too quickly.

“Do you think they'll take us?” I asked.

Sulad wiped his fingers on a leaf. “You haven't asked them.”

The buyer was already walking toward the water. I folded the empty packet and put it beside the jar so that it would not blow across his baskets. Then I followed him.

The buyer reached the water before I did. He stopped beside a man holding a pole and waited while the boat came close enough for its passengers to step ashore.

It was wider than it had looked from behind the screen. Jars stood in two rows beneath the mat, each wedged against its neighbors with coils of leaf and old cloth. The man at the stern lifted one corner of the covering and told the buyer to look without coming aboard.

“Three are cracked,” he said.

“Three were cracked when you took them,” the buyer answered.

“Then you remember better than the man who gave them to me.”

The man turned one jar until a pale line on its shoulder faced the shore. I could not see the crack from where I stood. The buyer crouched and examined it without touching.

“Did you bring what he asked for?” he said.

The man pointed toward a smaller jar near his feet. “He asked for one. He will get one when he gives me the empties.”

“You can wait here for him.”

“I cannot wait for his brother as well.”

The buyer stood. They knew each other well enough to quarrel over who had promised what. I had been hoping that would make the next question easier.

“Are you taking anyone down the coast?” I asked.

The man at the stern looked at me, then at the buyer. “Who is he?”

“He moved my baskets,” the buyer said.

“Then he can move baskets.”

“I can work aboard,” I said.

“Can you make a jar lighter?”

I thought he was mocking me. He shifted the mat aside and showed me the space between the rows. There was barely room for a man's foot, and even that space disappeared when the hull moved. A boy sitting forward held a coil of binding in his lap. His knees were drawn up against the nearest jar.

“We have to lift these one by one,” the man said. “If one falls, I answer for it. If I put you there, where does he sit?”

The boy did not look up. He had already heard enough to know the answer concerned him.

“I could take his place at the pole,” I said.

“And where will he go?”

I looked again at the boat. I had been counting the men as if they were cargo that could be exchanged.

Sulad came down the path with our jar. He stopped beside me but said nothing.

“We could bring food,” I told the man.

“Do you have food?”

“We have eaten.”

“That is not what I asked.”

The buyer bent to inspect the smaller jar. I could feel Sulad waiting for me to answer. The last rice from our breakfast was gone. Our own jar held water for the day, not for a voyage.

“No,” I said.

The man drew the mat back across the cargo. “Then you have nothing for this boat.”

“Could I earn a place by helping you unload?”

“These jars belong to a man farther along the water. He has already paid for their carrying. If I take you instead of his goods, whose payment do I return?”

“I could pay after.”

“After what?”

“After we reach the next place.”

“Where is that?”

I had been about to say Brunei. The word stayed in my mouth. I did not know where this boat would go after it delivered the jars. I did not even know whether it would leave the waterway.

“I don't know,” I said.

The man turned back to the buyer. He was finished with me.

Sulad set our jar down on the hard ground, out of the way of the men coming ashore.

“You asked whether we could go with him,” he said quietly. “You haven't asked where.”

“I was going to.”

“You offered to pay after we arrived.”

“I said I could.”

“With what?”

The boy in the boat pulled the binding tighter around his knees. The man at the stern called to him, and they began easing the smaller jar toward the edge. Neither waited for my answer.

The buyer caught my eye.

“Come back when he has finished with the empties,” he said. “I know someone who needs a pair of hands before the light goes. That is work. I am not telling you it buys a voyage.”

“How long will he be?”

The buyer looked at the jar being lifted between the men.

“As long as it takes not to break it.”

I watched them carry it past us. There was a narrow space in the boat where the jar had been. For a moment it looked large enough for a person. Then the boy moved into it, freeing his legs at last.

The man at the stern would not let the last full jar leave until someone brought the empty ones.

I watched the buyer argue with him from the shade. Each time the buyer pointed toward the houses, the man put his hand on the jar and shook his head. The boy aboard had loosened the binding around it, but he did not lift it. His place between the other jars was too narrow for him to turn.

“You said someone needed hands,” I reminded the buyer when he came back.

“I did.”

“Was it him?”

“He has hands. He needs his jars returned.”

The buyer led me behind the screen to a woman seated beside four empty jars. One lay on its side. She was drawing a thin cord through the carrying sling of another, replacing a loop that had frayed through. A shallow bowl stood near her knee, dark with water and grit.

“These must go down before the light fails,” the buyer said.

The woman did not look up. “Three must. The fourth has to be washed.”

“I brought you someone.”

“You brought me a mouth.”

“I can carry,” I said.

She pulled the cord tight and set the jar upright. “They all say that when the jars are empty.”

The buyer returned to his baskets. I had expected him to stay and tell her that I had worked for him. He left me with the proof I could give her myself.

“What will you pay?” I asked.

“One meal when they reach the boat.”

“How much food?”

“Enough for one person. I have no reason to feed a second.”

I looked toward Sulad. He had remained near our water jar, watching a man repair the edge of a woven mat. He was close enough to hear if I called, but I had no work to offer him that he had agreed to take.

The woman followed my glance.

“If you need two carriers, ask him yourself,” she said.

“I can carry one at a time.”

“Then you will walk four times. The man at the boat has already waited for the buyer's brother. He will not wait for you.”

I bent to examine the jar lying on its side. A pale line crossed its shoulder. I ran my thumb along it and felt the edge of a crack.

“This one is broken.”

“It holds nothing now.”

“If it breaks when I lift it, will you still pay?”

She set down her cord.

“What do you think?”

“I think it was broken before I touched it.”

“Then do not touch it until we agree what you are carrying.”

I had meant to impress her by finding the flaw. She had found the greater flaw in my question.

She turned the jar so that the crack faced the path. There was an old dark stain below it, where liquid had once seeped through. She pointed to the three sound jars and made me lift each one an inch off the ground. Their slings held. The fourth she lifted herself, with both hands beneath its body.

“You carry these three,” she said. “I will carry this one. If you break a sound jar, you tell me before anyone puts it aboard.”

“And the meal?”

“When the three are there.”

“Could I have some now?”

She looked at my hands. The cuts from yesterday's baskets had dried into narrow ridges.

“If I feed you now, what brings you back after the first trip?”

“My word.”

She said nothing. I could hear the buyer counting again on the other side of the screen.

“I carried his baskets yesterday,” I said.

“He paid you?”

“In fish.”

“Then he knows whether you finished.”

She called to him. He did not come over.

“Did he finish?” she asked.

“He did,” the buyer answered without turning. “After I showed him where the last one belonged.”

The woman nodded. It was less praise than I had hoped for, but she had asked a question and received an answer.

“Three jars,” she said. “One meal. I will see them placed aboard.”

I took the first sling in both hands. The jar was light enough that I could have carried it against my chest, but its mouth struck my forearm each time I walked. By the second bend in the path I had learned to hold the bottom away from my body and keep the sling short.

At the landing the man at the stern was still waiting. He saw the jar and pointed to a space beside the bow.

“Not there,” the boy said. “The edge will strike the other one.”

I stopped. The boy crawled between the rows and shifted a coil of binding with his foot. He showed me where to lower it.

The man at the stern inspected the sling before taking it. “Two more?”

“Three,” I said. “She is bringing the cracked one herself.”

He glanced uphill. “Tell her I won't count it as a sound jar.”

“She knows.”

“Then tell her I said it.”

I climbed the path again. Sulad was still by the mat-mender, now holding one edge while the man drew a new strip through it.

“Is he paying you?” I asked.

“He is showing me how he binds the corners.”

“Will that feed us?”

“Not yet.”

I wanted to tell him about the meal. Instead I asked whether he would keep our water jar clear of the path while I made the next trip.

“Yes,” he said. “I was already doing that.”

The woman was washing the cracked jar with a handful of wet leaves. She looked at my empty hands.

“One aboard?”

“One.”

“Did he count it?”

“He checked the sling.”

“Good. Take the next.”

I lifted it. Down at the water the boat rocked against the pole, and the boy called for the men ashore to give him room. I had asked the man to take me with him, and he had refused. Now I was helping him leave sooner.

For a moment I considered setting the jar down and asking again.

The woman was watching from the path. The meal had not been earned, and the man at the stern had not changed his answer. I tightened my hold on the sling and went down to the boat.

The second jar reached the boat without breaking. By the time I came back for the third, the woman had finished washing the cracked one. She had put it on a folded leaf mat, away from the three she had asked me to carry.

“Will that one go too?” I asked.

“I will take it.”

“He says he won't count it as sound.”

“He has eyes. So have I.”

She put both hands beneath its belly. I took the third sound jar by its sling and followed her down. She walked more slowly than I did, not because the jar was heavy but because she could not afford to let it strike a stone. I wanted to hurry her. The boat was already turning with the water, and I could see the boy leaning out to watch us.

At the bank the man at the stern inspected my jar before he let me put it aboard. He touched the sling where the new binding crossed the old. Then he made the boy shift a coil so that the jar would not strike its neighbor.

“Three,” I said.

“Three,” he agreed.

The woman held out the cracked fourth. He did not take it.

“You said you would return the empties,” he told her.

“I said I would bring them. This one is cracked.”

“Then it is worth less than when I left it.”

“It was cracked before he touched it,” she said, nodding toward me.

“I didn't say he cracked it.”

They argued about the fourth jar while the boy held the third in place. I had finished my part. I stayed because the woman was still holding something she could not put down, and because I did not want her to forget the meal.

She noticed me watching.

“Go up,” she said. “Your food is beside the screen. I left it covered.”

“You said when the three reached the boat.”

“They have reached it.”

I looked at the jar in her arms.

“This one isn't mine to carry,” she said. “Don't make it yours.”

I went up alone.

Under the screen lay a leaf packet weighted with a smooth stone. Inside was a handful of rice and a piece of fish smaller than the two the buyer had given me that morning. I had worked through the hottest part of the day for it. The rice was still warm enough to cling together when I pressed it.

I sat where I could see the water. The man at the stern finally took the cracked jar, but he set it apart from the others. The woman stayed beside the boat while he made a mark on a small piece of wood. I could not hear what they agreed. When she came back, her hands were empty.

Sulad was beside the man who had been mending the mat. The two of them were examining a corner where the binding had pulled through. Sulad held it taut while the man worked a fresh strip beneath it. Neither looked toward my packet.

I ate a little rice, then folded the leaf over the rest.

“Did you get paid?” Sulad asked when he joined me.

“Yes.”

“For three?”

“For three.”

He looked toward the boat. Its crew had begun lifting the poles clear of the mud. The boy was sitting again between the jars.

“And the fourth?”

“She took it herself.”

“Good.”

I held out the packet. “There is some left.”

“Did she give you food for two?”

“No.”

“Then eat what you earned.”

I had expected him to take it. I had also expected him to be hungry. He was both hungry and unwilling to let my packet become an agreement between us.

“I can share it,” I said.

“You can.”

He sat beside me but did not reach for the food. I broke off a piece of fish and placed it on the leaf between us. After a while he took it. We ate without deciding whose hunger was greater.

The boat pushed away. I watched the empty space between its rows close as the boy stretched out his legs. I had carried the jars that made it ready to leave. Nothing in our bargain had said I would go with it.

The buyer came back to his counting board as the light began to move off the screen. He saw the folded leaf in my hand.

“You have eaten,” he said.

“I have.”

“Then you were paid.”

I wanted to ask whether he knew a boat that would take us. He had already told me what he knew about the one now leaving. Instead I asked when he would be at the landing again.

“When there is work.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Most mornings.”

“May I ask you then?”

“You may ask. I may have nothing to tell you.”

Sulad rose and went to fetch our water jar. I watched the buyer return to his baskets. He did not look toward the departing boat, and he did not ask what I would do with the remaining daylight.

I had earned two meals since morning. The silver was still gone. The jar vessel had left without us. We would have to ask the woman for another night beneath her roof, and I did not know what work she would want in return.

Sulad came back with the jar against his hip.

“Where now?” he asked.

I looked toward the house above the landing. Its roof was still visible between the trees.

“To ask,” I said.

He started up the path. This time I followed without saying what answer we would get.

* * *

The woman was taking down the screen when we reached her house.

I stopped beside the step. The screen had been tied to the post that morning, and beneath it was the ground where Sulad and I had slept. She had lifted one end clear and was rolling it toward the wall.

“Has the roof come loose again?” I asked.

“No.”

“Then why are you taking it down?”

“My sister is coming back. Her things need to be dry.”

She rested the roll against her knee and reached for the cord. I had come prepared to ask what work would buy another night. There was no room for the question in what she had said.

“When?” Sulad asked.

“She sent word she would be here by morning. She may come before.”

The child sat on the step, sorting three shells into a leaf. He looked up when I moved toward the eaves, then went back to the shells.

“We could sleep beside the path,” I said.

“You could sleep wherever the people who own that ground allow it.”

“I meant here.”

“I know what you meant.”

She wound the cord around the screen. It slipped from her fingers and dropped into the dust. Sulad bent to pick it up, but she was already reaching for it herself. He waited until she had straightened.

“May I help with the screen?” he asked.

“Yes. Hold the end away from the post.”

He did. I took the other end when she asked me, and we carried it into the house. Her sister's rolled mat lay against the wall. The woman made us stand the screen beside it, where the child could not pull it over.

When we came out, the space under the roof looked wider and less like a place to sleep.

“We filled your jars,” I said.

“Yesterday.”

“And the binding is holding.”

“I can see that.”

I wanted her to say what the work had earned. She looked past me to the darkening path.

“You may stay there tonight,” she said, “if my sister has not arrived by the time you lie down. If she comes, you make room. At first light you take your things away, whether she has come or not.”

“What shall we do for you?”

“Nothing tonight. I have water. The roof is tied.”

“Then why let us stay?”

“Because I said you could ask again. You have asked.”

I could have thanked her. Instead I said, “What if she comes while we're asleep?”

“I will wake you.”

She lifted the child and went inside. I stood beneath the bare edge of the roof. There was no screen now to keep a wind from reaching our faces.

Sulad set our jar where the woman had told us to keep it, away from the door.

“You could have stopped after she said yes,” he told me.

“I wanted to know what she expected.”

“She told you.”

“She might change her mind.”

“She might.”

He sat on the hard ground and turned his right hand over. A strip of leaf was caught in the cut beside his thumb. He worked it loose with a fingernail.

“Where did you get that?” I asked.

“At the mat.”

“Helping the man?”

“Watching him. Then he let me try.”

I remembered seeing the two of them bent over a loose corner while I carried the jars. I had thought Sulad was passing time until I finished.

“He asked me to come back tomorrow,” Sulad said.

“To mend mats?”

“To learn to mend them properly. He has three to finish before the next market. He'll give me food for the work I can do, after he sees whether I can do it.”

“For how long?”

“He said to come before the sun reaches his screen. He didn't ask me to stay three days. I asked how much work there was.”

“Would he take me too?”

“He didn't ask about you.”

I felt the same heat I had felt when the jar boat refused us. The man had seen Sulad's hands, not mine. I had been carrying jars for food while Sulad found a way to be useful without waiting for me to arrange it.

“You didn't tell me,” I said.

“I am telling you.”

“We're trying to find a boat.”

“You are. I said I would come this far.”

“You said you might go home.”

“I still might.”

The child called from inside. The woman answered him without coming to the doorway. A pot touched the ground, then scraped against it.

“Would you stay here and work?” I asked.

“I haven't decided.”

“And if I find passage tomorrow?”

“Then you can tell me what the person aboard has agreed to.”

I wanted to say that if I found a boat he would have to decide quickly. But there was no boat, and he had already found a man willing to speak about tomorrow.

“Does the mat-mender know where you would sleep?” I asked.

“No. I didn't ask him to feed me at night or put a roof over me.”

“Then what good is the work?”

Sulad looked at the jar. “It might be good work.”

He got up and walked toward the path.

“Where are you going?”

“To tell him whether I will come in the morning.”

“You said you hadn't decided.”

“I can ask him when he needs an answer.”

I followed him as far as the bend. Below, the landing was losing its color. The buyer had covered his baskets. The mat-mender sat beside his screen, working a strip through the corner Sulad had held earlier.

Sulad spoke to him. I was too far away to hear. The man put down the strip, turned the mat so its loose corner faced Sulad, and showed him something with two fingers. Sulad tried the movement. The man made him undo it and try again.

I waited for Sulad to look back. He did not.

A boy came past me carrying a shallow tray on his head. I stepped into the path to let him through. He looked toward the house above us.

“Is the woman there?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Tell her the sister is on the way. I saw her at the far path.”

“Tonight?”

He shrugged under the tray. “She was walking.”

He went on before I could ask how far she had come. I climbed back to the house and gave the woman the message. She did not seem surprised.

“Then move your bundles to the side,” she said. “And keep the doorway clear.”

I did. There was room under the eaves for both bundles, but I left a space between them in case we had to pick them up quickly. When Sulad returned, I told him about the boy.

“The mat-mender said I can come tomorrow,” he said. “If I am there before he begins.”

“Will you?”

“I said I would answer in the morning.”

“You'll lose the work.”

“I might.”

He put the jar beside his bundle and sat. I could see the landing through the trees. I had spent the day trying to get aboard a boat that could not carry us. Sulad had spent part of it learning a knot that another man wanted him to make again.

I lay down with my feet toward the path. Every time someone came through the trees, I raised my head to see whether it was the sister. The woman did not call us inside, and no one came to take our place before the light was gone.

* * *

The sister came after the woman had put out her lamp.

I heard her before I saw her: feet dragging through the leaves, then the scrape of something set down against the step. Our host opened the door. In the thin light I saw a woman with a rolled cloth under one arm and mud to her knees. She looked first at the child asleep inside, then at the two shapes beneath the eaves.

“Who are they?”

“Two who carried water,” our host said. “They have been here since yesterday.”

“Are they staying?”

“Not now.”

The sister rested her forehead against the doorpost. I had been ready to argue that we had been promised the night. She had walked far enough to be leaning on the house to stand.

Sulad was already folding his cloth. I picked up my bundle, then reached for the water jar. Its base knocked the post, and the child stirred inside.

“Careful,” our host said.

I held it clear. The sister stepped aside to let us pass. She did not thank us or apologize. She had come home.

At the turn in the path I looked back. The woman was unrolling the mat we had not been allowed to touch. Her sister sat on the step while she worked. Neither was looking after us.

“Where will we sleep?” I asked.

“Where we can,” Sulad said.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the one we have.”

The landing had grown smaller in the dark. The buyer's baskets were covered, and the fisherman had taken his net away. A lamp burned beside the mat-mender's screen. He sat cross-legged with a length of leaf between his teeth, trying to hold a torn edge flat against his knee.

Sulad stopped.

“You said you would answer him in the morning,” I reminded him.

“He is still working.”

The man took the strip from his mouth when he saw us. “Did the roof send you away?”

“The woman's sister came home,” Sulad said.

“Then the roof belongs to her again.”

He moved a rolled mat with his foot to make room beside the screen. It was not an invitation to sleep there. He pointed at the edge in his hands.

“This one has to go before the light comes. I can mend it alone, but I would rather not.”

Sulad crouched. The man gave him the strip and watched him make the first turn. Sulad pulled too sharply. The woven edge bent out of line.

“Undo it,” the man said.

Sulad did. The man showed him how to hold the old binding with his thumb while drawing the new strip through. I stood behind them with our jar, listening to the water touch the bank.

A woman came up from the shallows carrying a short pole. She set it against the screen and looked at the mats.

“How many are ready?”

“Two,” the mender said.

“I was told three.”

“You were told I would try for three.”

She pressed the heel of her hand against the mat Sulad was holding. Its edge lifted.

“This one will open if it gets wet.”

“Then let him finish it.”

“Who is he?”

“A man who may have good hands.”

She looked at me. “And him?”

“I carried jars today,” I said.

“That is not what I asked.”

I told her my name. She gave hers, but I lost the first sound beneath a shout from the water. When I asked her to repeat it, she did, without slowing the rest of her speech. I did not pretend I had caught more than her name.

She had come for the mats. Her brother and another man were holding a narrow boat near the bank. I could see its dark shape between the poles. Two bundles already lay across the middle, leaving an open stretch near the stern.

“Where are you taking them?” I asked.

“To the next landing where they are wanted.”

“And after that?”

“After that I will see what there is to carry.”

“Could you take two people?”

She looked at the jar against my leg, then at Sulad, who had not stopped working.

“Two people with what?”

“This. Our bundles.”

“Food?”

“We can work.”

“That was not my question.”

I had heard those words from the jar carrier earlier in the day. I made myself answer them this time.

“We have no food for tomorrow.”

“Then you have no food to take aboard.”

She bent over the mats. “I have room for two bodies if the load stays as it is. I do not have food for two more mouths. I will not take a promise of food from someone who has none.”

“Could we earn it before you leave?”

“You can carry those bundles to my boat. They must be kept above the water. At the next landing you can carry them ashore. If you do both, I can take you that far. I have food for my crew, not for you.”

The mat-mender cleared his throat.

“I owe this one food if he finishes the edge,” he said, pointing at Sulad. “Not before.”

The woman looked at the strip between Sulad's fingers. “And the other?”

“I can carry the mats,” I said.

“You can carry them when they are finished. If I need another hand for the load, I can give you a portion from what I have ashore. But if you eat it now, you cannot eat it tomorrow.”

“Then I will keep it.”

“People say that when they are full.”

“I am not full.”

She examined my hands. The cuts from the baskets had opened again while I carried the jars. She took one wrist, turned it toward the lamp, and let go.

“Can you lift without dropping what belongs to me?”

“I have carried six baskets and three jars.”

“Did you break any?”

“No.”

“Who can tell me that?”

“The buyer.”

“He is asleep.”

“He will be here in the morning.”

“He may be. I will not wait for him to praise you.”

The mat-mender pointed toward the screen. “I saw him bring the jars down. The woman who owned them paid him. I did not see him break one.”

It was a smaller account of my day than I would have given. The boat woman accepted it as far as it went.

“One bundle now,” she said. “The second when he finishes. If you cannot carry them without letting the mat drag, I will use my brother. The food is for the work, whether or not we sail. The passage is to the next landing only, if we leave and there is still room after my own goods are aboard.”

“And if you take other goods?” I asked.

“Then I tell you before you load mine. I will not make you work for a place I have already given away.”

I wanted her to say she would refuse other goods. She had said something I could test.

“Will you take us both if there is room?”

She turned to Sulad. “Do you want to go?”

He held the strip between his thumb and finger. The mat-mender had let go of the edge so that Sulad had to keep it straight himself.

“I want to finish this first,” he said.

The woman gave a short laugh. “That is the first sensible answer I have had tonight.”

“It isn't an answer about the boat,” I said.

“It will be his,” she replied.

I carried the first bundle down. The mat was longer than my arms. I held one end against my chest and lifted the other high enough to keep it from the damp ground. At the bank the woman's brother showed me where to set it across the supports. He would not let me lower it until the second man had moved a pole clear.

“Now,” he said.

I put it down. The bundle did not shift.

When I returned, Sulad had finished the torn corner. The mender ran a finger along the binding, found a loose turn and made him tighten it. Then he folded a leaf packet and placed it beside Sulad's knee.

“For that edge,” he said. “If you stay, I have two more tomorrow.”

Sulad did not reach for the packet immediately.

“Will you need me before the sun is high?” he asked.

“Before it touches the screen.”

“And if I go?”

“Then I mend them myself, or find someone else.”

He looked at the man for a long time. I had expected him to choose the boat because I wanted it. The packet beside his knee was proof that another choice existed.

“I will take the work I have done,” Sulad said. “I won't promise tomorrow.”

The mender pushed the packet toward him. “I did not pay you for tomorrow.”

Sulad picked it up and came to where I stood. He looked at the boat, then at me.

“One landing,” he said. “I will go one landing if the woman agrees to take us both. After that you ask me again.”

I felt an answer rise in my mouth, something about how far we still had to travel. I swallowed it.

“One landing,” I said.

We told the woman together. She made each of us repeat the work we would do: I would help load the last bundle and unload both at the next landing; Sulad would help keep the mats clear of water and unload them, but he would not be put at a pole he did not know how to use. Our jar could come if it was tied down. We would keep the food we had earned. She owed us no further passage, and if the boat could not leave, we would still be paid for the work already done.

“Be here before the light reaches that tree,” she said, pointing above the screen. “If the water is wrong, we wait. If you are late, we do not.”

The second bundle was ready. We carried it together, one at each end. Sulad walked backward until the path narrowed, then made me stop while we changed sides. The woman watched from the bank without calling for us to hurry.

When the second bundle was secured, she fetched a leaf packet from beside the screen and put it in my hand. “For the carrying,” she said. “You have earned that whether we leave or not.” I kept it closed. I would need it in the morning.

Afterward we sat near the screen with the jar between us. The mat-mender had gone inside. Sulad divided his packet, wrapped one portion again for morning, and placed the other in my hand. I tucked it inside my cloth without opening it. The boat rocked once against its pole and settled.

There was no roof over us. I could not see the next landing, and the woman had not told us what lay beyond it. But I knew which bundle I had carried, what she had promised, and when she would leave.

Sulad lay down with his back toward the water. His hands were still moving in the dark, making the turn the mat-mender had taught him.
