The first thing the river brought back was a broken reed mat.

It came sideways along the bank, caught on the sounding stake, and spread open under the pull of the water. Two boys went after it from the higher path. One took off his shirt and wound it around his hand before reaching down. The other told him to leave it. Neither listened to me when I called. The mat tore at its middle, and the loose half passed between their feet and went downstream.

The tide had not yet begun to fall.

Behind them the small boat was coming toward the upper step. I knew its crewman by the way he stood at the bow with one knee against the gunwale. He had brought his paddle back. The owner was not with him.

There was another man at the stern, older, his back bent over the steering oar. Between them stood three covered jars and two baskets with their mouths sewn shut. The jars had been set on wet leaves so they would not knock together. A woman on the bank called out when she saw the baskets.

"Keep those dry. The buyer will turn away if the covers split."

The crewman lifted his hand to show he had heard. Then he pointed at the step.

I stepped down to the old landing and stood where he could see me.

"Stay off the new wood."

He put his paddle across the bow.

"We have come back."

"I see that."

"He said to bring the other load before the water turned."

"Where is he?"

"Upstream. He took the first payment to the man who owns the jars."

The older steersman eased the oar. The boat slid sideways until its bow faced the current again. A line had been coiled on the forward plank. The crewman picked it up.

"Not the sounding stake," I said.

"I know what it is."

He had spoken sharply, but he lowered the line.

The woman came down from the path. She was carrying a folded cover under her arm and had a smear of dark oil along her wrist. I had seen her once at the hall buying cord. She looked from me to the boat without greeting either of us.

"Why have you stopped them?"

"The step has not been inspected."

"It held yesterday."

"It held a smaller load yesterday, before the water came over it again."

"These are the same jars."

"They are not the same jars," the crewman said. "The ones yesterday went on the first trip."

The woman made a small impatient sound. "I mean the same kind. Three jars, not a house."

The boat rose against a wave left by a passing canoe. The jars shifted together. The older man put his bare foot against one and held it there until the hull settled.

I could see the new step through the brown water. Its pale upper edge was gone. The old post still stood above it, dark and swollen at the foot. The soft bank beneath the repair had been cut away in a narrow curl. I could not tell whether the piece Raya had braced was moving or whether the water only made it seem so.

"Raya said she would look after the next high water," I told the woman.

"Then call her."

"She is at her house."

"Do you know where it is?"

"Yes."

"Then why are you standing here?"

The crewman laughed once, without pleasure.

"Because he will tell us not to tie until the owner comes," he said. "Yesterday they told me to fetch him. Today he is on the river, and we have brought what he sent us to bring."

"Did your owner tell you that these jars had been paid for?" I asked.

"He told me we had two journeys."

"That is what you told us yesterday."

"Then you heard me."

He leaned forward. The basket nearest his knee moved with him. I saw that its cover was sewn with two different colors of fiber, one old and one new. Someone had spent the morning closing it. The woman followed my glance.

"Those are wax cakes. If the sun reaches them before they are in the shade, they will soften against the weave. I have a man waiting at the market path. He has to leave before the noon meal."

"Who agreed to carry them?" I asked.

She looked at the crewman.

"The boat's owner."

"At what price?"

"That is between us."

"Was it paid?"

"No. He was to take his share after delivery."

The crewman shook his head.

"He said this was the second journey of the payment already made."

The woman stared at him.

"Made by whom?"

"By the woman yesterday. Two baskets."

"Those were her baskets. These are mine."

He opened his mouth, then closed it. The older man at the stern looked over his shoulder toward the river bend, where their owner had gone.

I remembered the bamboo on the keeper's table. One long cut. Two short ones. I had watched the owner put his thumb on each of the shorter marks. I had thought that was enough.

"Bring the boat outside the old post," I said. "Do not put the line on it yet."

The older man turned the oar and held the boat in the slower water. It cost him effort. His shoulders moved beneath the thin cloth he wore, and his toes searched for purchase on the wet floor.

"How long?" he asked.

"I will fetch Raya."

"How long will that take?"

"I don't know."

He looked at the crewman, who looked back at me.

"If I take this boat home with the goods," the young man said, "he will say I refused a journey. If I stay here until the buyer goes, he will say I lost his payment. Which one will you tell him happened?"

"I will tell him what I saw."

"You did that yesterday."

I had no answer that would make the work easier for him.

The woman pushed past me to the end of the old landing. Her foot struck a loose stone. It went into the water with a soft, heavy sound.

"Can they unload at the upper path?" she asked.

"That path belongs to the households beside it. Dala uses the ground below for her nets. We have no leave to make it a landing."

"Then ask her."

"She is working."

"Everyone is working. So am I."

A man carrying a basket on his shoulder stopped behind us. He had been heading toward the hall. The woman called to him and asked if he would carry the jars from the boat if they could be brought ashore. He named a price. She named another. They stood apart for a moment, measuring each other. The man shifted his basket and said he could not wait while we argued over where his feet would go.

He left.

The woman watched him climb the path. She had not called him back before he disappeared.

The older steersman moved the boat out of the current again. His heel slid. The crewman caught the oar handle before it struck the jars.

"You cannot keep us holding here," the older man said.

"I know."

"Then let us use the post."

"The post is for a boat that has permission to land. If you tie there, the next one cannot come in."

"No one else is here."

"Not yet."

He looked at the empty water, then at the bank. He was old enough to know how little an empty river promised.

The crewman pulled the line through his hands, paying out one arm's length and gathering it back. He was not trying to defy me. He was making sure it had not caught beneath the baskets.

"I was told to come twice," he said more quietly. "I came the first time. I have come the second. Whatever the marks say, that is the work I was given."

"Who pays you for waiting?"

"The owner."

"Did he say so?"

"He pays me for taking the boat where he sends it."

"That is not the same thing."

"Then perhaps you can explain the difference to him when he comes."

The woman lowered her cover onto the dry part of the path. She opened it, folded it again, and made a shade with it over the nearest basket. Her fingers were quick, but she had to reach from the old landing, and the bow kept drawing away from her. At last she took the cover back.

"Where is the keeper?" she asked.

"In the hall."

"Bring the strip."

"The owner has not come to say what he heard."

"I want to see what was cut."

"The keeper will show it. She will not let it be changed."

"I didn't ask her to change it."

She walked up the path. The crewman watched her go.

"She thinks we made the marks," he said.

"I know who made them."

"Then tell her."

"I will."

He turned his face toward the stern, where the older man had begun to breathe through his mouth. The current was running harder now. The older man's feet were braced against opposite sides of the boat. His left hand trembled as he held the oar.

I went down to the water's edge and took hold of the line the crewman threw me. I could not tie it to the new step or the sounding stake. I stood with it around my forearm and let the boat come closer until the older man could rest his shoulders.

The pull was stronger than I expected. The rope burned where it moved over my skin. Mud slipped beneath my heel. I set my other foot against the old landing and felt the boat's weight pass through my arms.

"Do not let it pull you in," the crewman said.

"Then keep the bow into the water."

"We are trying."

For a little while no one spoke. The old post was close enough that I could have thrown a turn around it. I did not. The boat was waiting, and I had told them to wait, so I held the line myself.

A girl from the woman's household came running with a message. She stopped at the top of the path when she saw me standing in the mud.

"The buyer has left the shade," she said. "He is going to the far bank."

"Tell her," I said.

"She knows. She sent me to find out if the jars have come."

"They haven't."

The girl looked at the boat. It was almost touching the old landing. One basket was no farther from her than a grown person's reach. She could have stepped down and touched it, but she did not.

"Will they come before he crosses?" she asked.

"I cannot promise that."

She turned and ran.

The crewman saw her go. He had not heard every word, but he knew what it meant.

"Now she will say we were late."

"She may."

"We were here."

"I saw you."

"Then say that when he comes."

"I will."

He rubbed his thumb against the rope where it passed through his hands. His skin was already raw. The older man looked at me and nodded toward the bank.

"You have held us long enough to learn the weight," he said. "Let us go to the bend where the water is quiet. We can wait there until you have someone who may answer."

The crewman did not want to move. I could see it in the way he kept his knees fixed against the planks. But the older man had already turned the oar.

I let the line run out slowly and gave the last of it back. The boat swung away. The jars knocked once and were still.

"I will fetch Raya," I called.

"Fetch our owner too," the crewman said.

"I cannot find him if I don't know where he has gone."

"That is what I have been telling you."

The boat moved beyond the stake. The torn reed mat was gone. Nothing in the water marked where it had caught.

When I reached the hall, the keeper had put the disputed strip on a dry board. The woman was standing across from her with both hands pressed against the table. She had already explained the jars and the buyer.

"Did you let the boat in?" the keeper asked.

"No."

"Good."

"I held its line while they waited."

She looked at the wet mark around my forearm.

"Then you know what the crewman was doing."

"Yes."

The woman lifted her hands from the table.

"My buyer is crossing to the other bank. Your marks have cost me a sale."

The keeper touched the long cut with one finger.

"These marks were for yesterday's journey."

"I know that now," the woman said. "The man on the boat does not."

She turned to me.

"Will you tell the owner that his crew came when he ordered them? Will you tell him I brought the goods and did not refuse to pay?"

"Yes."

"And will you tell my buyer to wait?"

I looked at the strip, then at the light coming through the doorway. The buyer was already on his way. I had spent the morning holding a rope while the people who needed an answer were somewhere else.

"I can go after him," I said. "I cannot make him stay."

"Then go," she said.

The keeper kept the strip on the table. I went out with the wet rope mark still rising on my arm.

* * *

By the time I found the man who had wanted the jars, he had already paid to cross.

He stood on the far side of the landing with one foot in a boat and the other on a stone. I called from our bank. He turned his head but did not come back. I could see the pole-handler waiting behind him, his pole planted in the mud.

"The jars are still here," I said.

"So am I," he answered.

"Only for a moment."

He looked toward the woman who had followed me down the path. She raised a hand.

"Come back after the water falls," she called. "I will send them by a smaller boat."

"I have paid for this one."

"I will pay the difference."

He glanced at the pole-handler, who made no movement. The man took his foot off the stone.

"You said they would be here before the water rose."

"I did."

"Then I cannot wait until it falls."

He stepped into the boat. The pole-handler pushed away. I watched them cross while the woman beside me counted the jars aloud, as though saying the number might keep them from being lost.

"Three," she said. "Three jars. Two baskets. None delivered."

"I saw them."

"I know you saw them. You have been watching all morning."

She turned away before I could answer. At the hall she took her cover from the table, shook dust from it, and folded it under her arm. The keeper did not ask her to stay. The woman's bargain with the boat owner had been made without the keeper, and she had no reason to wait for our quarrel to end.

The small-boat owner came after the midday heat had begun to break. He arrived on foot from the upper path, carrying a cord bag in one hand and a wet sandal in the other. His name was Tawan. I had heard it when he paid for the repaired step's first trial, but I had not used it since. He had been the one to give Raya the cloth for Madan's timber.

He saw the bamboo strip on the table before he saw me.

"Has he brought the second load?"

"He brought it."

"Where is the boat?"

"Beyond the bend."

"Why?"

"The step has not been cleared after the high water. Raya must look at it again."

He set the sandal on the floor.

"Then why did you tell him to come?"

"I did not."

"I told him to come. You told me there were two journeys."

The keeper was sitting on a low stool near the doorway. She rose when he said that. She did not touch the strip.

"I said there were two baskets," she answered.

Tawan looked at her with the patience of a man who had already decided that patience would be required.

"You said two. I put my hand on the two short cuts. You held the strip where I could see it."

"Those cuts were for the baskets."

"You made them while we spoke of the boat."

"We spoke of a journey carrying two baskets. The woman paid you for that journey."

"And why would I be paid for a journey I had already made?"

"You had not yet made it," the keeper said.

He turned to me.

"Did I touch the two cuts?"

"Yes."

"Did she say there was no second journey?"

"She said, 'Nothing here promises another journey.' I heard her."

He looked down at his broken sandal. The keeper had spoken at the landing with the strip against her knee, after Raya and Dala had given their conditions. The boat had begun to draw against its line. I had stood close enough to hear every word. I had seen him touch the short cuts and had assumed he heard them too.

"I did not hear that last part," he said. "The line was moving. I thought the two cuts were for the two turns I had asked about."

"You should have asked again," I said.

"So should you. You saw me touch them."

He took the sandal from the floor and worked a finger through its broken thong. It had been mended once with dark fiber. The new break was beside the old knot.

"I did not come here to take another woman's baskets," he said. "I came to see why my crew was told to wait without a place to tie. He has been holding the boat since morning. He says you sent him away."

"I did."

"On whose word?"

"On the keeper's account and the cargo owner's payment."

"Did you ask mine?"

"You were gone."

"That was why I left the crewman. He was to take the boat back."

The keeper held the strip toward him by its uncut end.

"Look at it again. One long cut for one journey. Two short cuts for the goods."

He did not take it.

"Those are the marks you say you made. I know what I heard."

The keeper lowered the strip. For the first time I saw her look at me rather than at him.

"You stood with us," she said.

"I did."

"You saw him touch the marks."

"I did."

"And you did not ask him to say what he understood."

"No."

Tawan looked from one of us to the other. The room had become very quiet. Outside, a child struck a pot with a stick and was told to stop.

"Fetch the woman who paid," I said.

Tawan sat down on the threshold.

"I will wait."

The keeper sent a boy to the house where the cargo owner had gone after yesterday's delivery. I went toward the bend to find the crewman. Tawan followed me, carrying his sandal. He walked faster than I expected, and I had to step aside when the path narrowed.

The boat was in a pocket of quiet water below the roots of a large tree. The older steersman had set the oar across the gunwales. He was sitting with his knees drawn up, his eyes closed. The crewman stood on the bank, trying to work a splinter from his palm with the edge of a shell.

"Did you bring him?" the crewman asked when he saw us.

"I came myself," Tawan said.

The young man held out his hand. The splinter was still there.

"I brought the jars. They sent me away."

"I know."

"The buyer has gone."

"I know that too."

Tawan took the shell from him, held his wrist against the light, and lifted the splinter free. The crewman winced but did not pull back. The owner gave him the shell again.

"You did what I told you," he said.

"Then will you pay me for today?"

Tawan's hand stopped in the air.

"I paid you for the first journey."

"And I have worked the second."

"The second was not carried."

"I was here."

The older steersman opened his eyes.

"I have held the boat in the current since morning," he said. "That work does not disappear because we never touched the step."

Tawan rubbed the broken sandal thong between his fingers. He had come ready to accuse us of changing a bargain. His own crew had now brought him a claim he had not prepared to answer.

"I will pay you for the waiting," he said to the older man. "I must first collect what is owed for the jars."

The older man looked at the empty water beyond the bend.

"The jars have not been carried."

"Then I will find another way."

The crewman held up his palm. The small cut where the splinter had been was beginning to bleed.

"Will you pay me too?"

Tawan looked at him.

"Yes. I have said so."

"You said you would pay me for two journeys."

"I said I would pay you for the work."

"Then tell me how much waiting is worth."

The owner did not answer. The older man lowered his feet into the bottom of the boat and leaned forward to examine the line. It had been rubbed raw where it passed over the bow.

I had thought bringing the owner would end the dispute. It had made three disputes visible.

"Come to the hall," I said. "The woman who paid for yesterday's baskets is being fetched."

Tawan picked up his bag.

"Will Raya be there?"

"I have not found her yet."

"Then we will still have no step."

"Not until she says it can be used."

The crewman looked at me. "And if she says it cannot?"

"Then it cannot."

He turned to Tawan.

"You told me they had agreed to let us come twice."

"I believed they had."

"Will you tell the woman that?"

"I will tell her what I heard."

The older man lifted the line and began coiling it. He did not follow us. He had been promised payment for waiting and had no reason to spend the rest of the afternoon hearing the same words.

The woman whose two baskets had gone upriver was at the hall when we returned. She stood beside the keeper, holding the same mat that had covered her goods. A small piece had torn from one corner. She smoothed it while Tawan came through the door.

"I paid you for one journey," she said.

"I carried your baskets."

"You did."

"Then we agree on that."

"We agree that you carried them once. I paid for that."

"You spoke of two."

"Two baskets. I held them both up. You put them beside each other in your boat."

Tawan set his cord bag down.

"I was beside the boat when you said two. The boy was pulling the line. I came closer when the keeper held up the strip. I saw two cuts. I thought they answered the question I had asked."

The keeper looked at him.

"I said one boat, two baskets, after Raya and Dala gave leave. I said nothing there promised another journey."

"I heard the first part. I did not hear the last."

"You touched the marks without asking me to repeat it."

The cargo owner stopped smoothing the mat.

"Whatever either of you heard, I did not buy a second journey for another woman's jars."

Tawan looked at the strip. This time he took it by the uncut end and turned it toward the light. He touched the long cut with one finger and the two short cuts with another.

"I see how you meant it," he said to the keeper. "I did not see it yesterday."

The keeper took back the bamboo.

"Then we should have asked you to tell us."

Tawan nodded, but his face had not softened.

"That would have cost a moment. Today has cost my men a day."

"It has cost me a buyer," the other woman said from the doorway.

She had returned without her cover. I had not heard her approach. Tawan turned toward her.

"I was told you had another load."

"I did. You told me you could carry it. You did not say the journey belonged to someone else's payment."

"I thought the step was ours for two turns."

"It is not yours," the keeper said.

Tawan looked at her sharply.

"I know the wood is not mine. I helped pay for it. I did not buy the bank."

He said it as if the distinction were obvious. I had been ready to accuse him of treating the step as his property. He had just said the opposite.

"Then we have to speak of the new load separately," I said.

The woman folded her arms.

"After I have lost my buyer?"

"I cannot bring him back."

"No. You cannot."

She left again. This time no one called after her.

Tawan stayed beside the table. The cargo owner took her mat and went home, satisfied that no one had asked her to pay twice. The keeper laid the bamboo flat where all three of us could see it.

"I will not cut another mark over these," she said.

"I am not asking you to," Tawan answered. "I want something I can understand before I send a man with my boat."

I had wanted to rule for the keeper when he first arrived. I still thought she had recorded the bargain she and the cargo owner intended. But I had watched Tawan touch the marks. I had seen him leave with a different understanding, and I had been pleased to let him go.

"The crewman heard you say two," I told him. "He did not hear what was carried by that number."

"He was at the bow."

"Yes."

"He was doing his work."

"Yes."

Tawan looked down at the bamboo.

"Then he cannot answer for what we failed to say to each other."

I nodded. It was the first thing that day on which we had all agreed.

The keeper reached for a fresh strip. I put my hand on the table before she could cut it.

"Wait," I said. "We should ask who else has to hear the words."

Outside, the river was falling away from the repaired step. Raya had still not examined it. The crewman's pay had been promised but not counted. The woman who had lost her buyer was already finding another bank.

The keeper set the uncut bamboo beside the first strip and left them both where we could see them.

* * *

The keeper put three stones on the table. One was white, one dark, and one had a thin red vein through it. She had taken them from the path because they were flat enough to stand without rolling.

"Which one is a journey?" she asked.

Tawan looked at the stones and then at me.

"All of them, if I am carrying them."

The keeper moved the dark one aside.

"Suppose this is the boat. These two are baskets."

"Then I carry two baskets," he said.

"How many times?"

"Once, if I have the sense to put them both in."

The crewman had followed us from the bend. He stood near the doorway with the split skin of his palm wrapped in a strip of cloth. When Tawan said that, he gave a short laugh.

"You did not put them both in the first time," he said. "The woman made us take one out to keep it from pressing on the other."

"We carried them in one journey," Tawan said.

"That is what I am saying. One journey. Two loads in the boat, if you count what we lifted."

The keeper pushed the stones together.

"That is what the marks failed to tell you."

"They told me two," Tawan said.

"Because you asked a question we did not hear."

He reached for the uncut bamboo strip. She moved it out of his reach.

"Let him hold it," I said.

"He may hold it when we agree what it is for. The first strip was passed from hand to hand before anyone knew what the other meant."

Tawan looked at me, waiting for me to tell her that he had a right to touch it. I had been ready to do so. I kept my hands on my knees.

The crewman picked up the white stone.

"Here is the journey," he said. He put it at one end of the table. "And these are the baskets." He set the other two beside it.

The keeper nodded.

"Then make one mark for that journey and two for those baskets."

Tawan took a piece of charcoal from the brazier. He drew one line across the table's edge and two shorter lines beneath it.

The crewman leaned close.

"That is the same as yesterday."

Tawan looked at his own marks. The charcoal had broken under his thumb. He rubbed it away with his palm, leaving a black smear in the grain.

"What if we make the journey longer?" I asked.

"We did," the keeper said. "You saw it."

"Then put the marks on different pieces."

The keeper brought two short strips of bamboo. On one she cut a notch. On the other she cut two. She laid them beside the stones.

Tawan took the first strip.

"One journey."

He took the second.

"Two baskets."

"Now tell me what you will carry," the keeper said.

"Two baskets, one journey."

"And when?"

"Before the next high water."

The crewman looked toward the doorway.

"What if Raya has not cleared the step?"

Tawan's fingers tightened around the strips.

"Then we wait."

"Who pays us for waiting?" the crewman asked.

Tawan set the bamboo down.

"We have spoken of that."

"You said you would pay us. You have not said how much."

The keeper reached for a third strip.

Tawan shook his head.

"If you cut a mark for every word we say, we will need a whole tree."

"Then perhaps we should say fewer things."

The crewman did not smile. He had taken the cloth off his hand and was looking at the small red cut in the palm.

"I need to know whether I am paid for holding the boat, or only for arriving where you told me."

Tawan looked away. The keeper left the third strip uncut.

I had wanted the two strips to make the matter simple. The crewman had found another question before I could enjoy the result.

"Could we tie a knot for the waiting?" I asked.

"What does a knot mean?" Tawan said. "One hour? One tide? A meal?"

"A tide," I said.

"And if the water does not fall enough to work?"

"Then another tide."

"Another knot?"

I put the cord down. It had been a poor answer.

The keeper pushed the stones aside. She placed the two strips on the dry board, away from the original tally.

"Let us try it with something that has not been carried," she said.

The crewman went outside and brought back a small basket of empty shells from the path. He set it beside the table. The basket belonged to the woman who collected shell grit for the potters. He had asked her before taking it, and she followed him to the doorway to see what we were doing.

"That is mine," she said.

"We will put it back," he answered.

"Who will?"

"I will."

She nodded once and remained by the door.

The keeper took the basket and moved it to the other end of the table.

"One basket, one journey," she said.

Tawan looked at the two strips. He held up the one with the single cut.

"One journey."

"And the goods?"

He reached for the other strip and hesitated. It had two cuts.

"That one says two baskets."

"So it cannot be used for this basket," the crewman said.

The keeper brought another blank strip.

"Then we make one for this load. But first, whose basket is it?"

The woman at the door raised her hand.

"Mine."

"Who may carry it?"

"The young man asked. I said he could carry it here and bring it back. I did not say he could take it on a boat."

The crewman put down the strip.

"Then I cannot carry it on a boat."

"Correct," the keeper said.

The woman took the basket back. She counted the shells before leaving, not because she thought we had stolen any, but because she had brought them for a purpose and did not want to lose one.

Tawan watched her go.

"Now we must ask the owner of every basket?"

"Before we carry it away," the keeper said.

"That much I understand."

He picked up the original strip and looked at it for a long time. Then he put it down without touching the cuts.

"Yesterday I thought the marks were about my boat. They were about her baskets. I see it now."

The crewman pointed to the door.

"And the path?"

"What path?"

"The path from the boat to the hall. If I carry a basket, where do I put my feet?"

I looked toward the river. The upper step was still wet. Beyond it the narrow strip of ground where Dala set her frame lay in shadow. Her drying rack had been moved back to its old place. There was room for one person to pass if the nets were lifted. I had been thinking of that as a small inconvenience.

"Above the step," I said. "Then along the path."

"Past Dala's frame?"

"Only if she is not using it."

The crewman looked at me.

"Did she agree?"

I had not asked her. I had thought the new strips were enough to make the next journey safe.

"She has used the path every day," I said.

"That was not his question," the keeper answered.

Tawan rose.

"I will ask her."

"She may be fishing," I said.

"Then I will wait until she comes back."

He walked out before I could stop him. The crewman stayed, rubbing the edge of the bamboo with his thumbnail. The keeper took the trial strips off the table and placed them beside the uncut one. She left the original strip alone.

Dala came to the hall while the light was still strong. Tawan walked beside her, carrying her broken frame. Pawi followed with a coil of cord. The frame had been bound at one corner with an old piece of fiber, but the split still showed through.

Tawan put it on the floor.

"She says the path is hers," he said.

"It is the way to her fishing ground," I answered.

Dala looked at me.

"That is not what I said."

"What did you say?"

"I said I use the path to bring my frame to the water. I do not want men carrying jars over it while my net is spread. The last time a boat came close, its line pulled my frame sideways and broke the joint. I have not been paid for the fish I lost."

"I remember."

"Then why did you tell them they could pass?"

"I said they could pass if you were not using it."

"Who will tell them when I am using it?"

I looked at the crewman. He had no answer. Tawan held the broken frame with both hands, as if it were heavier than it was.

"Could we make a separate path?" I asked.

Dala shook her head.

"Through whose ground?"

I looked past her toward the houses. The nearest yard belonged to Amaya's household. I owed Amaya a cloth and had no right to send strangers across her yard.

"I don't know," I said.

Dala took the frame from Tawan.

"Then do not promise one."

The keeper brought the trial strips to the doorway.

"We were trying to make the next agreement clearer," she said. "One strip for the journey, one for the goods. We had not asked about the ground."

Dala examined the cuts.

"You could make a hundred of these," she said. "None would mend my frame."

The keeper did not defend the strips. She laid them on the table where Dala could see them.

"The claim for your frame is on its own strip," she said. "It has not been marked paid."

Dala looked at the old claim hanging beside the doorway. Then she looked at the new strips.

"Keep them apart," she said. "People will think a new mark means the old one is finished."

I went to the wall and moved the claim strip farther from the trial pieces. I left it where anyone entering could still see it.

"Is that enough?" I asked.

"No. But it is clearer."

Tawan set his bag down and looked toward the river.

"Then we have two strips for a journey we cannot make, and a path we have not been allowed to use."

"Yes," I said.

He rubbed his forehead.

"I came here because I thought the first strip gave me two journeys. Now I see that even a new strip would not give me one."

Dala lifted her frame onto her shoulder. Pawi took the coil of cord and went ahead to clear the doorway. She followed him without waiting for us to settle anything.

The keeper left the trial strips on the dry board. She did not cut a mark for the path. There was no agreement to record.

Outside, the water had fallen far enough to show the pale edge of Raya's repair. It looked sound from where I stood. I knew better than to call that an inspection.

Tawan went to find his crew. I remained beside the table, looking at the two new strips and the old one. I had wanted a way to make a promise survive the journey between two people. I had forgotten the people whose ground lay between the boat and the hall.

* * *

The next morning I found the keeper taking the bamboo down from the doorway. She had laid a dry mat on the floor so the strips would not touch the mud carried in on people's feet. The old claim for Dala's broken frame lay at one end. The trial marks for Tawan's boat lay at the other. Between them was the original strip, the long cut and the two short cuts dark with yesterday's handling.

"Are you putting them away?" I asked.

"Moving them out of the rain."

She pointed to a dark patch spreading across the wall above the doorway. The roof had leaked in the night. The mat beneath it smelled of damp reed.

"Where will anyone see them?"

"Where I can keep them dry."

A boy came from the houses with a jar balanced against his hip. He stopped at the threshold, watching her lift the strips one by one.

"My aunt says the fisherwoman has been paid," he said.

The keeper did not put down the strip in her hand.

"Who told her?"

"The man who buys her fish. He saw the old claim taken down."

"It was taken down because the wall is wet."

"He says there is new cord for her frame. He says the marks have been cleared."

I looked at the strips on the mat. None had been scraped smooth. Dala's claim was still there, its cut edge rough where Pawi had helped mark the loss. But I could see what the boy had seen from the doorway: the keeper carrying the old strip away, the new ones lying beside it, and a woman promised some cord.

"Where is the fish buyer?" I asked.

"At the bank. He says he will come back when she can fish."

"She has been fishing," the boy said after a moment. "Only not with the big frame."

The keeper set the original journey tally on the highest shelf, beyond the drip. She left the claim on the mat.

"Tell your aunt that I have marked nothing paid."

The boy shifted the jar.

"Will you tell the buyer?"

"I will," I said.

I went down to Dala's rack before he could ask what authority I had to speak for her. Pawi was there with his feet planted in the mud, holding the damaged frame upright. The joint had opened again. A loop of old binding dangled from it, dark and swollen. Dala sat beside a shallow basket of small fish, sorting the ones she could dry from those that had to be eaten before evening.

"Someone says your claim is finished," I told her.

She did not look up.

"Then someone has caught my missing fish."

"He saw the keeper move the strip. He heard about the binding."

"The binding I have not seen?"

"Yes."

She put a small fish aside and lifted a larger one by the tail. Its belly had torn where it was caught.

"Take that to him," she said. "Ask if he wants to buy it as a whole fish."

"I came to correct him."

"Do that, then. But don't tell him I have stopped asking for what I lost."

Pawi lowered the frame. The broken joint scraped his ankle. He caught it before it fell into the fish basket.

"Would a new binding hold it?" I asked.

"Perhaps," Dala said. "Perhaps it would pull the split wider. We need to know how the wood takes the strain when the net is wet."

"The basket maker offered a short trial."

"She offered to make one when she finished her other work. I have not hired her to mend this."

I knelt beside the frame. The wood was rough where it had cracked. The old fiber had sunk into the split and left a pale groove.

"Could I take it to her?"

Dala drew the frame back toward herself.

"You may carry it if I ask you. I want to see what she makes first."

Pawi held the loose end steady while Dala lifted the joint clear of the mud. She pressed it with her thumb, then released it. The two sides parted again.

"I need a piece that will sit across this," she said. "Cord alone may hold it for one pull. Then it will break where the cord ends."

"I know a maker who works wood."

"Raya has enough work on the step. Don't promise her hands."

I had been about to do exactly that. Dala wiped her thumb on her skirt and looked at the path.

"Go and find the buyer. He thinks I have stopped selling because the hall has settled my claim. He has not come for two mornings. If he won't buy what I can still catch, that is another loss."

I left the frame with her and went toward the bend. The buyer was sitting in the shade of an overturned canoe, picking scales from a reed tray. His own small basket was empty.

"Dala has fish today," I said.

"Not enough for my household and the drying shed."

"She says you have not come."

"She says her frame is broken. Yesterday I heard she had new cord and the hall had cleared her account. If she is paid, she can mend it and bring me a proper catch."

"She has not been paid."

He looked toward the hall.

"I saw them take down the strip."

"The wall leaked. They moved it to keep it dry."

"Then why are people saying it is settled?"

"Because the maker offered to try a binding."

"Has she made it?"

"No."

"Has Dala taken it?"

"No."

He pulled a scale from the tray and flicked it away.

"Then what did you come to tell me?"

"That she still has fish. You can buy what she has, or refuse it because there is too little. But the hall has not paid her claim."

He stood and brushed sand from his knees.

"Did she send you?"

"She told me to correct you."

"Then I will hear what she has caught."

He took his empty basket and walked past me. He did not thank me. I had expected relief when he agreed to look; instead I felt the cost of two mornings when he had not come.

At the hall the keeper had found a dry place for the strips on a low shelf beneath the window. She had tied the old claim with a loose loop of plain cord, leaving its cuts visible. The original boat tally lay separately on the high shelf. The new trial strips were on the table, where anyone could pick them up and mistake them for an agreement.

"Those should not be beside the claim," I said.

"They aren't."

"They were, when the boy came."

"They were on a mat because the roof was leaking."

"He saw you take the old claim down. The buyer heard it had been cleared."

The keeper put both palms flat on the table.

"Then I must tell people what I did."

"Yes."

She looked at the roof. A drop fell beside the mat, darkening it.

"And you must help me move this table before the next rain."

We carried it across the room. One leg caught on a raised board. The keeper lifted her end while I pulled mine, and the leg came free with a splintering sound. We set the table beneath the dry wall, away from the window. The strips were not damaged. The table leg was.

"That will need a wedge," she said.

I bent to inspect the split. The hall had no pile of spare timber waiting for me. I remembered Madan's wood and how many people had tried to spend it before he agreed to sell it.

"Do not take anything from Amaya's store," the keeper said.

"I was not going to."

"You looked toward the store."

I had. I straightened and went to find a small offcut among the scraps left from the repair. Raya's nephew was sitting beside them, scraping a handle. He told me which pieces had already been promised and which were fit only for fire. I asked for one thin wedge. He held it up, examined the grain, and said I could have it if I helped him turn a plank before the tide.

I helped. The plank was heavier than it looked. We carried it shoulder-high across the mud and laid it where Raya had told him to keep it, well above the waterline. By the time I returned with the wedge, the keeper had taken the strips down again and was holding them before two women from the houses.

"This is the boat's disputed mark," she said, showing the original without letting it out of her hand. "It has not been changed. This is Dala's claim. Nothing on it says she has been paid. These are trials we made yesterday. They give no one a journey or a path."

One woman nodded. The other asked whether a man who had waited all day could demand a boat's payment from the hall.

"No," the keeper said. "He can ask the man who hired him. If they bring the matter here, we can hear what each promised."

"Then what good are all these marks?" the woman asked.

The keeper looked at the strips in her hands.

"They keep us from pretending the questions have disappeared."

The woman turned away, unsatisfied. The keeper put the strips back on their separate shelves and let me fit the wedge into the table leg. It held when she leaned on the table, but I would not have trusted it under a full jar.

Near midday I went to the basket maker. She had the returned fourth basket open on her knee, pulling out a damaged row. Her boy was turning a coil of fiber so it would dry evenly.

"Have you made Dala's binding?" I asked.

"No. I told you what work came first."

"The fish buyer thinks it has been made and that her claim is settled."

"Did you tell him that?"

"No."

"Then find who did."

"I told him it was not made."

She tested a strand and laid it across the basket's rim.

"If I make a short length for Dala, I will want something for the work. The fiber was mine before anyone spoke of her frame. And if it fails in wet use, I will not have you say I sold her a mended frame."

"What would you take?"

She looked at the basket on her knee.

"Help my boy carry the clay jars from the potter's yard tomorrow. He cannot take them alone while I finish this. Then I will make a short trial length. Dala can test it before she decides whether to pay for more."

"I can carry them."

"Ask the potter first. The jars are his until I buy them. Do not lift them because I told you to."

I laughed once, without amusement. It seemed everyone had learned to speak that way after meeting me.

"I will ask him."

She went back to her basket. No fiber changed hands. I left knowing I had promised my own morning, and nothing of Dala's lost catch had been paid.

At the rack, the fish buyer had taken six of Dala's smaller fish. He was counting them into his basket. Pawi stood beside him, watching the count. Dala had put aside two for her own pot.

"Did he buy them?" I asked.

"He paid for six," she said. "He did not buy the frame."

The buyer tied his basket and went away. He had taken less than he usually did. Dala watched him go, then turned the broken joint over in her hands.

"The maker will try a short binding after I help carry jars," I said.

"Who asked you to promise her that?"

"I did."

"Then it is your work. Do not call it my payment."

"I won't."

She set the frame down beside her basket.

"Come tomorrow after the water falls. Pawi and I need to lift the rack clear so we can see whether the joint is splitting under its own weight. You can hold one end. That will tell us more than looking at it here."

"I will come."

"You have also promised the maker's jars."

"In the morning. The rack after the water falls."

Dala gave me a long look. She knew as well as I did that the tide might fall before the jars were carried.

"If you cannot be here, say so before we lift," she said. "I can ask someone else. I cannot hold the frame in the air while you are finding out where you ought to be."

I promised to send word. It was a smaller promise than the one I had made to the boat owner, but this time I asked her to repeat what she needed from me. She said: one pair of hands at the rack, after the water fell, and warning if I could not come. Nothing about a repaired frame or a paid claim.

When I returned to the hall, Tawan's young crewman was waiting beside the table with a strip of frayed rope in his hand.

"My owner has found someone who will take the jars from the far bank," he said. "He wants to know whether your new way of speaking can get us one safe journey before then."

"Raya has not cleared the step."

"I know."

"Dala has not given leave for the path."

"I know that too."

He put the rope on the table.

"Then send someone to ask them. He is tired of hearing what cannot be done."

The keeper reached for a clean strip and stopped. I could see the question in her hand: whether a new mark would record a promise we had no right to make.

"Come back when the water is lower," I said. "I will ask them what they are willing to inspect. I cannot promise you a journey."

The crewman took up the rope again.

"Tell Tawan that yourself," he said.

He left without waiting for the keeper to cut anything. On the dry wall Dala's old claim remained visible, separate from the boat's disputed strip. The roof still leaked. The table held on a wedge that had cost me half a tide of labor. I had corrected a false report and made two promises with my own hands. Neither would put Tawan's jars on a boat.

* * *

I found Raya kneeling beside the repaired step with a bowl of water in her hand. She poured it slowly into a crack between the inner timber and the bank. The water vanished into the dark earth. She waited, then pressed her thumb into the place where it had gone.

"Does that mean it is loose?" I asked.

"It means there is a space. I have to find where the water goes."

She took a narrow piece of wood and pushed it into the crack. The tip came out stained with wet soil. Her nephew stood above us holding a coil of line, careful not to let it drag over the new timber.

"Tawan wants to send jars before the next tide," I said.

"He can want them sent. He cannot make the earth harder by wanting."

"Will you look at the step?"

"I am looking at it."

I sat on a dry stone and watched her scrape mud from the edge. The river was falling, leaving little ridges of silt around the old post. The sounding stake stood farther out, where it had always been. No one had tied a boat to it since Raya forbade it.

She made her nephew stand on the inner timber. He placed one foot, then the other, and waited. Raya looked at the crack. It did not widen.

"Step back," she said.

He did. She had him repeat it with a small stone in his hands. The inner timber held. When she asked him to shift toward the outer edge, the wet soil moved under the wood. Raya slapped the timber with her open palm.

"Enough."

He came off at once.

"One person with a light load on the inside," she said. "No one at the outer edge. No jar passed from one man's arms to another over the water. And not until the next high water has gone and I have looked again."

"That will be too late for the buyer."

"Then the buyer must use another landing."

"Could we shore the edge now?"

"With what? The pieces Madan sold were for the repair we made. I will not pull them out to make this look better."

Her nephew coiled the line. I had no timber to offer and no right to borrow from the houses.

"Will you tell Tawan yourself?" I asked.

"Bring him. He ought to see where his crew would put their feet."

I went up the path to find him. Tawan was waiting by the hall with the young crewman and the woman whose jars had missed their buyer. She held two clay jars in a carrying frame. Their mouths were stopped with folded leaves and bound with fiber. The weight pulled at her arms; she put the frame down as soon as she saw me.

"I have found another buyer across the water," she said. "He will take both if they reach him before he leaves for the fields."

"Have you agreed on a price?"

"He has seen what is in them. He says he will pay when he receives them."

"Then the jars are still yours."

"Yes," she said. "They were mine yesterday, when your people made me wait."

I did not correct her word people. Tawan was looking at the ground. The crewman had a fresh strip of cloth around the cut in his palm.

"Raya has looked at the step," I said. "She wants you to see it."

The woman lifted the frame. I offered to carry one end, and she gave it to me only after asking whether I expected payment. I said I did not. She let me take the lighter end, though I was not sure how she had judged it lighter.

At the bank Raya made us set the jars well above the water. She showed Tawan the wet crack. He crouched and touched it, then wiped his fingers on his thigh.

"I could keep my boat close to the old post," he said.

"You can keep the boat wherever you and the post can hold it," Raya answered. "That does not make this edge fit for jars."

"Could we carry them one at a time?"

"Not today. After the next water I will inspect it again. Then I may allow one person with a light load on the inner part. I have not said a full jar is light."

The woman took the carrying frame off the ground.

"Can we use the path above Dala's rack?"

"Dala decides that," I said.

Tawan looked at me, then toward the nets spread on the bank.

"Yesterday I asked her," he said. "She did not give leave."

"We can ask again. We cannot count her silence."

The woman put the frame down harder than she meant to. One jar knocked against the other. She separated them with a folded mat and ran her fingers around both rims, searching for a crack.

"If I lose these, none of you will pay for them," she said.

"I will pay if my boat breaks them," Tawan answered.

"And if they fall from your men's hands on the step?"

He glanced at Raya.

"Then we should not carry them there."

It was the first answer that had not tried to make someone else promise safety. The woman studied him for a moment, then lifted the frame again.

We found Dala near her rack. She had hung the smaller net to dry and was sorting hooks into a shallow dish. Pawi was binding a cracked handle with a piece of old cord. He put it down when he saw the jars.

"You have brought another load to my path," Dala said.

"We have brought it for you to see," the woman answered. "We have not stepped on the path."

Dala looked at the jars, then at the wet earth beside her rack.

"Not while this net is spread. When I take it down there may be room for one person. But I will not move my frame into the mud so that a boat can save an hour."

"Could you tell us when the way is clear?" Tawan asked.

"I can tell you when I am finished. I cannot tell you what the tide will do."

"And if Raya says the step is safe then?"

"I will look at the path again."

The woman exhaled through her nose. She had been holding the weight of the jars so long that her fingers had left pale marks on the carrying frame.

"Then I cannot tell my buyer when I will arrive."

"No," Dala said.

The woman set down the jars. She did not accuse Dala. She opened one leaf stopper to make sure the contents had not spilled, then closed it again.

"I will use the far bank," she said. "I can cross above the bend with the jars on the dry ground. It will cost me another porter."

Tawan stepped forward.

"My boat could meet you there."

"You offered me this landing. I have spent two days learning that it is not ready. I will find the porter myself."

She lifted the frame and walked away before I could offer to help. Her back was straight, but she stopped twice on the path to shift the weight.

Tawan watched her go. The crewman had said nothing. Now he sat on the root of a tree and began to unwrap his palm.

"She has gone," Tawan said.

"Yes."

"Then we have lost the work."

The crewman looked up.

"You have lost a fare. I have already done the waiting."

Tawan turned toward him.

"We spoke of that."

"We spoke of paying. You have not counted it."

Tawan reached into the small bag at his belt. He took out a folded packet of rice and put it beside the crewman.

"That is what I can give you now."

The young man did not touch it.

"How much of yesterday does that pay?"

"Half the time you waited."

"And the other half?"

"After I collect for the next journey."

"What next journey?"

Tawan looked toward the path the woman had taken. He had no answer.

The older steersman came up from the boat, carrying a length of rope with a frayed end. He had heard enough to understand the question.

"You promised me for holding the boat too," he said.

"I know."

"Then don't hire me again until you can pay for the last day."

Tawan held the packet in his hand. He was not a cruel man. I could see him trying to divide a little food among claims larger than it. I had been tempted to say the hall would feed the men, if only to make the argument end. Amaya's stores lay up the path, under her roof. I kept my mouth shut.

The crewman took the packet at last.

"Half," he said. "I will take this for half. I will not call it all."

Tawan nodded.

"And I will not take your boat down that path tomorrow," the young man continued, "unless you tell me before I leave what you will pay for carrying and what you will pay if the step is closed."

"Then we will speak before we load."

"With the steersman there."

"With him there."

The older man wound the frayed rope around his wrist. He did not agree to another day's work. He went back toward the boat to put away his gear.

I asked the keeper, who had come down from the hall, to bring a blank strip. She held it without cutting.

"Tell her what you have agreed," I said to Tawan.

"Half the waiting paid now. The other half still mine to pay. No new work hired until we speak again."

The crewman shook his head.

"Not just speak. Count it. If the step closes, I will still have come here."

Tawan looked at him.

"Then we must agree on that too."

The keeper lowered the strip.

"There is no new journey to mark," she said.

"Then mark what has been paid," Tawan said.

"Not on the old boat strip," the crewman answered quickly.

The keeper gave him a small uncut piece and asked him to show the rice he had accepted. He set the packet beside it. She cut one short notch for the half he acknowledged, then left room for the other half. Tawan and the crewman both repeated the amount in their own words. The older steersman's claim remained separate; he had not accepted payment.

"This does not give the boat a journey," the keeper said.

"I know," Tawan answered.

"Nor the hall a debt," I added.

The crewman looked at me.

"Good. Then I know whom to ask."

He carried the packet toward the boat. Tawan remained by the old post, watching him go. The water was rising again, though none of us had used the step. Raya had taken her nephew home. Dala's net still hung across the path. The woman with the jars was already beyond the bend, looking for another way.

I had brought the keeper down hoping she could cut a clear mark for a safe journey. The only mark she made was for a debt that belonged to Tawan. The crewman accepted half his pay and refused the next assignment until the rest was made clear. The boat stayed where it was.

* * *

The potter was already loading his jars when I came to ask whether I could carry them for the basket maker. He had arranged them in two rows on the packed earth outside his yard. A boy had put a handful of dry grass between each pair so the rims would not strike.

"She sent you?" the potter asked.

"She asked whether I could help her boy."

"Then take the ones with the red marks. The others belong to a buyer who has not come."

I bent to lift the first jar. The potter stopped me.

"How many can you carry without knocking them together?"

"Two."

"Try one."

I put my arms around it. The clay was cool where it had been shaded and warm where the sun had touched it. When I lifted, a line of wet earth stuck to the base. I shifted the weight against my hip.

A woman came from the river path and called my name. It was Pawi's sister. She had been sent because Dala and Pawi were ready to lift the rack before the water rose again.

"I told Dala I would come after the water fell," I said.

"It has fallen. They are waiting."

The potter looked at the jar in my arms.

"Will you carry it or put it down?"

I set it back in the grass. I had promised the maker the morning and Dala the falling water. Both had understood me to mean the same hours.

"I cannot do both now," I said.

"Then tell the maker before her boy waits for you."

I went to her house. The boy had already tied a carrying pole to the first pair of jars. The maker was trimming the returned basket, her knife moving in short, sharp strokes.

"I have to help Dala lift her rack," I said. "I cannot carry the jars this morning."

She stopped cutting.

"You told me you could."

"I thought the water would fall later."

"You thought without asking."

"Could I come after the rack is lifted?"

"The potter has his own work. He will not keep the jars for me because you misread the tide."

"I can come tomorrow."

"I will find someone else today."

"And the trial binding for Dala?"

She set down the knife.

"I said I would make it after the jars were carried. If someone else carries them, I will pay that person or owe them work. Your promise does not buy my fiber."

"I know."

"Then go. Dala is waiting."

Her boy untied the pole and began looking toward the houses for another pair of hands. I left him to find them. I had made a smaller mess than the one at the landing, but it was mine, and the maker would remember it when I next asked for a favor.

Dala had spread a mat beside the rack so the wet net would not touch the mud. Pawi stood with his shoulder beneath the long side of the frame. They had loosened the old cords and were waiting for someone to take the other end.

"You came," Dala said.

"I told the maker I could not carry her jars."

"Then take this corner."

We lifted together. The frame had held when it was dry, but the wet net dragged it sideways. Pawi's shoulder dipped. I caught the far end and felt the split joint twist against my palm.

"Down," Dala said.

We lowered it onto the mat. She ran a finger along the crack. The old binding had opened at the point where the wood bent.

"That is what I wanted you to feel," she said.

"More cord will not make it straight."

"It may hold a short time. It will not give back the fish I could not catch."

Pawi brought a thin piece of wood from the edge of the rack. It had been cut for a handle, not a brace. He held it against the broken joint and showed how a longer piece might cross the split.

"I can shape one if we find wood fit for it," he said. "But I won't cut from the rack while it is still doing work."

Dala nodded. She had not asked me to choose a repair for her.

We moved the net to a higher line and set the broken frame in the shade. Dala could still use her smaller gear while she decided what to do. My arms ached from the weight. I had not paid for a lost fish or a length of wood. I had helped them see why a promise of fiber was not a repair.

When I reached the hall, Tawan was there with the young crewman and the older steersman. The woman who had paid for the first journey stood beside the keeper, holding the mat that had covered her two baskets. She had come because the keeper asked whether she would repeat the first bargain once more, with the owner present.

Tawan had brought his own small strip of bamboo. He placed it on the table beside the original tally but did not touch the old one.

"I have thought about what I heard," he said. "I saw two short cuts and believed I had leave for two journeys. I was wrong about what the cuts meant."

The keeper held up the original strip by its uncut end.

"I said one boat, two baskets, after Raya and Dala gave leave. I said nothing here promised another journey."

"I did not hear the last words. That is what I have said."

"I believe you did not hear them," she answered. "I still said them."

The woman with the mat put her hand on the table.

"I paid for the two baskets to go once. They went. I owe nothing for the jars that were not mine."

"You do not," Tawan said.

The crewman looked at him.

"Then you did not send me for a journey she had bought?"

"No. I sent you because I thought I had leave for a second one. I did not."

"Will you tell the man who was waiting with the jars?"

"I will tell him myself."

The keeper set the strip down where all could see it. The long cut was still there, and the two short ones. No one proposed scraping them away.

"Then the first bargain is finished," she said. "One paid journey. Two baskets carried. No second journey bought or allowed."

Tawan repeated it. The woman repeated it. The crewman repeated it, though he kept his eyes on the rice packet tied at his belt.

"What of the time I waited?" he asked.

Tawan put his hand on the new strip they had cut beside the bank.

"I paid you half yesterday. I owe the other half. I owe the steersman for holding the boat. Those are mine to answer."

The older man spoke for the first time.

"Then do not put a mark on my behalf until I have said what I accept."

The keeper moved the crewman's strip away from the steersman's empty place on the table.

"I will not."

"Could you pay him today?" I asked Tawan.

"I can give him the same amount of rice when I have it. I will not promise a tide for which I have no fare."

The steersman shook his head.

"I may take other work before then."

"You may," Tawan said.

The crewman folded his arms.

"And I will not take your next load until we speak of both the journey and the waiting."

"I heard you yesterday."

"Then say it again here."

Tawan looked at the keeper's clean strip. He did not ask her to cut it. There was no new cargo, no permission from Raya, and no leave from Dala.

"I will ask before I hire you," he said. "I will tell you what I can pay if the landing closes. You can refuse."

The crewman nodded. He had not forgiven the lost day, but he no longer had to defend a second journey that never belonged to him.

The keeper laid three separate pieces on the table. The old tally was for the completed basket journey. The newer piece recorded the crewman's half payment and what remained due. The third was blank.

"This is where we begin when there is another load," she said. "We ask whose goods they are, who carries them, where they may pass, what the owner is paying, and what happens if the way closes. We say it aloud. We ask each person to say it back."

"That is a great deal of speaking for two baskets," Tawan said.

"It was a great deal of waiting when we did not," the crewman answered.

No one laughed. The keeper left the third piece uncut.

Tawan picked up his bag and went toward the river. The older steersman followed at a distance, carrying his own rope. The young crewman stayed long enough to ask the keeper whether he could see the strip for his waiting pay whenever he returned. She said he could. He went after the others without agreeing to another journey.

The woman with the mat took her things home. She had not been made to pay for a stranger's jars. Tawan had accepted that his first bargain gave him no second trip. The crewman had a claim against his employer that could be shown without making the hall its debtor. None of this opened the step. Raya still had to judge it after the water. Dala still controlled the path.

Amaya came in as the keeper was setting the strips on their dry shelf. She carried a folded length of cloth across her arm. It was old, patched at one end, and clean. Behind her stood a woman from a neighboring household whom I knew by sight but not by name.

"I heard you have begun keeping promises where anyone can see them," Amaya said.

"We are keeping the ones people bring us," the keeper answered.

Amaya laid the cloth on the table.

"This is the kind he owes me."

I knew what she meant. I had promised her one cloth for the use of her household's stores when the landing work began. I had spoken of finding it after the first boats paid. The first boats had brought disputes instead.

"I still owe it," I said.

"Then why is there no strip for that?"

The keeper looked at me. I had spent the morning asking Tawan to own his unpaid men's work. I could not tell Amaya to wait outside while I decided how my own debt should be remembered.

"Ask her what she wants marked," I said.

Amaya unfolded the cloth. The neighboring woman held one corner while Amaya showed its width and the patch. It was not a new cloth, and she was not offering it as payment. She wanted the keeper to see what she meant by one piece fit for household use.

"One cloth of this measure," she said. "Not the torn end of a sail. Not something taken from my own store and handed back to me."

"Have you agreed when he will bring it?" the keeper asked.

"No."

I looked at Amaya.

"I can bring it after I earn it."

"That is what you said before."

"Then I have no day to promise."

The keeper did not cut a date. She took a blank strip and held it toward Amaya.

"Will you accept a mark that says he owes the cloth, with the time still open?"

Amaya looked at the neighboring woman, then back at me.

"I will accept that the debt is spoken here. I have not agreed to wait forever."

The keeper cut a single mark. She asked Amaya to say what it meant. Then she asked me. I repeated the measure, the purpose, and the fact that I had not paid. The neighboring woman had heard both of us. The keeper placed the strip on the dry shelf, separate from Tawan's wages and Dala's damaged frame.

Amaya took up her old cloth.

"When you bring the new one," she said, "you will bring it to me. You will not put it in the hall and call the matter finished."

"I understand."

She turned to leave. The neighboring woman remained beside the table, looking at the shelf.

"If he does not bring it," she asked the keeper, "who tells him he has waited long enough?"

The keeper looked toward Amaya, who had stopped at the door. Then she looked at me.

I had asked the boat owner to accept a hearing when the river could not give him what he wanted. Now the person I owed was standing in the same doorway, and the keeper's clean bamboo was running out. Outside, a boat passed the bend without coming to our step. I waited for Amaya to answer.

<summary>Historical Notes &amp; Sources</summary>
<p><strong>Fictional marks and hearings.</strong> The keeper, Tawan, Dala, Raya, the bamboo tally, its separate goods and journey marks, and the hearing at the Maynila landing are fictional. No known source establishes this particular early-sixteenth-century system of port records, appeals or wage accounting. The narrative's limited procedure is a reconstruction, not a claim about an attested institution.</p>
<p><strong>Writing and records in the wider region.</strong> The Laguna Copperplate Inscription, dated 900 CE, records a debt clearance in a Malay-language inscription written in Indic script. It demonstrates that written records of obligation existed in the region long before the period of this novel, but does not document a tally-strip practice in early-1520s Maynila. See Elsa Clavé and Arlo Griffiths, <a href="https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol70/iss2/2/">“The Laguna Copperplate Inscription: Tenth-Century Luzon, Java, and the Malay World”</a> (2022), and Antoon Postma's <a href="https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol40/iss2/3/">1992 text and commentary</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Trade and later testimony.</strong> Tomé Pires's <em>Suma Oriental</em> (composed 1512–1515) describes regional Luzon trade through Borneo and Malacca; it does not attest Ache's individual transactions or a Maynila landing docket. See the <a href="https://asj.upd.edu.ph/mediabox/archive/Vol15%2CApr-Aug-Dec1977.pdf"><em>Asian Studies</em> discussion of Pires</a>. Juan de Plasencia's <a href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Philippine_Islands%2C_1493%E2%80%931898/Volume_7/Documents_of_1589"><em>Customs of the Tagalogs</em></a> (1589) describes later differentiated social obligations. It cannot establish the precise labor and property arrangements of this fictional generation. Original Portuguese and Spanish passages have not been collated for this chapter.</p>
