The first man came over the rail with one foot still on the rope.

He looked down before he moved the other. I had been waiting for him to look at the master or at the men with poles, but his eyes went to the hook buried in the wood beside us. He tested the line with his hand. Another man behind him shouted something. The first lowered himself onto the deck.

The injured sailor caught my wrist.

His hand was hot and wet. He had pulled free of the cloth I had tucked beneath his shoulder, and his splinted leg lay across the narrow space behind the cover. The boy beside him held the cup with both hands. A little water ran down its side.

"Stay here," I told them.

The boy said, "Where would we go?"

I did not know.

Two more strangers climbed aboard. One wore a strip of cloth tied below his hair. The other carried a short blade and kept his left hand against his ribs. Their feet found places between broken pots and wet rope. Neither looked as if he had stepped onto a deck he trusted.

The master came forward with his palms open. He said something in the harbor trade speech, slowly, as if the shape of each word might make it understood. The man with the blade answered in a different tongue. The master tried again. A fourth stranger appeared above the rail and gestured sharply for everyone to move toward the stern.

The master's crew did not move. They looked to him.

He lowered his hands and told them to go.

A young sailor near the bow threw his sounding pole into the water before he obeyed. It struck the side and floated away. One of the strangers shouted at him, then pointed his blade toward the stern. The sailor walked past me with his jaw clenched.

The older woman remained by the rail, basket against her hip. She was looking toward the outer passage, where her son had swum. The stranger with the cloth around his head approached her. He pointed at the basket. She pulled it closer.

"Open it," the master told her.

She stared at him. He had spoken to the foreigner with his hands open; now he was giving orders to her.

"Do you know whether he reached the shore?" she asked.

"No."

"Then I will wait here."

The stranger reached for the basket. She struck his hand with its rim. He caught her wrist, not hard enough to pull her down, but hard enough that she could not turn away. I stood.

"She is waiting for her son," I said.

He looked at me without recognition. I repeated the words in the trade speech. He watched my mouth and then my hands. I pointed toward the water. He pushed me back with his forearm.

The master stepped between us. He took the basket from the woman himself, opened its lid, and showed the stranger what was inside: a folded cloth, two small bowls, a length of dried fish wrapped in leaf. The stranger touched the bowls, then waved the basket away.

The woman took it back without thanking either of us.

Another man had climbed over the rail. He had a cut across his cheek and a dark stain on his sleeve. He saw the injured sailor behind the cover and called to the others. The man with the blade came toward us.

The boy tried to stand over the injured man's legs. I put a hand on his shoulder.

"He cannot walk," I said.

The stranger pointed at the stern. I pointed at the splint.

He looked at the wood tied against the leg, then at the boy. He said something, and the boy shook his head though he could not have understood. The stranger reached down to lift the injured man beneath the arm.

"Wait."

I put my hand between his and the binding. He stopped. For an instant we were both holding the same strip of cloth. He turned to the man with the cut cheek, who spoke quickly. The stranger let go and stepped back.

I thought I had made him understand.

Then he seized my wrist and twisted it behind me.

The deck moved under our feet. I lost my balance and struck the cover with my knee. The injured man cried out as the wood shifted. The boy threw himself across the splint to keep it from moving.

"I was showing you," I said.

The stranger held my arm higher. He said something close to my ear. I heard anger, but it might have been an instruction. I could not see his face.

The master shouted. The stranger loosened his grip enough for me to turn. The master pointed at the injured man and then at the bow, where two of his crew had begun to carry a rolled sail.

"He wants to put him on the sail," the master said to me. "Help them."

The stranger was still holding my wrist. I nodded, and he released it.

We spread the sailcloth beside the cover. The boy crawled out of the way only when the injured man told him to. Four of us lifted him by the cloth, keeping the splinted leg as still as we could. He screamed once. After that he breathed through his teeth and stared at the mast.

The stranger with the cut cheek stood near the bow, watching the transfer. When we had the injured man down, he took a water vessel from his belt and offered it to him. The sailor turned his face away. The stranger left the vessel beside the cloth and went back to the rail.

I had been afraid he would strike us for taking too long. I had been wrong about that. I did not know what else I had been wrong about.

At the stern, the master was being made to stand with his crew. The young sailor who had thrown the pole away had his hands held behind him by a foreigner scarcely older than he was. A thin cord ran from one wrist to the other. The sailor looked at the master, who shook his head once. He did not resist.

The stranger with the blade pointed to my waist. I wore a small working knife there, the one I had used to cut cord that morning. I drew it by its wooden handle and laid it on the deck.

He gestured for me to step away.

I stepped away.

He picked up the knife, turned it over, and gave it to another man. There was no ceremony in it. I had carried the knife through two voyages and a campaign. In his hand it was a tool he did not want me to have.

The boy followed as we moved the injured man. He kept one hand against the sailcloth so he would not lose his place. A foreigner blocked him near the stern. The boy tried to go around. The foreigner put out an arm.

"He is helping," I said.

The boy looked at me. He had trusted me to explain him.

I pointed at the man lying on the cloth and then at the boy. The foreigner looked from one to the other. He let the boy through but pushed me toward the rest of the prisoners.

For a few breaths I could see everyone. The older woman stood with her basket. The master was beside the young sailor, both facing the stern. The boy knelt at the injured man's head. The man with the cut cheek was kneeling on the other side, trying to see whether the binding had slipped.

Then another rope came across from the high ship.

A foreigner called for men to hold it. Two of the master's crew obeyed before they were told a second time. The junk rocked as the line took its weight. I heard wood scrape against wood and felt the deck lift beneath my feet.

They were drawing us alongside.

The master shouted that the hull would strike. He pointed at a broken spar caught between the two vessels. One of the foreigners looked down and began shouting back to the ship. Someone above threw a coil of rope. A sailor caught it and made it fast. The broken spar swung away.

For a moment the master and the stranger were working at the same problem. They did not need to agree on anything else to see what would crush them.

I moved toward the injured man. The boy had shifted the cup so it would not spill. I wanted to tell him what was happening, though he could hear the hulls.

The stranger who had twisted my wrist stepped into my path. He held out his hand, palm up. I thought he wanted something from me. I opened my empty hands.

He took my left arm and turned me toward the rope ladder.

"That man cannot climb," I said, pointing behind me.

He followed my gesture. Then he pointed up the ladder again.

I understood that he meant me.

I looked for the master. He had been pushed toward the bow. His crew were calling to him, but he could no longer answer all of them. The older woman had sat down beside the mast, her basket between her feet. No one was asking her to climb yet.

I turned back to the stranger and touched my own chest, then pointed to the injured man.

"I came aboard for him."

The stranger gripped my arm harder. He spoke over his shoulder. A second man came and took my other arm.

I could have pulled away. I had enough strength left for that. There was nowhere on the junk I could have gone, and the boy was watching.

I let them bring me to the rail.

The ladder was not the one I had climbed from Safi's boat. This one hung against the foreign ship's side, high enough that the top disappeared above me. The rungs were slick, and the two vessels moved differently in the water. One stranger climbed first. The other kept a hand at my back.

I placed my foot on the lowest rung.

From there I could see over the junk's cover. The boy was looking at the injured man, not at me. He had begun to pour water slowly against the man's lips. The older woman had risen again. She was speaking to the master, who shook his head and looked out toward the passage.

The stranger pushed at my heel.

I climbed.

Halfway up, the vessels struck together. The ladder jumped away from the side. My right foot slipped and caught on a lower rung. The stranger above seized the back of my clothing and hauled me close enough to grip the next rope. My arm burned where it had been twisted.

When I reached the upper deck, I turned to look down. A man with a long pole was trying to keep the hulls apart. Beneath him, the junk's deck was crowded with people who could no longer choose where to stand.

The boy looked up at last. I raised my hand to show him I was still there.

A stranger beside me caught the raised arm and pulled it down.

I did not know whether he thought I was calling for help, giving an order, or threatening him. He turned me away from the rail before I could try again.

I heard the boy shout something below. The wind and the movement of the ships took the words from me.

The men led me across a deck that smelled of damp rope, smoke and old water. I could still hear the junk striking the side behind us. I could no longer see the injured man.

I had gone to him because I could not bear to watch another boat carry its wounded without help. Now the boy was doing the work, and I had been taken where my hands were of no use to either of them.

---

They made me sit beside a coil of rope.

There were three other men there. One had a strip of cloth over his eyes, tied too loosely to be a blindfold. Blood had dried along his neck. Another wore a wet jacket with a broken fastening. The third was a boy older than the one I had left on the junk, with a split lip and no sandals.

None of them spoke when I came.

A man on the foreign ship's deck stood over us holding a length of cord. He did not bind my hands. He pointed at the boards and waited until I sat. Then he went to the rail and shouted to the men below.

The ship moved differently from the boats I knew. Its deck rose slowly, hung for a moment, and settled with a force that traveled through the wood. A line of dark water marked the planks near the side. Beyond the rail I could see the upper part of our junk's mast and nothing of the people on its deck.

I tried to stand to look down.

The man with the cord pointed at the boards again.

I sat.

A wooden cup passed from one prisoner to the next. The man in the wet jacket drank and gave it to the boy with the split lip. He held it for a long time, looking into it. The man with the cloth over his eyes reached toward the cup but missed it.

"Give him some," I said.

The boy looked at me. He had not been refusing. He was frightened of dropping it. He put the cup against the man's fingers and waited until they closed around it.

When the cup reached me, it held little more than a mouthful. I drank. The water tasted of wood and something sour. I had been thirsty since the watch boat left the landing.

I passed the empty cup back. The man with the wet jacket watched me.

"Who are you?" he asked in the harbor trade speech.

"Ache."

"Whose boat?"

"I was helping on the one below."

He looked toward the rail.

"That is not what I asked."

"It is not my boat."

"Then whose man are you?"

I told him that I carried messages for Jamil under a limited order, and that I had climbed onto the junk to help a wounded sailor. I could hear myself giving the sort of account I had demanded from others after Laoe. It was true. It did not answer the question of who would come to claim me.

The man rubbed his hands against his jacket.

"If they ask you that again," he said, "tell them whose son you are."

I looked at him more carefully. He had a narrow face and a pale line of old scar tissue beneath his ear.

"Why?"

"Because they have been asking who owns the cargo. They may ask who owns the men."

"No one owns me."

He laughed once, then stopped as if the sound had cost him something.

"Then tell them that."

Two foreigners came across the deck with the junk's master between them. He had lost his headcloth. A scrape ran from his temple into his hair. He saw me and stopped, but the men behind him urged him forward.

The man with the wet jacket moved to make space.

"Your injured man?" I asked the master.

"Still below when I left."

"The boy?"

"With him."

"And the woman?"

"With her basket."

He sat against the rope coil. One of the foreigners held up a hand and said something sharply. The master moved away from the coil, leaving a space between us. He had been permitted to sit but not to choose where.

"Did they take anyone else?" I asked.

"They took two from the stern. I could not see the bow."

He pressed a thumb against the scrape on his temple. Blood came away on it.

"Your brother," I began.

"My brother was at Laoe. I told you."

"I remember."

"You remember his name?"

"You did not tell me."

The master looked away. He had wanted to hear that I remembered more than I did.

A sailor came with another cup. This one held water enough for everyone. The sailor was dark from the sun and had a small knot of hair tied at the back of his head. He spoke a few words in a tongue I recognized from markets, then tried another. The master answered him.

The sailor looked relieved. He crouched before us and began asking questions.

He knew enough of the trade speech to ask for a place and a name, but not enough to distinguish a man who had been sent by a ruler from one who had bought cargo for him. He asked the master three times whether he was the owner of the junk. Each time the master answered yes. The sailor then pointed at me.

"His owner?"

"No," the master said.

The sailor pointed again.

"He came from shore," the master said. "He climbed aboard after the firing."

The sailor frowned and looked at my clothes.

"Who sent him?"

"Jamil," I said.

He repeated the name as if it were a place.

"Who is Jamil?"

"A man who serves the ruler here."

The sailor spoke to the foreigner with the cord. The foreigner looked at me and then toward the shore.

"Are you Jamil's son?" the sailor asked.

"No."

"His servant?"

"No."

The master said, "He was at Laoe."

The sailor turned back to him.

"He fought there," the master continued. "Ask him whose orders took the boats to Laoe."

I had no answer that would satisfy the master. I had served the ruler's expedition, and I had helped men after the fighting. Neither fact would bring his brother home.

The sailor repeated the name of Laoe uncertainly. The foreigner with the cord said something to him. He asked me to stand.

I stood. My right arm ached.

"Name," he said.

"Ache."

He repeated it, making the last sound too short.

"From?"

"Maynila. Luzon."

He looked to the master. The master nodded at Luzon.

"Father?" the sailor asked.

I told him whose son I was.

He asked again. I repeated it. He turned to the foreigner with the cord and spoke rapidly. The foreigner called another man over. The second had a writing board and a short stick of dark material. He made marks while the sailor spoke.

The master watched the marks being made.

"What did you tell them?" he asked me.

"My name."

"Only that?"

"Where I am from."

"Then why is he writing?"

I could not tell him. The sailor was already asking whether I had come from the fighting at Laoe with the ruler's men.

"I came back with them," I said.

"Captain?"

"No."

He looked confused.

"Boat leader?"

"I was given responsibility for agreed routes. I did not command that junk."

The sailor seemed to catch only the word for responsibility. He turned to the writing board again.

"Tell him I was not its master," I said.

The master spoke before the sailor could answer.

"He was not. I was."

The sailor nodded, but he did not alter the marks. He asked how many men I had brought to the harbor. I said I had brought none to fight. He asked whether the boats that had crossed the water were mine. I said they belonged to their owners.

He put down the cup and looked at me for a long moment.

"Important man," he said at last.

I did not know whether it was a question.

"Important enough that they will ask for him," the man in the wet jacket said.

The master turned on him.

"They should ask for all of us."

The man shrugged. He had not said they should not.

The sailor called for another foreigner, one wearing a narrow belt over his clothing. They spoke near the rail, looking toward the landings. I heard my name twice, badly pronounced, and the word Luzon.

The master leaned toward me.

"When they send word for you," he said, "tell them there is a man below with a broken leg. Tell them the boy is with him."

"I will."

"Tell them there is a woman who has not seen her son come out of the water."

"I will."

"And tell them I own the junk."

"Yes."

He gripped my arm, hard enough to make me look at him.

"Do not say we are your men."

"I will not."

The foreigner with the belt approached. He spoke to the sailor, who translated one word at a time.

"Where is your ruler?"

I pointed toward the town.

"Here?"

"In Burné."

"Can he send boats?"

I did not answer at once. The ruler could send orders. I had seen what happened when men were sent before they understood the water or the enemy. The stranger repeated his question.

"People here can send messages," I said. "Boats have owners."

The sailor translated. The stranger frowned, perhaps because he had heard no number. He asked whether my father would pay to have me returned.

I looked at the master. He had heard the question, though the sailor had spoken it badly.

"What would they want?" I asked.

The sailor looked to the stranger. He did not seem to know the word I had used. He touched his own palm with two fingers and said a word for gold.

I thought of the gold that had passed through the hands of merchants and rulers in Maynila. I thought of the goods still disputed after Laoe, of Nasir's sister paying for inquiries with money she could scarcely spare. I had no right to promise a payment from any of them.

"I cannot answer for my father," I said.

The sailor repeated only the last part. The stranger looked displeased. He spoke to the man with the writing board, who made another mark.

The man with the wet jacket shifted closer to me.

"If you have a name worth asking about," he whispered, "use it."

"To do what?"

"To get a boat sent."

"For whom?"

He looked at the master, then at the boy with the split lip. His answer came slowly.

"I do not know."

The sailor told the master to stand. The master rose, but the foreigner with the belt pointed for him to remain beside the rope coil. Instead they took me away.

"Where?" I asked.

The sailor pointed toward a covered space near the ship's middle. I could see men moving there with bundles and cups. A guard stood by a low doorway.

I looked back at the master. He had his hand against the scrape on his temple. He did not ask me to come back.

The boy with the split lip was watching. I tried to give him the little assurance I had wanted to give the boy below, but he looked away before I could speak.

At the doorway, the sailor asked me to say my name again.

"Ache."

He repeated it to the guard. Then he added something about Luzon and my father. The guard straightened and moved aside.

I had spent much of my life learning how to make men hear me. On that ship I could not even tell which of my words had survived the crossing from one tongue to another.

My name had gone ahead of me.

I followed it through the doorway, leaving the master and the other men where they had been told to sit.

---

The room beyond the doorway had no place to sit without touching someone.

Two men were already on the floor, their backs against a chest bound with iron. A woman in a blue-sleeved garment sat on a folded mat with her knees drawn up. A younger woman beside her was holding a piece of cloth against her ear. The room smelled of wet wool and water that had been kept too long.

A foreigner told me to go to the far wall. I went there, then moved when he pointed to a different place. He closed the door but left the upper half open. Light came through a narrow gap beneath the roof.

The woman in blue watched me settle.

"Are you from the junk beside us?" she asked in the harbor speech.

"Yes."

"Did they take its master?"

"He's on deck."

"His crew?"

"Some of them. I could not count."

She nodded. She had not asked me to count the people I had not seen.

The younger woman lowered the cloth from her ear. There was blood on it, but I could not tell whether it came from a cut or from the sound of the guns.

"We were on another vessel," the woman in blue said. "They brought us here before they brought you."

"Was anyone killed?"

"Yes."

I waited for more. She looked at the younger woman and said nothing else.

A sailor came with a shallow bowl of water. He placed it near the door and spoke to the woman in blue. She answered him in words I did not know. He frowned, repeated himself, and left.

The woman lifted the bowl and drank. She passed it to the younger woman, who drank without taking the cloth away from her ear. Then she offered it to me.

"Keep some," I said.

"For whom?"

I looked at the two men by the chest. One shook his head. The other took the bowl, drank, and gave it back.

"Water is the one thing they have brought us without asking for something," she said. "Do not teach them to bring less."

She set the bowl in the middle of the floor.

I had not been in the room long enough to know whether she was right. I had already spoken as if I knew how much water each person should have.

The ship gave a long shudder. A rope scraped overhead. Someone outside shouted, and several men answered. I heard the thud of a heavy object against the hull.

"They are moving the junks," I said.

The woman in blue looked at the door.

"Or moving away from them."

We listened. The thud came again, farther off.

One of the men by the chest began to pray. He kept his voice low, almost hidden in the movement of the ship. The other man closed his eyes. The younger woman pressed the cloth against her ear harder.

I wanted to know whether the boy from the junk had come up. I went to the doorway and looked through the opening.

The foreigner outside raised his hand to stop me.

"There is an injured man below," I said.

He shook his head.

"A boy is with him."

He pointed back into the room. I tried the trade speech. He answered in his own. I could not make him understand that I was asking a question, not trying to leave.

The woman in blue called from behind me.

"Ask him if they have carried anyone up on a sail."

I turned. "Do you know his words?"

"Some words. Not all. My uncle traded with men who used a tongue like his."

She came to the doorway. The guard looked at her, surprised to hear her speak. He answered more slowly. They exchanged several short phrases, each repeating the other's last word.

"He says men are being brought aboard," she told me. "He does not know which."

"Can he ask?"

"He can. He has not said he will."

She spoke again. The guard looked toward the deck and then back at us. He made a gesture for her to wait.

She sat down before I did.

"Thank you," I said.

"Do not thank me yet."

After a time, the guard returned with a second man. They told us to stand. The younger woman asked whether they were going ashore. The woman in blue told her that no one had said that.

We were led out onto the deck.

The light was strong after the room. The ship had moved away from the junk, leaving a stretch of water between them. I could see men on the junk's deck but could not distinguish their faces. The vessel lay low at the stern. A piece of torn cloth trailed in the water.

Near the foreign ship's mast, a group of prisoners sat in two uneven lines. Some were wet to the waist. One held a hand over his forehead. A man in the farther line had lost a sandal and was trying to keep his bare foot away from a dark stain on the boards.

The master of the junk was there. He raised his hand when he saw me.

"Where is the boy?" I asked.

He pointed toward the far side.

I followed his hand. Two men were carrying a roll of sailcloth on a wooden board. The boy walked beside them, keeping one hand on the cloth. The injured sailor lay inside, his splinted leg raised on a bundle. His face was turned toward the sky.

I started toward them.

A guard stopped me with the end of a pole. I stepped back.

The men carrying the board set it down near the mast. The boy knelt. He lifted the injured man's head and held the cup to his mouth. The man swallowed once, then turned away.

He was alive.

I felt a rush of relief so strong that I wanted to laugh. Then I saw how far the boy had come without me, and I did not.

The older woman from the junk was brought aboard next. She had her basket. She stood at the top of the ladder until a foreigner tried to take it from her. She pulled it away and sat down on the last rung.

The men above argued. A sailor brought a rope and lowered the basket separately. Only then did she climb the rest of the way.

She came onto the deck, looked around, and asked the master whether he had seen her son.

"No," he said.

She sat near the boy and put the basket between them.

The foreigner with the writing board returned. He went from group to group asking questions. He made marks when someone spoke. Sometimes he marked a line without asking anything. I watched him stop beside the two women who had shared the room with me. He spoke to the woman in blue. She answered. He looked at the younger woman's ear, then wrote something.

"What is he recording?" I asked the woman when she came near me.

"Names, perhaps."

"Perhaps?"

"He asked where we came from. He did not ask our names."

She looked toward the men by the mast.

"If he writes that we came from the same boat, will he remember which of us lost a brother?"

I could not answer.

The foreigner with the belt approached with the sailor who knew a little trade speech. They called for me. I stepped away from the other prisoners.

"Men ashore," the sailor said. "Men from this ship."

"Where?"

He pointed toward Burné.

I knew there had been foreign visitors on land before the attack. I did not know where they were now.

"Your ruler has them?" the sailor asked.

"I do not know."

The foreigner with the belt spoke sharply. The sailor tried again.

"Some of our men went ashore. They did not come back. Where are they?"

"I was at the landing when the guns began. I did not see them."

"Ask your ruler."

"I am here."

The sailor looked at me as though I had chosen the wrong answer.

"Send word," he said.

"With whom?"

He pointed at the junk and then at the shore. I looked across the water. Our vessel was still afloat, but its master stood captive on this deck. I did not know who could take a message or who would be permitted to carry it.

The foreigner with the belt spoke to the man with the writing board. The board was turned toward me. There were marks in rows, none of which I could read.

The sailor touched one row and said a word I took to mean men. He touched another and said a word for land. Then he drew his finger across both.

"Exchange?" I asked.

He nodded eagerly. It was the first word of mine he had understood without asking again.

"Men ashore for men here?"

He nodded again, though I could not tell whether he meant all of us or some.

"Who will be sent?" I asked.

He looked back to the foreigner with the belt. They spoke for a while. The foreigner made a small cutting motion with his hand, separating the prisoners into groups.

The master of the junk had come close enough to hear.

"Tell him my men are not yours to offer," he said.

"I know."

"Then say it."

I turned to the sailor.

"The master must speak for his crew," I said. "The people here are from different vessels. I cannot promise them."

The sailor translated part of it. The foreigner with the belt looked at the master and asked him something. The master answered with his own name and the name of his junk. The sailor seemed not to understand the second name.

The woman in blue stepped forward.

"He is the master," she said in the foreigner's tongue, carefully. She pointed to the junk. "That vessel."

The sailor repeated her words. The foreigner looked from her to the master and made another mark on the board.

She had been able to say what I could not. The master looked at her with surprise and then bowed his head a little.

The foreigner with the belt told the sailor something else. The sailor turned to me.

"You can go to the shore if a boat comes. You can tell them about our men."

"And the others?"

He did not answer.

The boy by the injured sailor was watching us. He had heard the word shore. He stood, leaving one hand on the sailcloth, and took a step toward me.

I wanted to tell him that a way home had opened. I could not tell whether it had opened for him.

The foreigner with the writing board drew a line beneath the last mark and turned away. The guard moved us back toward the room.

I looked once more at the injured sailor. The boy had knelt again and was holding the cup. The older woman sat beside him, looking not at the ship but at the water between it and the land.

The boy lifted the cup to the injured man's mouth again. The writing board had gone with the guard.

---

They did not send me ashore.

Instead they gave me a piece of cloth and told me to hold it where it could be seen from the landing. I stood by the rail with a guard at my side while a man above us worked a line through a block. The cloth was pale and had a red edge. It might have meant peace. It might have meant that the ship wanted someone to come out.

I asked the sailor who knew the harbor speech.

"Tell them we have men here," he said.

"Which men?"

He looked at the guard, then toward the shore.

"Men they know."

I lowered the cloth.

The guard reached for it. I held it up again before he could take my arm. I had learned how quickly a gesture could become an accusation on this deck.

Across the water, people stood on the landing. I could see movement beneath the eaves where Hasan's clerk had taken Tahir's message. A narrow boat left the shore, stopped, and turned back. Another came out farther down the harbor and held its place against the current.

The sailor shouted to the men on the foreign ship. They lowered a small boat with two rowers. One carried the end of a line. The other held a board with marks on it. They went toward the boat waiting beyond the shallows.

I watched them meet. The two boats did not touch. A man on the harbor boat held up a bundle. The stranger with the board pointed toward the ship, then toward the land. They shouted across the gap between them.

The current carried the harbor boat away before they had finished.

It came back a little later. The man with the bundle had taken off his headcloth and tied it around the bundle instead. He held it up again. The stranger's boat moved closer. Something small passed from one to the other.

I could not tell what.

The guard beside me said something and laughed. I looked at him. He had been standing with one foot against the rail, watching the same exchange. His laugh was not for me. Another sailor had made a joke I could not hear.

I thought of how many times I had mistaken a man's face for his intention that day.

They took me below when the boats separated.

The woman in blue was sitting beside the younger woman, whose ear had stopped bleeding. The man with the wet jacket had been brought into the room. He held his knees and looked at me as I entered.

"Did you see a boat?" he asked.

"Two. They spoke."

"From the ruler?"

"I could not see who sent them."

He looked disappointed, as if I had chosen not to know.

The woman in blue asked whether I had seen any of the prisoners sent away. I told her I had not.

"Then what did they exchange?" she asked.

"I could not see."

She nodded. Her hands were folded in her lap. I had seen those hands grip the water bowl firmly enough that the younger woman could drink without spilling it.

A sailor came to the door with bread and a vessel of water. He gave the food to the guard, who placed it on the floor. The man with the wet jacket reached for a piece. The younger woman was closer but did not move. The woman in blue divided the bread with her hands and gave each person a share.

I took mine and waited.

"Eat," she said.

"I thought the boy outside might need it."

"The boy outside has people near him. You have not eaten since morning."

"Neither have you."

She looked at me.

"Then we will both eat."

The bread was hard and had a sour smell. I chewed it slowly. I did not know what grain it had been made from. The woman in blue saved a piece for the younger woman, who could not bear the noise of chewing and sat with her hands over her ears.

Later the sailor came back for me.

The foreigner with the belt was waiting near the mast. He had taken off his outer garment and rolled his sleeves above the elbows. The man with the writing board stood beside him. A third man, older than the others I had seen, held a small object wrapped in cloth.

They asked me to sit.

The sailor pointed at the wrapped object. The older man opened it. Inside were pieces of gold, some worked into small shapes and others flattened. I had seen gold carried that way before, but never by men who could not tell me where they had obtained it.

The sailor asked whether it was good.

I looked at the pieces.

"I cannot judge them from here."

He moved the cloth closer.

I had handled gold in markets and courts. I knew that its weight could be tested and its color compared. I also knew that a man who wanted to be paid would hear a promise in any word I used.

"It looks like gold," I said.

The sailor translated. The older foreigner smiled. He covered the pieces again.

"More," the sailor said.

"More what?"

"Gold."

"For whom?"

He pointed at me.

I looked toward the prisoners by the mast. The junk's master was sitting apart from them, a guard nearby. The boy with the injured man had been allowed to stay close to the sailcloth. The older woman was not in sight.

"For all of us?" I asked.

The sailor turned to the foreigner with the belt. They spoke. The sailor looked back.

"For you."

"And the others?"

He spread his hands.

I asked about the injured sailor. He pointed toward the far side of the deck, where the sailcloth lay beneath an awning. I asked about the boy, the master and the older woman. He repeated my words one at a time, then said something I could not follow.

The foreigner with the belt grew impatient. He tapped the cloth holding the gold and then tapped the rail.

"Shore," the sailor said. "You go."

I looked at the gold again.

"Who brought that?"

He answered with a word that might have meant people from the land.

"Whose gold is it?"

He did not understand.

I tried the question differently. "Who gave it to you?"

He pointed toward the harbor.

I thought of Hasan's clerk, who had counted messages and carriers while the guns sounded. I thought of the ruler, whose name men used for orders that he had not yet given. I thought of merchants who could produce gold quickly but would expect something in return.

The foreigner with the belt asked a question. The sailor turned to me.

"Your father will send more?"

"I do not know."

The foreigner looked displeased. He spoke to the older man, who folded the cloth and took it away.

"Do you know the names of the men from this ship who are on land?" I asked.

The sailor shook his head.

"Then how can I ask for them?"

He looked at the writing board. The man holding it turned a page and showed him a row of marks.

"Men ashore," the sailor said. "Return."

"Are they alive?"

He did not answer.

I asked again. He looked at the foreigner with the belt. The foreigner gave a short order, and the sailor stepped back.

The guard took me toward the prisoners.

As we passed the mast, the junk's master stood.

"What did they offer?" he asked.

"To send me ashore."

"For what?"

"Gold."

He looked at the wrapped object in the older foreigner's hand.

"Whose gold?"

"I do not know."

"And us?"

"I asked."

"What did they say?"

"They did not say."

The master sat down. He looked at the boy beside the sailcloth.

"Then they have bought nothing yet," he said.

I wanted to tell him that I had refused to promise more gold. He had not asked whether I had been brave.

The guard pushed me toward the doorway. I stopped long enough to look at the injured sailor. The boy had taken the cloth away from his face. The man's eyes were open, but he did not turn when I called.

The boy raised his hand to stop me coming closer.

"He is sleeping," he said.

I nodded.

"Can you still keep the cup steady?" I asked.

He looked at the cup in his hand.

"Yes."

The guard moved me on.

Inside the room, the woman in blue asked what had happened. I told her about the gold, the questions, and the offer to send me ashore.

"Did you agree?" she asked.

"I did not know what I would be agreeing to."

"That is an answer."

"Not one they liked."

She looked at the younger woman, who was sleeping against the chest.

"Will they take you out again?"

"Perhaps."

"And if they do?"

I did not know. The foreigner had shown me gold and the shore. He had not shown me the people who would be left behind.

The man with the wet jacket leaned against the wall.

"If they let you go," he said, "you can tell the ruler we are here."

"I can."

"You can tell him to send men for us."

"I can tell him what I saw. I cannot make him send them."

He looked at me with tired contempt.

"You speak as if every promise must be weighed before it is made."

I thought of the boats at Laoe, the wounded people whose names I had written, and the men who had followed orders I had not understood.

"Some promises have been too light," I said.

The woman in blue shook her head.

"Then do not make another one to ease this room."

The ship moved. Water slapped against its side. From somewhere above came the sound of a bell or a piece of metal struck against another. I had no way to tell whether it marked the hour, an order, or a warning.

I sat down beside the bowl and waited for the door to open.

Through the wall came a scrape of rope and a man's voice calling from the water. I could not tell whether a boat was arriving or leaving.

---

They woke me by touching my shoulder with the end of a pole.

I had been sitting against the wall. My head had fallen forward, and my neck hurt when I lifted it. The room was dim. The woman in blue was awake, watching the doorway. The younger woman still had the cloth pressed against her ear.

The guard pointed at me.

I stood.

"Where?" the woman in blue asked.

He did not answer. She spoke to him in the few words she knew. He looked toward the deck and said something short.

"He says you are going out," she told me.

"I will ask about you."

"Ask about the water first."

I looked at the bowl. It was empty.

She held it up. The guard took it and called to someone outside. Another sailor came with water and poured it into the bowl. The woman in blue thanked him. He looked surprised, then went away.

"Now ask," she said.

I followed the guard.

The sun had moved behind the ship's high side. The harbor was no longer bright enough for me to see faces on the landing. I could make out boats moving near shore, and farther out a dark line of clouds gathering over the passage.

A small boat waited alongside the foreign ship. It had a local hull and a crew of four. Two men stood near its stern, one holding a folded cloth. The other wore a cap I recognized from Hasan's storehouse, though I did not know his name.

The foreigner with the belt stood by the rail. The sailor who spoke some trade words was beside him. The older man with the wrapped gold was not there.

The sailor pointed toward the waiting boat.

"Go," he said.

I looked around the deck.

The junk's master was near the mast with two of his men. His headcloth had been returned to him. He was speaking to a foreigner who held a rope. Beyond them, the injured sailor lay on the sailcloth. The boy had placed a folded cover beneath his head. The older woman was beside them with her basket.

"Are they going too?" I asked.

The sailor looked toward the master. He spoke to the foreigner with the belt, who gestured toward the junk. It was still afloat a short distance away, held by two lines.

"Boat," the sailor said.

"Which boat?"

He pointed at the junk.

"Will they take it to shore?"

He nodded.

I had learned how little a nod could settle.

I walked toward the master. The guard moved to stop me, but the foreigner with the belt raised his hand. The guard stepped aside.

The master looked at me.

"They say I may take my vessel," he said.

"With your men?"

"Some."

"Who stays?"

"They have not told me."

He looked toward the injured sailor.

"The boy will not leave him."

"Can he be moved?"

"On the sail, if they give us time."

"Will they?"

The master rubbed his face.

"They want the junk away from their side. They do not care how long the boy needs."

I looked at the boy. He was holding the injured man's hand. He had stopped asking me what would happen.

"Do you want me to ask for more time?" I said.

The master shook his head.

"Ask them to keep their ropes loose. I can take the hull myself."

I turned to the sailor and repeated the request. He did not understand the words for loose and tight. The master came with me, took a length of rope from the deck, and showed him what he meant. He pulled it taut, then let it slacken.

The sailor understood. He spoke to the foreigner with the belt, who gave an order to the men at the rail.

The master went back to his crew.

It was the first useful thing I had done since they had taken my knife, and it had begun with the master's instruction.

The older woman rose when she saw me. She held out her basket.

"Will you take this ashore?" she asked.

"Are you not going with the junk?"

"I will go where they let me. But my son may have reached the landing. If you see a young man with a scar on his left shoulder, tell him I was on this ship."

"What is his name?"

She told me. I repeated it until she was satisfied.

"Tell him I waited," she said.

"I will."

She lowered the basket. She had not meant to give it to me. She had meant to make me stop long enough to listen.

Near the doorway, the woman in blue had been brought out with the younger woman and one of the men from the room. They stood apart from the prisoners being prepared for the junk. A foreigner pointed them toward the far side of the deck.

I went to the sailor.

"Those women," I said. "Are they leaving?"

He looked at them, then at the foreigner with the belt. They spoke. The foreigner shook his head.

"No," the sailor said.

"Why?"

He spread his hands.

"Ask him."

The sailor looked troubled. He repeated my question, but the foreigner with the belt turned away to give another order.

I approached the woman in blue. A guard stood between us. She raised her hand before I could speak.

"Do not tell me you have asked," she said. "I saw you."

"They will not send you now."

"Then remember that."

The younger woman leaned against her shoulder. The woman in blue adjusted the cloth over her ear.

"Do you have family on shore?" I asked.

"We have people who will look for us."

"Names?"

She gave me two names and a place near the river. I repeated them. The guard shifted his weight, impatient.

"Do not tell them we were released," she said.

"I will not."

"Do not tell them we are dead."

"I will tell them I saw you alive."

She nodded.

It was a narrow promise. It was one I could keep.

The foreigner with the belt called for me. The local boat had drawn close enough for a ladder to be lowered. One of its rowers held the ladder steady with a pole.

I went to the rail. The sailor who knew the trade words stood beside me.

"Your people bring gold," he said.

"Whose gold?"

He did not answer.

"How much?"

He held up his hand as though I had asked him to count something he could not count in my language. Then he pointed toward the boat.

"Go."

I looked at the junk. Its crew were working the ropes. The master stood near the stern, giving orders in a voice that carried across the water. The boy was still beside the injured man. The older woman had taken her basket to the place where the men were preparing to lower the sailcloth.

They were moving. That was all I knew.

The foreigner with the belt stepped closer. He held out my working knife by its wooden handle. I took it, surprised. The blade was clean. He pointed toward the ladder.

I put the knife back in its sheath.

There was no reason to thank him for returning what he had taken. I inclined my head because I wanted to leave without another misunderstanding.

The ladder swayed as I descended. A man in the local boat caught my arm when I reached the last rung. I stepped onto the bottom boards and crouched until the boat pulled away.

The man in the cap from Hasan's storehouse looked at me.

"Are you hurt?"

"My arm. Nothing that will keep me from walking."

"Who else is coming?"

"The master says he can take his junk. I do not know who will be allowed with him."

The man glanced toward the foreign ship.

"We were told you would be released."

"Who told you?"

"Men from that ship."

"Did they say anything about the others?"

He shook his head.

The rowers pulled hard. The boat moved away from the high hull and into the current. I looked back. The junk was turning slowly. Its sail remained furled. Men on the foreign ship were casting off lines. The master was at the stern, but I could no longer see the boy.

On the upper deck, the woman in blue stood beside the younger woman. She was too far away for me to know whether she could see us. I raised my hand.

She did not raise hers.

I could not blame her. I was leaving.

The man in the cap asked whether the foreigners had demanded anything.

"Gold," I said.

"How much?"

"I did not see a count."

"Was it paid?"

"I saw gold shown. I saw something pass between boats. I do not know what they agreed."

He looked toward the shore.

"They will ask you."

"I know."

The boat passed a piece of floating timber. One rower shifted his oar to avoid it. Another pointed toward the outer passage, where the current was carrying broken wood away from the harbor.

I thought of the older woman's son. He might have been on shore already. He might have been beyond the passage. I had promised to give his name to anyone who could look for him. I had not promised that I would find him.

We came close enough to the landing for me to see people waiting beneath the storehouse eaves. Some held bundles. Others had nothing in their hands. They were watching the boat as if the number of people aboard would answer every question they had carried there.

I stood too early. The boat rocked. A rower told me to sit until we touched the steps.

I sat.

The first person I recognized on shore was Tahir. His nephew stood beside him with his injured hand wrapped in cloth. Safi was farther back, speaking to a man I did not know. Both were alive.

I wanted to call to them. I wanted to tell them that I had survived.

Then I saw the faces behind them and understood how little that would answer.

The boat struck the landing. Tahir caught the bowline. I stepped ashore.

The foreign ship remained in the harbor, and there were people on it whose names I had only just learned.

---

Tahir did not let me leave the landing until he had counted the men in the boat.

There were four rowers, the man from Hasan's storehouse, and me. Tahir pointed to each in turn. His nephew held the bowline with his uninjured hand. When the count was finished, Tahir asked the rowers whether they had brought anyone else aboard during the crossing.

"No," the eldest said.

"Did anyone fall into the water?"

"No."

Tahir made a mark on his board.

I had imagined that the first thing I would hear on shore would be my name. Instead I heard a boat owner asking the questions that kept his men from vanishing into other people's accounts.

Safi came down the steps. His sleeve was dry now, but there was a dark stain at the cuff.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then walk with me."

We went beneath the storehouse eaves. Ruma was there with two of her crew, sorting wet covers from dry ones. She looked up as we passed.

"Is the junk coming back?" she asked.

"The master was taking it away from the foreign ship when I left."

"Was it afloat?"

"Yes."

"Was its sail up?"

"No."

She nodded, then went back to the covers. I had given her the only answers I knew.

Safi led me to a bench. Hasan's clerk sat at the end of it with his board and a new strip of writing material. He looked at my arm, then at my face.

"Start with where you left the watch boat," he said.

I told him about the injured man we had lifted from the water and the dead man beside him. Safi corrected the direction of the current and the side of the junk we had approached. I told him about the ladder, the injured sailor with the broken leg, the boy, and the foreign ship closing the passage.

"Who ordered you onto the junk?" the clerk asked.

"No one. I asked the men above if I could help."

Safi said, "The boat owner agreed to bring him alongside once. I agreed that we would leave with the men already aboard."

The clerk made a mark.

"Then you went without us," Safi said.

"Yes."

"Did you expect us to come back?"

"I hoped you would."

"That is different."

"I know."

He looked at me for a moment.

"I turned toward you once," he said. "The owner would not take the boat under the foreign hull. He was right."

"I saw you."

"Good. Then you know I did not abandon you."

"I know."

The clerk made another mark. He had not asked which of us was brave.

I told them how the boarding had begun. I described the ropes, the men climbing down, and the way the injured sailor had been moved on sailcloth. I tried to remember the order in which the master, the boy, and the older woman had been taken up to the foreign ship.

The clerk stopped me.

"You have said the boy was beside the injured man when you were taken away. Later you saw him on the foreign ship. Did you see him climb?"

"No."

"Then do not put him on the ladder in your account."

I looked at the marks on his board.

"How will you write it?"

"He was on the junk when you left. You next saw him on the foreign ship. What happened between is unknown to you."

It was the most accurate sentence anyone had made from my story that day.

I told them about the people held in the room, the woman in blue who could speak a few words to the foreigners, and the younger woman with the injured ear. I gave the names and the place near the river that the woman had asked me to remember. The clerk repeated them until I agreed he had heard correctly.

"Are they dead?" he asked.

"They were alive when I left."

"Are they free?"

"No."

"Are they still aboard?"

"They were when I left."

He made three separate marks.

When I told him about the gold, he set down his writing material.

"Who paid?"

"I do not know."

"Did you see it counted?"

"No."

"Did you agree to a sum?"

"No."

"Did you promise more?"

"No."

"Did you ask for anyone else to be released?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

I named the injured sailor, the boy, the junk's master, the older woman, and the two women from the other vessel. I said the master had been preparing to take his junk back, but I had not seen it reach shore.

The clerk looked at Safi.

"Has anyone reported that junk at the landings?"

"Not yet."

"Then leave its return open."

I had wanted to tell the story in the order it happened. The clerk was making me tell it in the order that could be checked.

Jamil arrived while we were still speaking. He listened without sitting. When I finished, he asked Safi for his account separately.

Safi gave it. He did not repeat my words. He described the watch boat's owner, the rescued men, the point at which the foreign hull closed the passage, and his return to shore. He said that the owner had been willing to make one approach and no more.

Jamil asked whether the owner would sail again.

"Not until he knows whether his boat has been damaged," Safi said.

"Then he will not be ordered."

Safi nodded.

Jamil turned to me.

"Did the foreigners know who you were?"

"They asked my name, my father, and where I came from."

"Who answered?"

"I did."

"Who translated?"

"A sailor who knew some harbor words. I do not know his name."

"Did you hear what he told the others?"

"Only parts."

"Then you cannot tell me what they believe."

"No."

Jamil looked toward the harbor. The foreign ship was still visible beyond the roofs.

"Your name has already been carried ashore," he said. "Men say the foreigners took a prince and released him for gold."

"Did they say who gave the gold?"

"Three men have claimed to know. None was in the boat that brought you."

I thought of the older foreigner unwrapping the pieces on the deck. I had seen the gold. I had not seen its source.

"Do you believe them?" I asked.

"Which men?"

I did not answer. He had made the point without raising his voice.

Jamil told the clerk to keep the account open. He asked Tahir to record every boat that returned from the outer passage. He asked Ruma whether she could receive injured people if they came ashore. She said she could, provided someone brought water and cloth and did not put the wounded on her repaired hull.

Jamil agreed.

Then he told me to come with him.

We went toward the court by a path above the landing. People moved aside when they recognized Jamil. Some looked at me. One man called my name. I turned, but he was asking whether I had seen his brother on the foreign ship.

"What is his name?" I asked.

He told me.

I had not heard it.

"Did you see him?" he asked.

"I do not know."

He stared at me as if I had refused to look.

Jamil waited while I repeated the name to the clerk who had followed us. The clerk wrote it down.

"Do not say he was there," Jamil told him.

"I will write that the family asked."

We continued.

The ruler received us with two men who had been speaking about the harbor. A map of the channels lay on the floor between them. I recognized the inner landing and the shoals, though the lines were not drawn as I would have drawn them.

Jamil gave his account first. He described the attack, the boats that had withdrawn, the messages that had reached shore, and the vessels still unaccounted for. He did not call it a victory or a defeat.

Then the ruler asked me what I had seen.

I told him.

He asked about the foreign ship's men on shore. I said I had been told some had not returned, but I had not seen them. He asked whether I had promised to send them back. I said I had asked how a message could be carried, and that the foreigners had spoken of an exchange.

"Did you offer men who are in my care?" he asked.

"No."

"Did you offer men who are not in your care?"

"No."

"Did they believe you had authority to offer them?"

"I do not know."

The ruler looked at Jamil.

"That is a dangerous answer."

"It is the only one I can give," I said.

The ruler looked back at me.

"Then keep giving it when it is true."

He asked whether I had seen gold delivered. I told him what I had seen and what I had not. One of the men beside him said that a merchant had already demanded repayment for gold taken from his store. Another said the merchant had offered the gold willingly to recover the prince.

"Which is it?" the ruler asked.

Neither man answered.

I had returned alive, and already men were using my return to make claims against one another.

The ruler told Jamil to investigate the payment separately from the release. He ordered that the names of missing harbor people be collected at the landings, without calling them dead or captive until someone had evidence. He asked about the foreign visitors still ashore and received no answer he trusted.

Then he turned to me.

"You had responsibility for agreed routes," he said. "Do you still believe you can carry it?"

"I can carry messages."

"That is not what I asked."

I looked at Jamil. He did not help me.

"I do not know which owners would still agree," I said.

"Then you will not speak for them."

"No."

"You will give your account to the men who must decide what to do next. After that, you will wait."

I bowed my head.

The order left me free to walk the harbor and without authority to send a single boat. I had come back expecting to be asked how I escaped. They wanted an account that other people could check.

When we left, Jamil did not speak until we reached the path above the harbor.

"Nasir's sister came again this morning," he said.

I stopped.

"Has there been news?"

"No."

"Did anyone search?"

"She has sent another inquiry. She asked whether you had returned."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you had not yet come ashore."

I looked down at the water. The light was fading, and the vessels near the landing were beginning to look alike.

"Tell her I am back," I said.

"She will hear."

"Tell her I have not forgotten Nasir."

Jamil turned toward me.

"Go and tell her yourself."

He walked on.

I stayed where I was for a moment. Below me, Tahir was still at the landing, counting boats. Ruma had laid clean cloth beside a jar of water. Safi was speaking with the owner of the watch boat. None of them had stopped working because I had returned.

Beyond the shoals, the foreign ship stood against the darkening sky. Somewhere aboard it were people who had asked me to remember their names. Somewhere on the water was the junk I had left. I could not see whether it was moving.

I had a name that men now used to explain a payment I had not made and a bargain I had not heard.

That evening I went to find Nasir's sister.
