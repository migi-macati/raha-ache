A narrow strip of the new step was white when the water fell away from it. Yesterday the wood had been dark all over. I could see where Raya had cut the bad end from Madan's timber, and where a pale line of mud had dried across the grain. The river had risen over it during the night.

Raya was already on her knees at the edge. She had taken off one sandal and put her bare foot against the timber. Her nephew held the other sandal in both hands, as if he had been given something breakable.

"Don't stand there," she told me.

I stopped on the old step.

"Did it move?"

"Come where I can see you without looking up."

I went to the path above her. She pushed the edge with her heel, then reached beneath it. When she brought her hand out, mud ran between her fingers. She wiped it on the bank.

"The water has taken some of the loose earth. The sound piece is still seated. I want to see it after another high water."

"Can the smaller boats use it today?"

"One at a time. With their lines on the old upper post. Yesterday's load was two jars and a basket, carried separately. Do not tell anyone I tested more."

"I remember."

"You remembered a rail made of split wood yesterday."

Her nephew looked down at the sandal. I had hoped he would not hear that.

Raya asked him for the short stick he had brought. She put it into the soft earth below the step and showed me how easily it sank. Then she pressed it against the firmer bank above.

"Here is what holds a foot. There is still nothing down there to take the pull of a boat."

The sounding stake stood farther out, upright in the shallows. Its shadow lay across the water like a line drawn in the wrong place. A man on a passing canoe called to ask whether he could tie there. Before I could answer, Raya raised her stick.

"Sounding. Not tying."

He laughed, or perhaps he was annoyed. His canoe passed without stopping.

"Did the owner bring your covering?" I asked.

"No."

"Did you ask him?"

"I told him to speak when he knew what he could spare. I will not go begging for the price of my work while he tells the river he built the step."

She took the sandal from her nephew and stood. Her knee had left a wet print on the new wood.

"I will look again when the water comes back. If you want someone to carry jars, find the people who agreed to carry them. I have work at home."

She walked up the path without waiting for me. Her nephew ran after her, carrying the stick. I watched them go until the path bent behind the houses.

A little below the landing Dala had spread a length of cord across a flat stone. The cord was dark where it had been in the water. She drew it through her fingers and stopped at each frayed place. Pawi held the broken corner of her frame upright. A thin splinter stood out where the joint had been forced apart.

"That will catch the net again," I said.

"It already has."

"Could the sound part be used?"

Dala turned the frame to show me a knot pulled hard against the split. The cord had worn a groove into the wood. She could cut it free, but then she would have less length to tie the next corner.

"Are you asking because you have brought me another piece?"

"No."

"Then I am showing you why I asked for one."

I looked toward the hall. The keeper had hung the strip recording Dala's claim where everyone could see it. From the landing I could make out the dark marks, but not the words the witnesses had agreed they meant.

"I can carry the frame to someone who can mend it," I said.

"Who?"

"I could ask Raya."

"She has just gone home."

"I know."

"And if she agrees, whose food will you give her? You are already asking people to wait for hers."

Pawi shifted the frame against his shoulder. The splinter caught his sleeve and he jerked away. Dala took the weight from him before it tore the cloth.

"Hold the sound side," she told him. "You need not stand where it hurts you."

He moved. She set the frame down, then looked at me.

"I have kept the broken cord. I have kept the pieces. The keeper has marked what I lost when that fish boat grounded. The mark has not brought back a catch."

"I haven't forgotten."

"I do not need you to remember for me."

She began drawing the sound cord into a coil. I had no answer that would mend the frame, so I stayed to hold the end while she wound it. She let me do that. When she was finished, she took the coil from my hands and put it beside the stone.

From the hall came the sound of a jar striking another jar. I went up to find the keeper moving the tally strips away from a patch of sunlight. Amaya stood beside her with a folded length of cloth over one arm. I thought for a moment that she had brought it for me.

"Raya has checked the step," I said. "It held through the high water."

"That is good," the keeper said. "What did she say we may use it for?"

"One small boat at the upper post. The same separate loads as yesterday. She will look again after the next tide."

The keeper repeated the terms before making a fresh mark. She left the older strip in place.

"Why keep yesterday's test beside today's?" I asked.

"Because yesterday happened. Today has not finished."

Amaya laid her cloth on a bench and smoothed the edge. It was not new. A line of small repairs ran along one side, each made with a different shade of thread.

"Is that for Raya?" I asked.

"It is for the screen above the sleeping place. The rain has found the seam."

"I thought perhaps we could give her something now."

"You can give her something of yours."

"I have nothing she asked for."

"Then you have not paid her."

Amaya folded the cloth again. I wanted to tell her that a working step might bring enough trade to settle all three claims. The thought had been following me since I woke. It had sounded more useful before I heard it spoken.

"I still owe you one," I said.

"You do."

"Would you let me repay Raya first, if I can find a piece?"

"Find it first. Then ask the people who are owed whether they will wait. You cannot ask me to give away what is mine and call that your repayment."

She took the cloth into the inner room. The keeper watched her leave and went back to her strips. I stood beside the bench, looking at the empty place where the cloth had been.

The first buyer arrived before the sun reached the doorway.

Her boat was small enough to come to the upper post, but its middle lay low under a covering of woven mats. Two people worked the poles. A third sat among the baskets, holding the covering down whenever the breeze caught it. The woman at the bow called to ask whether the step was open.

"For a small load," I called back.

"Mine is small when I carry it in my hands."

She smiled as she said it, but she kept the boat outside the other vessel's line. I could see eight covered baskets arranged in two rows, with a pair of jars wedged between them. The lids had been tied against the damp. I could smell fish when the wind came from her boat.

She gave her name as Sera. She had bought the baskets from people farther along the river and intended to sell them before the day's heat took what little dryness they had left. The jars were for a different buyer. She wanted a place to set the baskets while the people who had come to meet her counted them.

"Can you use the upper post?" I asked.

"If no one takes it before me. I cannot leave my baskets on a boat all day."

"The step has been tested for one person carrying one load."

"Then let us carry one at a time. I have hands."

She turned and pointed to the two people with poles. The older one shook his head before she had finished.

"We have hands for the boat," he said. "I agreed to bring you here. I have not agreed to carry your baskets up the bank."

Sera turned back to me.

"I can pay people here."

A few porters had gathered on the path, drawn by the smell and the promise of work. One was the man who had led me to the salted-fish owner yesterday. He lifted a basket to judge its weight without taking it from the boat. He named the food he wanted for a morning's carrying. Sera offered him a smaller share. They began to bargain.

I was relieved to hear it. Work was coming to the bank. A boat had chosen our step after the larger owner had refused it. I imagined the keeper making a new strip, the goods moving up the path, a share remaining in the hall. I imagined Raya going home with her covering and Dala with new cord. I could almost see Amaya unfolding the cloth I would bring her.

"Where will the baskets stand?" the keeper asked.

"Beside the upper path," I said.

"Whose place?"

I looked toward Dala's drying rack. It had been returned to the place her sister chose yesterday. The ground above it was narrow, but there was a patch near the path where a few baskets might stand without touching the rack.

"Here," I said, stepping into it.

Dala had come up behind us. She did not raise her voice.

"That is where I spread the net when the rack is wet."

"You are not using it now."

"I will be when the sun reaches it."

Sera leaned from her boat.

"I need it only until the baskets are counted."

"Then count them on your boat," Dala said.

"They must be seen by the people buying them."

"Let them come to the boat."

The older poleman looked toward the current. He had brought the vessel close enough for us to speak, but he had not tied it. His arms were beginning to tremble with the effort of holding her against the water.

Sera looked at the patch of ground again. She was trying to measure it with her eyes.

"What if I put four baskets there and bring the others later?"

Dala lifted the broken frame from Pawi's shoulder.

"Then you will put four baskets where I need to lay this."

The porter who had been bargaining with Sera set his hands on his hips.

"I can carry them farther up."

"To where?" the keeper asked.

"The hall."

Amaya had come back to the doorway. She stood with the repaired cloth still folded over her arm.

"The hall is not empty," she said. "And I have not offered it."

Sera's smile had gone. She turned to me.

"Do you have a landing or do you have a path where everyone may say no?"

I felt the question in my face. A boat was waiting before me, its owner willing to trade, and I was the man who had spent so much of the last week asking people to believe this bank could hold one.

"We can find a place," I said.

"Before the water turns?"

"I will see that you have the first use of the step tomorrow if you wait today. We can take a share of what you sell here and use it to pay for the rest of the work."

The keeper put down her marking tool.

"Whose share?"

"The landing's."

"From which goods?"

"From the baskets she brings."

"She has not brought them ashore."

"She is here."

Sera had heard us. She looked from the keeper to me.

"Are you asking me to give a share before you have given me a place?"

"No. After you sell."

"Then what are you promising for tomorrow?"

"First use."

The keeper lifted the strip for the upper step.

"Yesterday we agreed one boat could test it. Raya must inspect it again after the next high water. We have no second berth. I cannot mark a promise to put this woman's boat ahead of people who have not come, or to spend a share she has not agreed to give."

"I am trying to keep the trade here," I said.

"I know what you are trying to do."

She did not lower her voice. The porters could hear her, and so could Sera's crew. The keeper held out the strip to me, but I could not make its marks say what I wanted.

Sera bent and lifted the lid of the nearest basket. Inside, the fish were laid flat in layers, their edges pale with salt. She pressed one with her thumb, then showed me the dampness beneath the top layer.

"I can sell these today," she said. "Tomorrow I will have to explain why they smell different."

"Could you leave the jars and return with the baskets?"

"The jars belong to another buyer. If I separate the loads, I must pay the crew to make two journeys. Who will pay for that?"

I had no answer. She closed the lid and tied it again.

The porter who had offered to carry her baskets looked away. He had hoped for a morning's food and now had none. The older poleman pushed his boat half a length from the bank to keep its bow from striking the old post. Sera had not tied a line. She could still choose.

"Across the river they have a place to set the baskets," she said. "I did not come here to ask for your household's floor."

"You could come back when the step is ready for more," I said.

"Tell me when it is. Do not tell me to wait for a thing you have not been given."

She paid the porter a small piece of fish for the time he had spent lifting and judging the basket. He looked surprised, then put it inside his cloth. She gave the polemen their signal. The boat turned slowly, its loaded side coming around against the current. The man at the stern dug his pole into the bottom and brought the bow toward the far bank.

I watched until the covering became a single dark shape above the water.

"Could we have taken one basket?" I asked.

"Perhaps," the keeper said. "If she had agreed to leave it, if someone had bought it, and if there had been a place for it. None of those things happened."

Dala had begun carrying her frame back to the drying ground. Pawi followed with the sound cord. She did not look pleased that Sera had gone. She had wanted fish for the day as much as anyone. She had also wanted a place to mend the net that would catch her own.

I went to the path where I had offered to put the baskets. It was scarcely wider than my outstretched arms. The earth had dried enough to hold a load, but Dala's rack and the people going to the hall would have to pass through it. I had seen the empty patch and called it ours.

The keeper came down with two strips. On one she had marked Raya's inspection and the limits of the step. The other remained bare.

"What is that one for?" I asked.

"For the cargo you wanted to count."

"Nothing came ashore."

"Then there is nothing to mark."

She rolled the empty strip between her fingers and put it back into her basket. The marks for Dala's frame, Raya's covering and Amaya's cloth remained where she had left them. The new step had held through the night. It had not paid a single one.

Across the water Sera's boat was already alongside another landing. A man there took the first basket from her hands and set it on dry ground. I could see the motion plainly. I could not hear what they agreed to pay.

* * *

From our bank I could see Sera's baskets in a row beneath a roof made of palm leaves. I had watched the first one come ashore yesterday. By morning the row had shortened, and I could not tell whether she had sold the others or moved them out of the sun.

The keeper was hanging a new strip beside the old ones when I passed the hall.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"Across."

"For the baskets?"

"For the woman who owns them."

The keeper turned the strip so that its unmarked side faced her.

"Do you have a claim on her?"

"No."

"Then go without taking one of ours."

I had no tally in my hand. I had not thought I was taking anything. She looked at the cloth I wore and the empty cord around my waist.

"Yesterday you offered her first use of the step," she said. "She did not accept it. If she speaks to you today, she speaks to you, not to this landing."

"I know."

I had been saying that often. It was becoming an answer I gave before I had worked out what I meant.

There was no boat waiting to carry me across. The small vessels that used the upper post were unloading for the morning, and their crews had places to go afterward. I walked downriver until I found a man pushing a narrow canoe away from the reeds. He had a bundle of split bamboo behind his seat and was making for the opposite bank.

"Will you take another person?" I asked.

"Where?"

"To the market under the palm roof."

"That's farther than I meant to go."

"I can help with the bundle."

He looked at my hands.

"Will you carry it when we land?"

"If it belongs where you're going."

"It belongs at my sister's house. Not at the market."

I looked toward the far bank. His sister's house was upstream of the roof. I would have to walk back along the shore.

"I'll carry it there."

"And who brings it out of the canoe?"

"I do."

He moved his foot to make room. I climbed in, keeping my weight low while he pushed us clear. The canoe rocked beneath me. I had crossed the river many times and still disliked the moment when neither bank could be reached with a foot.

The bundle was tied with two loops of cord. The man told me to leave it where it lay until he had brought the canoe against the far shore. Halfway across, the current caught our bow and turned us. I reached for the bundle to keep it from rolling.

"Leave it," he said.

"It's moving."

"It can move. You cannot. Sit."

I sat. The bundle shifted a hand's width and stopped against the seat. He worked the paddle until the canoe pointed upstream again. I had offered him my hands and made them another thing he had to watch.

His sister's house stood above a narrow strip of stones. The canoe could come close, but not far enough to put the bundle directly on dry ground. He tied it to a root and stepped out first. I waited until he told me to lift.

The bamboo was longer than I had judged. I took the weight across one shoulder, and the ends struck the branches as I turned. The man walked ahead, calling which way the path bent. His sister was outside with a shallow basket on her lap. She looked up when she saw me.

"You brought someone," she said.

"He brought himself."

She showed me where to lay the bundle. It was not beside the door. It belonged under the eaves where the cut ends could dry. I put it there and stepped back.

"Do you want food?" she asked.

I was hungry. I could smell something cooking inside.

"I came to carry the bundle," I said.

"That was for him," she answered, pointing to her brother. "I asked whether you want food."

I took the small piece she offered. She watched me eat it before returning to her basket. Her brother untied the loops and counted the pieces of bamboo. I had carried the bundle, but he was still responsible for what arrived.

I went toward the market with the taste of warm rice on my tongue.

The far-bank path was crowded with people coming away from the river. Some carried empty baskets, others had jars against their hips. A child dragged a mat by one corner, leaving a dark line in the dust. Two women were arguing over a measure of grain. Neither looked at me as I passed. Here I was another person trying to find a place to stand.

Sera was kneeling beside her remaining baskets. She had opened one and spread the fish on a mat, turning the damp pieces toward the air. The smell was stronger than it had been on the boat. A buyer stood over her with a small knife, testing the flesh near a bone.

"You came," she said when she saw me.

"Your boat did."

"My boat came here. You walked."

The buyer cut a piece from the fish and tasted it. He shook his head, but did not leave. Sera waited until he named a price. She answered with another. They were still talking when I moved to the side of the roof.

One of her polemen recognized me. He was sitting on an overturned basket, rubbing a blister beneath his thumb.

"Is your step bigger today?" he asked.

"No."

"Then don't tell her to go back."

"I haven't."

He bent over his hand again. I watched him pull a thin strip of skin away and wince.

When the buyer left with two fish, Sera covered the rest.

"Did you come to buy?" she asked.

"I came to ask about a boat."

"You saw mine yesterday."

"Not yours. Someone I met years ago."

She folded her mat without looking at me.

"Where?"

"At a landing on my first journey away from home."

"Which landing?"

I tried to picture the shore. A post near the shallows. A fisherman with his net. A house with a repaired roof. A buyer who counted baskets. The people had been so particular about their own places that I had thought I would never forget them. Now I could not tell her which bend of the coast had held the post.

"I don't know its name," I said.

"Who was the buyer?"

"I never asked."

She sat back on her heels.

"And you came across the river to ask me to find a boat from a place you cannot name?"

"A young man led it. His name was Mulaw. He was taking cloth that belonged to his crewman's sister. The crewman was called Nima."

Sera glanced at the poleman. He did not look up.

"Did the young man say he would look for you?"

"No."

"Did he tell you where he was going?"

"Not to a place I could find."

"Then why do you want him?"

I had prepared an answer while walking from the canoe. I wanted to ask Mulaw which buyers still dealt with the people he knew. I wanted to know whether someone who had once seen me carry baskets might listen now that I spoke for a landing in Maynila. I wanted him to remember that I had kept my word about a fisherman's post.

"He told me to ask buyers about boats," I said. "I didn't ask enough then."

"That sounds like advice."

"It was."

"Not a debt."

"No."

She drew the covering over the fish again. A little of the salt had collected at the bottom of the basket. She shook it out onto the mat.

"There are men who hear names on every river," she said. "If they remember yours, they may ask. But a name alone won't tell them where to go."

"Do you know one?"

"Not one who works for nothing."

I looked at the remaining baskets. She followed my eyes.

"I have sold four. I have to move the rest before the sun comes over this roof. If you carry them to the woman who bought them, I can ask the people who come for my jars whether they have heard of a Mulaw. I will ask once. If they say no, that is the end of it."

"Will they be going toward his landing?"

"I have just told you I don't know his landing."

I almost asked whether she could find another person who did. The question was already reaching for work she had not offered.

"Where are the baskets going?" I asked instead.

She pointed past the market to a narrow house with a red clay jar outside. It was farther than I expected. There were four baskets left. I had carried a bundle of bamboo and eaten a stranger's rice, and my shoulders were already sore.

"All four?"

"Two now. Two after I have counted them with the woman."

"What will she pay you?"

"That is between us."

I nodded. She lifted one basket, then set it down again.

"Don't take it until she has seen the lid. I don't want you saying I sent her an opened basket."

We waited for the woman to come. She wore a dark cloth at her waist and had a little boy beside her who carried a shallow bowl. She examined the knots on the first two lids, pressed the wicker where it was damp, and made Sera show her the fish beneath. They disagreed over one basket. The woman wanted the damp pieces set apart. Sera said they had been damp when she bought them, and that she had turned them in the morning. They settled on leaving that basket for another buyer.

Only three went to the house with the red jar.

I carried the first one against my chest. The wicker rubbed my arms where the scratches from the old landing had long since healed. The boy walked ahead of me and called to his mother whenever I reached a turn. He was proud of being able to show a stranger the way. I followed him because the basket was too wide for me to see the stones underfoot.

At the house the woman made me set it beside the jar, not under the eaves.

"The shade moves," she said. "I will carry it in when I have room."

I went back for the second. By then Sera had sold the damp basket to a man who wanted it for his household, at a price I did not hear. She was counting the pieces of fish in the last two baskets with her poleman. He held his sore hand above the wicker and used the other to turn the pieces.

"Are you still carrying?" he asked.

"Two more."

"Then carry. The tide won't wait for either of us."

The third basket was heavier than the first. I had to stop halfway and put it on a stone to change my grip. A girl coming from the river stepped around me without slowing. She carried a jar on her head with one hand resting lightly against its side. I watched her go and wondered how long I would have to work before a basket could be carried without thinking about it.

When I returned, Sera had closed the last lid.

"That is the third," she said. "The woman has them?"

"She saw each one."

"Good."

She gave me a small fish wrapped in a leaf. I did not reach for it.

"I thought you were going to ask about Mulaw."

"I said I would ask. I didn't say I would pay you with an answer."

"Then why the fish?"

"Because you carried my baskets."

I took it. She looked past me toward the water.

"The man collecting the jars may come after the next tide. If he comes, I will ask whether he knows a boat leader by that name. I will tell him you asked. I will not send him to find a man I cannot place, and I will not promise he will come to Maynila."

"Can you tell him Ache asked?"

"I can tell him what you said. Whether he remembers is his own affair."

"Will you come to our landing again?"

"If there is room for what I bring."

There was no anger in it. She had sold her fish here. I had carried three baskets and earned a fish of my own. I had crossed the river hoping for a name that would open a route, and the only route I had found was a path between a market and a house with a red jar.

The older poleman rose and stretched his sore hand. Sera called him to help with the empty baskets. He took one, tested its weight, and passed another to the younger man. She did not ask me to carry them. My work was finished.

I went back by the house with the split bamboo. The man who had brought me across was no longer there. His sister was hanging the pieces beneath the eaves, turning each cut end toward the moving air. She saw the fish in my hand.

"Did you find what you came for?" she asked.

"I found someone who may ask."

She set one piece of bamboo beside another and studied the gap between them.

"Then you have found someone with work of her own."

I did not know whether she meant it kindly. I thanked her for the rice and walked toward the river.

On our bank the new step was visible between the people carrying jars. No boat had tied to the sounding stake. Dala's rack stood where it had been returned. The keeper's strips hung beneath the hall roof, dark against the light. I had no cloth for Amaya and no cord for Dala. I had a fish that I could eat, and the possibility that Sera would remember a question when another buyer came.

I put the fish inside my cloth and went to find out whether Dala had sent for me.

* * *

Dala had not sent for me.

Pawi was at the rack, holding a length of cord against the broken corner while she drew a knot tight with her teeth. When I came near, she took the cord from her mouth and looked at my hands.

"What have you brought?"

I showed her the fish wrapped in its leaf. I had carried it from the far bank with the salt drying against my skin.

"Food," she said.

"I thought you might want it."

"Did I ask you to buy fish?"

"I earned it carrying baskets."

"Then eat it. You will be hungry again when you come to help."

She turned the frame so that I could see the split. The sound cord had been saved, but there was not enough of it to bind the corner where the wood had given way. The loose ends made a little brush against her palm.

"Can I hold that?"

"When I have cut the bad part away."

She worked the joint loose. Pawi braced the other side with his knee. I waited for her to tell me where to put my hands. She did not.

"You came before I called," she said.

"I saw the rack from the path."

"You have seen it every day."

I put the fish back inside my cloth. The leaf had begun to soften. I was afraid it would leak onto the only dry part of my shirt.

A boat came in above the new step, close enough to show the scrape marks on its bow. Its steersman waited until the narrow vessel at the upper post had cleared its line. I recognized the man at the forward thwart only when he stood to take a bundle from beneath the seat.

Sulad.

He had tied his hair back with a strip I did not know. His feet were pale where the wet cord of his sandals had rubbed them. He passed the bundle to another crewman before stepping onto the old upper path. He did not use the repaired step until the owner told him the load was light enough.

I started toward him.

"Not with my frame," Dala said.

I had lifted one hand to wave. She had thought I meant to take the wood from her.

"I'll come back."

"When I send," she said.

Sulad saw me from the path. He did not smile until the crewman behind him had carried the bundle clear of the water. Then he came down with a short length of thick line over his shoulder. One end was wound around a wooden toggle. The other had been spliced back into itself, leaving a swollen place that would not pass through a narrow ring.

"You're here," I said.

"I was hired to come here."

"By whom?"

"The owner of that boat."

I looked at the line.

"Is that your pay?"

"My pay is not a rope."

He shifted it so that the wet part lay against his back rather than mine. A man called from the vessel. Sulad turned and answered that he had the line.

"Will you stay?" I asked.

"Until the owner takes his cargo onward. I have to return this first."

He had been gone while Raya repaired the step. I wanted to tell him how we had made the bank hold. I wanted him to see the new timber and the keeper's marks, and to know that I had done more than wait for someone to let me ashore.

"Did you hear about the step?"

"I saw it when we came in."

"Raya says it will hold a small load."

"Then let her say what it holds."

He started up the path. I walked beside him. The line brushed his heel with each step, leaving a wet mark in the dust.

"I went across yesterday," I told him.

"To work?"

"To ask after someone."

"Did you find him?"

"No. Sera may ask a buyer if he comes."

"Who?"

"Mulaw."

Sulad stopped beside a post where the shade from the hall crossed the path. He moved the line from one shoulder to the other.

"The one with the cloth?"

"The one who led the boat. Nima was guarding the cloth."

"I remember."

"He may remember us too."

"He may."

"I helped them at the landing."

Sulad looked toward the river. The crewman he had answered was carrying two small jars up the bank, one at a time. Sulad waited until the second jar was set down before speaking.

"You paid a man to hold their bow," he said.

"Yes."

"That is what he did."

"He kept the cloth from being soaked."

"Did he?"

"The boat stopped striking the wash."

"For a little while. The cloth stayed aboard."

"I was there."

"So was I."

I remembered the silver in the man's hand, shining wet after he came out of the water. I had wanted to take it back because the cloth had not come ashore. He had held the boat for the time I paid him to hold it. That had been the whole bargain.

"Mulaw saw what I did," I said.

"He saw you hire someone. He also saw you tell Nima to bring his sister's cloth onto a fisherman's ground."

"I was trying to find a dry place."

"You hadn't asked the fisherman."

"I asked after."

"After Nima refused."

Sulad put his hand beneath the line to keep it from sliding. He did not speak as though he had been saving the memory to use against me. He was telling me which part of it had happened.

"I thought I had done him a favor," I said.

"You may have. I don't know what he thought. But the man with the pole was paid. The fisherman kept his ground. Nima kept the cloth aboard. You cannot make the cloth dry by remembering it that way."

I had repeated the story to myself during the crossing to Sera's market. A silver piece, a steady bow, a young boat leader who would remember my help. I had left out the fisherman's foot planted beside his net. I had left out Nima's question about who would answer to his sister if the bundle spoiled.

"Did Mulaw thank me?" I asked.

"I don't remember."

"Neither do I."

The crewman came back down the path and pointed to the line.

"The owner wants it on the stern before the tide turns."

"I am bringing it," Sulad said.

I reached for the end that hung near his hand.

"Let me carry some."

"You can carry the loose end if you don't drag it."

I took it. The cord was rough and swollen with water. A broken fiber caught against the skin at the base of my thumb.

We went to the vessel together. Its owner stood with one foot on the old post's platform, watching the space between the hull and the bank. He had a broad scar across one knuckle. When he saw me holding the line, he looked first at Sulad.

"Who is that?"

"He is carrying the end," Sulad said. "I have not hired him."

The owner pointed to the stern.

"Lay it there. Keep the toggle out of the water."

I did as he asked. The toggle struck the board and made a hollow sound. The owner lifted it, checked the worn hole, and put it into a basket with two other pieces of wood.

"That has to be changed," he told Sulad.

"I saw the wear when we brought it in."

"Did you tell the man who tied it?"

"I told him to use the other."

"Good. We will not take a loaded boat out on that one."

He moved away to speak with the steersman. I stood with my hands empty.

Dala's frame was visible downriver, held upright by Pawi. I could see the pale line of the broken corner. The boat's line had looked sound enough to bind it, thicker than anything she had saved.

"Would the owner spare a length?" I asked Sulad.

"For what?"

"Dala needs cord."

"He needs this line."

"The end is worn."

"The toggle is worn. The line is still his."

"Could I ask him to cut the bad part off?"

"You can ask. He can say no."

I looked at the basket with the wooden toggles. The owner had already begun discussing a repair with the steersman. If I asked while they were busy, perhaps he would give me the loose end rather than have to explain why he wanted it.

Sulad saw where I was looking.

"Don't tell Dala I have cord for her," he said.

"I haven't."

"Then don't start."

I had been about to send Pawi to fetch her. I could feel the words still waiting in my mouth.

"The line is here," I said.

"It is here because I brought it back. I was paid to bring it back."

"Would you lend your share?"

"My share is the food and the cloth the owner agreed to give me. It is not the boat's gear."

I looked at his shoulder where the wet line had darkened his shirt. He had done the work. I had seen the line and counted it as something he could give away.

The owner returned and found us beside the stern.

"Is there a problem?"

"No," Sulad said.

I could have let him answer for both of us. The owner waited.

"Someone at our landing has a broken fishing frame," I said. "I wanted to ask whether you have any cord you would sell."

"Not that line."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

He pointed at the splice.

"That holds a boat when the water pulls. I cannot shorten it because another man sees a loose end. If you want small cord, ask the basket maker beyond the hall. He bought a coil from us last season. I don't know what he has left."

"Would he take fish?"

"Ask him."

The owner went back to his work. Sulad bent to check the knot securing the line in its basket. He did not need me to hold anything.

"You could have told me where to buy cord," I said.

"I could. You had not asked where."

"Do you think the basket maker has anything that will serve?"

"You can take Dala's old cord and find out."

I looked back at the boat. I had been asking other people to tell me what a man I had not seen in years might do. Sulad was standing beside me, and I had nearly done it again.

"You were with me at that first landing," I said. "I remembered him differently."

"You keep telling me he saw the silver. You leave out what happened to the cloth."

"Perhaps."

"Do not put my name beside his as a witness to a promise. I heard no promise."

He said it plainly. I had been considering whether to ask the keeper to mark Mulaw's name as a contact from my first voyage. If Sulad had agreed that the young man owed me a favor, I might have asked her to write more than she could prove.

"I won't," I said.

Sulad took a small packet from beneath the thwart. It was wrapped in dry leaves and tied with a strip of palm fiber.

"Mine," he said when he saw me look at it. "Food from the owner. I have already been paid for the first part of the journey."

"Will you eat with me?"

"After I return the other basket."

He stepped aboard to fetch it. I waited on the path. I could have told him to hurry; instead I watched him choose where to place his feet as the boat shifted beneath him.

When he came back, I showed him the fish I had earned from Sera.

"That is mine," I said.

"Then you have something to eat."

"I thought I might give it to Dala."

"Did she ask?"

"No."

"You have seen the broken corner."

"She needs cord."

"And a frame that will hold it."

He went up the path with his packet. I followed, carrying nothing for him this time.

The basket maker's place was beyond the hall, where the ground rose and the path narrowed between two walls. Strips of bamboo lay in a shallow trough, held beneath water by stones. A woman sat splitting one of them with a short blade. She stopped when she saw me looking at a coil hanging from the roof.

"That is not for nets," she said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were looking at it."

I told her about the broken frame and the cord Dala had saved. She asked how thick the old binding had been, whether it would stay wet, and how much of it had been cut away. I could answer only the last question, and even then I showed the length with my hands rather than naming it.

"Bring me a piece of the old cord," she said. "I will tell you what I can make or trade. Do not buy the wrong thing and tell the fisherwoman it is a gift."

"Would you take a fish?"

"For what? We haven't agreed on anything."

I showed her the leaf bundle. She shook her head.

"Eat it before it spoils. Come back when you know what you want."

On the way down I found Sulad sitting under the eaves with his food. He had left space beside him, but he had not called me. I sat and opened my leaf. The fish was smaller than I remembered when Sera gave it to me. I divided it carefully, putting the bones on the leaf where I could see them.

Sulad ate without asking for any. When he finished, he stood and wiped his fingers.

"I go with the owner again after the tide," he said.

"Will you come back?"

"If the work brings me back."

I recognized the words, though Mulaw had said them years earlier about his own boat. Sulad had been filling our jar when Mulaw spoke. I could not say whether he had heard. The words belonged to his own work now.

"Then I will ask when you return," I said.

"Ask me what?"

"Whether you have time to eat."

He nodded and went down to the boat.

Dala was still at the rack when I came back. I did not tell her that a cord had been offered, or that a buyer had been found, or that a man I once knew might come to Maynila.

"The woman above the hall asked to see a piece of the old binding," I said. "She will tell us whether she has anything that will serve."

Dala held out the frayed end she had cut away.

"Take this. Bring it back if she cannot use it."

"Will you come to choose?"

"When this side holds. Bring the sample back first."

I took the cord. It was damp and rough, with the knot still holding a splinter from the broken frame. I carried it between two fingers so the fibers would not catch on my cloth.

When I reached the basket maker's trough, she was still splitting bamboo. She saw the wet cord in my hand, set down her blade, and held out her palm.

* * *

The basket maker had no cord that Dala could use that morning.

She laid the frayed sample on her palm, turned it over with the point of her blade, and pulled a loose fiber free. Then she took down the coil I had been watching. Its strands were stiff and smooth. She bent a short length between her fingers and showed me where it held the bend.

"This will cut into that old binding when it swells," she said. "You need something that can take water with the rest of it."

"Can you make it?"

"Yes. Not before I finish the baskets I have promised."

"How long?"

"Ask me after the next market day."

I wanted to offer her my fish, but I had eaten it with Sulad. I had nothing left in my cloth except the damp sample.

"Will you keep this?"

"She told you to bring it back if I could not use it, didn't she?"

I nodded. The woman put it in my hand. Her knife was already moving through the bamboo before I reached the path.

By the next low water, Dala had found a way to brace the damaged corner without tightening it. She showed me where she had set a small piece of wood across the split. It kept the frame from falling when she lifted it, but she would not put the net under strain.

"She can make binding," I said. "After her other work."

"Then we wait."

"Could I help with the frame meanwhile?"

"You can hold it while I take the old pieces apart. That will not make it ready for the water."

I held the frame. She loosened the temporary brace and took it away. When she set the wood on the ground, the corner sagged again. I had wanted the visit to the basket maker to count as progress. Dala had a frame that still could not catch fish.

A call came from the upper path. The keeper was waving to someone beyond the hall, not to me. I kept my hands where Dala had placed them until she told me to let go.

"Go see what has come," she said. "I will send when I need you."

At the landing a broad boat waited out from the bank. Seven dark jars stood in two rows beneath a covering. The vessel rode low at the stern, where a woman was arguing with a man in a narrow canoe. She wore a strip of red cloth at her wrist and held one jar lid in her hand.

The man in the canoe was the small-boat owner who had helped test Raya's step. His bow was pointed toward the old upper post, but he had not tied there.

"She wants to move her jars in my boat," he told the keeper. "I can take two at a time. I won't carry all seven for the price of one crossing."

The woman held up the lid.

"I am Lana. I have buyers here. They told me there was a sound step."

"There is one," I said.

She looked at me, then at the edge of the bank.

"For a boat my size?"

"No. The upper post is for the smaller vessel. Raya has to see it after high water."

"Then why did they send me here?"

"Who sent you?"

"A man who bought oil from me before. He said I could bring it to Maynila now."

"Did he say where you could put it?"

"He said there was a step."

The keeper went to find Raya. Lana turned the lid in her hands. Its rim had been rubbed smooth by use, but the jar it belonged to was covered and I could not tell whether the seal beneath it was sound.

"Can you wait for the maker?" I asked.

"I can wait until the tide turns. Then I have to decide where to sell."

Her crewman had put a hand against the larger boat's side to steady it. He looked toward the far bank, where a row of roofs stood above a broad strip of dry ground. I recognized the landing to which Sera had taken her fish.

Raya arrived with her nephew and a short stick. She tested the upper step from the bank, then knelt and reached beneath it. The small-boat owner watched her fingers disappear into the mud.

"Has it moved?" he asked.

"Not enough to close it for a single load," she said. "But no one is to stand a jar on the outer edge. The line stays on the old post. I will not answer for the larger boat."

Lana pointed at her seven jars.

"Two at a time?"

"Only if this man agrees to carry them. I am judging the step, not his boat."

The small-boat owner looked at Lana.

"Two jars for each trip, paid whether you sell them or not. If the buyer makes me wait, he pays for my waiting or you do."

"How much?"

He named a portion of oil. Lana shook her head.

"That is enough to feed a household for a week."

"Then find another boat."

She lowered the lid and studied the jars. Her crewman spoke quietly to her. I could not hear his words. She answered without looking at him.

"One trip first," she told the small-boat owner. "Two jars. If the buyer takes them, we can speak of another."

"And if he doesn't?"

"I pay for that trip."

He nodded. The keeper asked them to repeat the terms while she marked them on a fresh strip. She left a space beneath the marks for the second trip, but did not fill it.

I wanted to tell Lana that Maynila could take all seven if she gave us time. The sight of the empty space on the keeper's strip stopped me. She had agreed to one crossing, and I had no place to put even the first two jars.

The buyer came while the crew were lifting the first jar. He was a narrow man with a basket on his back and a young assistant carrying a folded mat. He pointed to a patch of earth above the step.

"Set them there. I want to see the seals."

Dala's rack stood beside that patch. Her sister had moved it back after the tide test, and the frame lay under its shade.

"That is where I work," Dala called.

The buyer looked at the patch again.

"I only need the ground until I have opened them."

"Then ask me how long I need it."

He came to her. I followed, hoping they might find a way to use the space for a few moments. Dala was kneeling with her broken frame across her legs. She showed him the brace she had taken out.

"Can you put your mat here without touching this?"

He tried to lay the folded mat between the path and the rack. It overlapped the place where Pawi had set the sound cord.

"Move that," he said.

"It belongs here."

"Then where do you expect people to trade?"

"I expect them to ask where I fish."

The buyer stood. He did not insult her. He looked toward the hall instead.

"Can I use the floor inside?"

Amaya had heard him. She came to the doorway and looked at the two jars now resting in the small boat.

"What is in them?"

"Oil."

"Are they sealed?"

"I need to open them to know."

"Then I will not take them among the dry cloth and grain."

"I can pay for the floor."

"That does not make the grain dry again if something spills."

He glanced at me. I had begun to raise my hand, as though I could settle the matter by asking Amaya to move her goods. She saw it and turned back into the hall.

Lana called from the larger boat. The narrow vessel had reached the upper post and the small-boat owner was holding it there. The first jar was on the new step. His crewman stood with the second against his chest, waiting for the path to clear.

"Where do I put this?" he asked.

The buyer went down to him. He took the folded mat from his assistant and spread it across the middle of the narrow boat, away from the jar being carried ashore.

"Can I inspect them aboard?"

The small-boat owner watched the mat settle.

"One at a time. Keep your feet where I tell you. If you break a jar, you answer to her, not to me."

The buyer agreed. His assistant stayed on the path. The crewman carried the first jar back down from the step and set it on the mat. He had lifted it once without selling it, and now had to lift it again. I helped him steady the base as it came over the gunwale.

Lana leaned from her boat.

"Do not open both until you have agreed on the first."

The buyer lifted the lid. He loosened the binding beneath it, touched a little oil to his finger, and smelled it. He asked Lana where she had filled the jar. She told him, and he asked whether the other was from the same pressing. She said it was. He did not accept that as a reason to buy without looking.

They agreed on the first jar. The assistant carried it up the repaired step while Raya watched his footing. He set it on the path only long enough for the buyer to lift it onto his shoulder.

The second jar had a dark patch beneath the binding. The buyer turned it and found a place where oil had worked into the outer cord.

"That seal has been wet," he said.

"The jar is sound," Lana answered.

"Then why is the binding dark?"

"Because the boat is wet."

"Not under the lid."

Lana asked him to taste the oil. He refused. He had paid for the first jar and would not take the second at the same price. She offered a smaller amount. He shook his head again.

The small-boat owner held the narrow vessel against the post while they spoke. The current was beginning to pull harder around the bow.

"You can bargain on your own boat," he told Lana. "I have held this one long enough."

She looked at the buyer.

"Will you take it for less?"

"Not today."

She told the crewman to bring the jar back. He lifted it from the mat and nearly struck the gunwale with its base. The small-boat owner caught his elbow, then let go as soon as the weight was steady.

"One trip," he said to Lana. "That was our bargain."

She paid him the agreed portion from a small vessel she had kept beneath the covering. She measured it herself and made him inspect it before he took it. He did not thank me for helping with the jar. He had never hired me.

The buyer and his assistant went uphill with their purchase. I had watched one jar enter Maynila and one return to the broad boat. No place had been taken from Dala. No oil had been put in Amaya's hall. The keeper marked one sale witnessed at the upper step and one jar returned unsold. She left the second-trip space empty.

"Will you bring the others?" I asked Lana.

"Not here."

"We could find another buyer."

"Where would he look at them?"

I looked at the path. The mat was already being folded. Dala had returned to her frame. Raya was feeling the step where the crewman had turned with the jar. The small-boat owner was gathering his line.

Lana called to her steersman.

"Take us across."

A larger vessel was moving from the far bank toward her. Its owner was the man who had asked Raya to build a lower berth for his salted-fish cargo. He had kept his own goods across the river after she refused. He brought his boat close enough to speak without tying to Lana's.

"I can put your jars under a roof there," he said, pointing across. "I know two buyers who will look at them. I take a share if they sell."

"How much?"

He named it. She answered with less. They argued while their vessels drifted slowly downstream, the crewmen keeping the hulls apart with poles. Neither asked me to decide.

I could have called out that she should wait for our landing to improve. The buyer who had come for her oil was already gone. The step had held the one jar that was sold, but it could not give her a floor or another buyer before the tide changed.

Lana reached an agreement with the larger owner that I could not hear. She gave her steersman a signal, and the two boats turned toward the far bank. Six jars remained aboard her vessel. I could count their dark lids beneath the covering.

The keeper rolled the strip she had made for the crossing.

"Did we receive a share?" I asked.

"From whom?"

"I thought the buyer might give something for using the step."

"He paid Lana. Lana paid the small boat. Neither offered us a share."

"Could we ask the next one?"

"We can ask what we are giving in return."

I watched the boats reach the other landing. Men there were already clearing a place beneath a roof. One jar was lifted onto dry ground, and a second followed. They did not wait for a man from Maynila to tell them where to stand.

Raya called me down to the step. A little mud had been pressed from beneath the outer edge where the crewman turned with the unsold jar. She showed me the mark it left on the timber.

"It held the load we agreed," she said. "Do not call that proof it can take another."

I helped her put a stone beside the edge so she could see whether the next water moved it. I could still smell the oil on my fingers from the jar I had steadied. I had no oil of my own to show for the morning, and no payment for Dala, Raya or Amaya.

Across the river Lana's crew had begun carrying the remaining jars beneath the roof. She had found a place for them. It had not been ours.

* * *

The jar had left a dark circle on the step.

It was not oil. Raya scraped it with her nail and held the dirt beneath my nose. It smelled of wet earth. Where the crewman had turned with Lana's unsold jar, his heel had driven the mud out from under the outer timber. The stone Raya had set beside the edge was leaning toward the water.

"Does that mean we close it?" I asked.

"It means we do not put another man on that corner until I know what has moved."

She stood with her feet apart on the higher bank and watched the current pass the old post. A boat was waiting upstream. Its owner had brought two covered bundles of palm leaves, each small enough to carry without a second man. He could see us examining the step.

"Can I come in?" he called.

"At the old path," Raya answered. "Keep your load away from the outer edge."

The owner considered the muddy slope and brought his boat toward the place she had named. He had to wait while two women carried their jars past him. When the path cleared, he lifted the first bundle himself.

I had wanted to show him the new step. He had watched Raya refuse it and had come ashore anyway.

"Will you need wood?" I asked her.

"I may need to lift the edge and pack it again. That does not mean I need you to find a tree."

"I can help."

"After the tide. You will be in the way if you stand here asking me what I need."

She pressed the leaning stone upright with her heel and left it there as a mark. I walked away before she had to tell me twice.

Above the hall the basket maker was taking her finished baskets from beneath the roof. They were nested in two stacks, the smaller ones inside the larger. Her nephew had set them on a low platform so their bottoms would not rest in the damp. He was trying to pull one free by its rim. It caught on the next and bent.

"Lift the whole stack," she told him.

He let go at once. I came forward and took the weight from the other side. The baskets were lighter than Sera's fish, but their open rims caught against my arms.

"Where do you want them?"

"Under the eaves, where the sun will reach them after it clears the roof. Not on the ground."

I put them down where she pointed. She checked the bent rim, wet it with a little water from a bowl, and pressed it back against the weave. The boy watched with his hands behind him.

"Did Dala send you?" she asked.

"No. I came about the binding."

"You brought her piece yesterday. I gave it back."

"She has it."

"Then you know I cannot make hers before I finish these."

I set Dala's frayed sample beside the water bowl.

"I could carry the baskets."

"To where?"

"Wherever you sell them."

She took the largest basket from the stack and held it against her hip.

"Do you know who is buying them?"

"No."

"Then you are offering to carry a thing you have not asked about, to a place you do not know."

"I can ask."

"You can. You can also help me turn the baskets without promising a journey."

She showed me how to lift the nested stack without dragging one rim across another. The boy stood opposite me and copied the way she held it. When we set the baskets beneath the strip of light, she looked at the dampness along their bases.

"These will need another morning," she said.

"Then perhaps you have time for the binding."

"I have the other baskets to finish. My hands do not grow another pair because your friend has a broken frame."

"She is not asking you to leave your work."

"You are."

I looked at the two unfinished baskets beside her trough. One had a gap where a strip had split. She had cut the bad end away but had not yet replaced it. The boy had been carrying the dry strips to her one at a time.

"Could I prepare those?" I asked.

She gave me a strip and pointed to the blade lying beside the trough.

"Split it where it is already opening. Stop when the grain turns. If you force it, I will have less to weave with."

I worked slowly. The first length came apart cleanly. The second wandered sideways and left one half too thin. I held it up before cutting farther.

"That one will serve for a narrow place," she said. "Put it apart. Do not hide it among the sound lengths."

I wanted her to see that I could learn quickly. She wanted the usable strips kept from the bad ones. We worked without speaking for a while. The boy fetched water, then sat on the platform and began counting the baskets by touching their rims.

A woman carrying a folded mat stopped at the entrance. She had come from the far bank. I recognized her as the buyer who had inspected Sera's fish, though she was alone now.

"Sera's jar man has come," she told the basket maker. "He wants two small baskets before he goes."

The blade slipped against my thumb. It did not cut deeply, but a line of blood appeared at once.

"From you?" the basket maker asked.

"He asked who made the ones beneath her roof."

"I have none ready for him. Tell him the small ones are spoken for."

The woman nodded and turned to leave. I stepped after her.

"Is he the man who buys jars from Sera?"

"That is what I said."

"Does he know the boat leader Mulaw?"

She looked at me as though I had asked whether she knew every man on the river.

"I don't know."

"Will he stay until the tide?"

"He said he would look at the baskets and go. Ask him if you want."

"Where is he?"

"Across, where Sera had her fish."

She shifted the mat to her other shoulder.

"If you are coming, come. I have my own things to carry."

I took a step toward the path. The woman did not wait. By the time I reached the turn, she was already below the hall, walking quickly between two people with jars.

From the workshop I could hear the boy asking whether I had left the strips in the right place. The basket maker answered him, then called my name.

I went back.

"You have blood on my work," she said.

I had been holding the split length in my injured hand. A red mark ran along one edge. She took it from me and set it aside.

"Wash that. I cannot put it into a basket someone has paid for."

I poured water over my thumb. The cut stung. It was small enough that I could close it by pressing my other fingers against it.

"I could go across and return before dark," I said.

"You can go wherever you like."

"Would you make Dala's binding if I stayed?"

She put the blade down.

"I have told you when I can make it. You cannot buy tomorrow with work you have not done today."

"I could do the work tomorrow."

"You have not asked what I would want done."

"What would you want?"

She looked at the baskets, the boy, and the trough where the strips were soaking.

"I need the finished baskets carried to the house above the bend when they are dry. Their owner will pay me when she sees them. If you carry them and bring back what she pays, I will put aside the fiber for one length of binding. That is what I can offer. I will not promise it will mend Dala's frame. She must look at it herself."

"How much binding?"

She took Dala's sample from where I had laid it beside the bowl. I had forgotten to take it back after showing it to her. She measured it against her forearm and indicated a length with her fingers.

"Enough to test one corner. Not enough for the whole net."

"Could I carry the baskets today?"

"They are wet. Tomorrow if the air stays dry. The owner may not be home. You will not be paid by me for waiting outside her door."

"And the fiber?"

"After the baskets are delivered and the payment comes back. I cannot give you my material against a promise."

I had been asking her to do exactly that since I arrived. I looked toward the far-bank roofs. The jar buyer might still be there. Sera had said she would ask him about Mulaw if he came. I could cross and try to make him answer more than she had asked. He might know nothing. He might be gone before I found a boat.

"Can the boy take me to the house tomorrow?" I asked.

"He can show you the path. He will not carry your load."

The boy looked pleased. Then he looked at the baskets and less pleased.

"Does Dala know you are making this bargain?" the woman asked.

"No."

"Then do not tell her I owe her binding. I will owe you material when you have done the carrying. What she accepts from you is her choice."

I wiped my thumb on the inside of my cloth, away from the bloodied strip.

"I'll come when the baskets are dry."

She handed me Dala's old sample. I took it without catching the fibers against my cut.

When I reached the lower path, Dala was lifting her frame from the shade. Pawi stood ready to take the other end. I asked whether I might hold it instead.

"You are late," she said.

"I went to the basket maker."

"I did not send you."

"I know. She can make a short binding after I carry her baskets and bring back the payment. Enough for one corner, if you want to try it."

"Who pays for the rest?"

"I have not found that yet."

"And the catch I lost while this stood broken?"

"I have not paid that either."

She shifted the frame so that its damaged side faced me.

"If she makes a piece, bring it here before you put it on. I will decide whether it is fit. Do not say the frame is mended because you have found a way to earn cord."

I nodded. Pawi looked from her to me, waiting to see who would lift.

"Can I help you move it now?"

"Take that end. Keep it away from the post."

We carried the frame between us. The broken corner pulled against my fingers, and I had to change my grip before we reached the drying place. Dala saw the blood on my thumb.

"What happened?"

"I split a strip badly."

"Then don't use that hand to hold the wet corner."

She took the weight from me and gave Pawi the end I had carried. He had been standing ready all along.

Across the river a small vessel pulled away from Sera's roof. I could not tell who was aboard. I watched it pass behind a larger boat and disappear beyond the bend.

I had not spoken to the jar buyer. I had not learned where Mulaw was. Sera might have asked her question; I had no word from her. The man I remembered had never promised to come when I called.

Dala set the frame down where the sun would reach the sound wood. She ran her thumb over the split and removed a small splinter.

"Come tomorrow if you have the binding," she said. "If you do not, come when I send."

The basket maker's baskets were still drying above the hall. I had promised to carry them when they were ready, and she had promised only to set aside material after I brought back their owner's payment. It was less than I had hoped to gain by asking after a name. It was something I could return and do.

* * *

The baskets were dry enough to lift, but not all of them were sound.

The maker found the bad rim before I did. She ran two fingers beneath the third basket and turned it toward the light. One strip had risen from the weave where I had helped stack it the day before.

"That was not there when I finished it," she said.

"Did I bend it?"

"You carried the stack. The boy pulled one by its rim. I do not know which of you did this."

Her nephew stood beside the trough with his hands at his sides. I could see him trying to remember how hard he had pulled.

"I can take it out," I said.

"You can carry it with the others. The woman who ordered them may accept it, or she may send it back. Do not press it down and tell her it was always sound."

She tied the four baskets in two pairs with a cord she kept separate from Dala's sample. The boy led us along the path above the bend. He walked fast when he was not carrying anything, then waited for me whenever the baskets struck my knees.

The house had a low gate made of split wood. Behind it stood a roof with no wall on the river side. A woman was arranging small packets on a mat beneath the roof. Her left arm was wrapped in cloth from the wrist to the elbow. She had a shallow wooden tray beside her and a pot of water within reach.

"Your baskets," the boy announced.

She did not look up until I set them down.

"I asked for four," she said.

"There are four," I answered.

"That one is bent."

"The maker saw it. She asked me to show you."

The woman took the basket and pressed its rim against the floor. It rocked on one side. She turned it and found the raised strip.

"I can take three. The fourth goes back."

"Would you keep it until she can mend it?"

"I have no place for a basket I cannot use. My roof is already full."

She pointed to the packets on the mat. They were wrapped in leaves and tied in pairs. A faint smell of wax rose from the one nearest the sun.

"What do you put in the baskets?" I asked.

"Goods that should not sit in water."

"Where are they going?"

"With a boatman who has agreed to carry them. I have not asked you to find him."

I held my tongue. She counted the three sound baskets, set them beside the packets, and took a folded piece of cloth from the tray. She showed it to the boy, then to me.

"Tell the maker I accepted three. This is what I give for three. She may bring the fourth when it is mended, and we will speak about it then."

The boy repeated the number. The woman made him say it again while he was looking at the rejected basket.

"Three accepted. One returned."

"Good. Now take that one away before I put something in it by mistake."

I put the bent basket inside the empty carrying cord. The woman watched me lift it.

"Are you the man who speaks for the upper landing?" she asked.

"Sometimes."

"Then perhaps you can tell me why the boatman says the step is closed."

"Raya is checking the outer edge. The old path is open for small loads."

"My goods are small. He wants to take them after the next low water. I have paid him for one journey, if he can bring his boat alongside without breaking anything."

"What boat?"

"The narrow one that carried the oil jars yesterday. He came to ask me about these packets before he took Lana's jars across."

I recognized the small-boat owner. He had refused to carry Lana's seven jars for the price of one crossing. He would refuse this woman too if the weight or waiting changed.

"Have you spoken to Raya?"

"No. He told me you would know where to find her."

"Raya decides what the step can hold. Dala needs the path beside it. The hall cannot keep your packets while you wait."

"I have my own roof," she said. "I am asking for the length of a carrying path, not a room in your house."

I looked at the three baskets she had accepted. She was already fitting the packets inside them, pressing the leaf-wrapped pieces into the corners so they would not slide.

"How many baskets will go?"

"Two. The third is for the things I keep here."

"Will you take them down yourself?"

"My nephew can carry one. I can walk with the other. The boatman will take them aboard."

She flexed the fingers of her wrapped arm and winced. The movement made me look away.

"Would you like me to carry the other?" I asked.

"Not unless you want payment for carrying. I have paid the boatman. I have not hired you."

"I can help for nothing."

"Then I would have to wonder what you expected afterward."

I had no answer that would sound clean to her. She turned back to her packets.

"Find Raya. If the step is not safe, the goods can wait here. They will not spoil before the next tide."

I carried the bent basket back to the maker. The boy ran ahead to tell her the count. He arrived breathless, and she made him sit before he spoke.

"Three accepted," he said. "One came back."

I put the folded cloth in her hand. She unfolded it and examined the edge for a tear. Then she compared it to the work she had sent.

"That is payment for three," she said. "You brought it all."

"I did."

She put it away beneath a dry bowl, where the boy could not accidentally wet it.

"You have done the carrying. I can set aside the fiber for Dala now."

"Can you make it today?"

"I can begin it after I finish the basket she returned. It will be a short length. Dala must see whether it is fit."

She gave me a little bundle of raw fiber to show Dala what she meant. It was rougher than the finished cord I had imagined. She warned me not to pull it apart before she worked it.

"This is mine until I make it," she said. "Bring it back when you have shown her."

I thanked her and went down to the landing. Raya had lifted the outer timber enough to see the earth beneath it. Her nephew was holding a stone in the place where the leaning marker had stood. She pressed fresh earth under the edge and tamped it with the flat of a small piece of wood.

"Will it hold?" I asked.

"After the next water I will know whether it moved. For now the inner part can take a man with a light load. Not two men passing each other."

"A woman above the bend has two baskets to send. The small-boat owner has agreed to carry them if the step is sound."

"Then he should come and ask me."

"He will."

"Bring the woman too. She knows what is in them."

Dala was near her rack, turning the broken frame so the sun reached its wet side. I showed her the bundle of fiber. She pulled a single strand loose and rubbed it between her fingers.

"It may serve," she said. "But the old binding is soft. I will not put this on until the maker has worked it and I have tried it wet."

"She will make a short length when she finishes a returned basket."

"Then we are still waiting."

"Yes."

She looked toward the upper path.

"Is there another boat coming?"

"A woman wants to send two baskets. The step may take them after Raya looks again."

"Where will they stand?"

"She will bring them from her own house. She needs only the path."

"Not through my rack."

"I will tell her."

"Tell her yourself. Do not tell the keeper I agreed before she comes."

I returned the raw fiber to the maker, then went up to the woman's house. By the time I arrived, the small-boat owner was there. He had one foot on the low gate and was looking at the two filled baskets.

"I said I would carry them if the landing held," he told me. "She says you will find out."

"Raya wants you both to come."

"Then we will."

The woman covered the baskets with a mat. Her nephew carried one and she carried the other, its weight against her unwrapped arm. I offered to take it once. She shook her head. I walked behind them so that no one could say I had brought the goods on the hall's behalf.

At the landing Raya had finished packing the edge. She made the small-boat owner put his foot on the inner timber while his boat remained tied to the old post. He shifted his weight once, then stepped back.

"One man at a time," she said. "Keep the baskets close to the bank. If the edge moves after the next high water, I close it again."

The woman looked at Dala's rack. Dala was standing beside it.

"I can carry these without touching your frame," she said. "Will that path be open when the water falls?"

"After I take my net down," Dala answered. "You may pass when I call. If your people come early, they wait above."

The small-boat owner nodded.

"One journey with two baskets," he said. "I will take them to the buyer she named. She pays me whether he takes them or sends them back. I am not keeping them in my boat beyond the journey."

The woman agreed. The keeper repeated the words while she held a clean strip of bamboo against her knee. She cut one long mark for the journey and two short marks for the baskets. Then she held the strip where the owner and the woman could see it.

"One boat, two baskets, after Dala clears the path and Raya allows the step," she said. "Nothing here promises another journey."

The woman touched the long cut. The owner touched each short one. I watched them both, relieved that they seemed to understand the same thing.

When Dala called, the woman's nephew carried the first basket down. The small-boat owner steadied it from the boat while the nephew stepped back onto the old path. The woman brought the second herself. She did not ask me to carry it. I held the boat's loose line only when the owner told me to, and I returned it as soon as he had secured the load.

The boat moved out from the old post. It took the bend slowly, keeping clear of the sounding stake. I watched the two covered baskets disappear behind the low bank. Their buyer was still upriver. No sale had been made in front of us, and the woman had not promised a share to the landing.

The keeper put the marked strip with the day's other records. Dala lifted her frame back into the sun. Raya went to inspect the timber again. Amaya remained in the hall, where the cloth I owed her had not appeared. The maker still had to finish the binding, and her own buyer had accepted only three of four baskets. None of those things had changed because a boat had left with two loads.

The owner's young crewman came up the path after the boat was gone. He had been sent back to fetch a small paddle left beside the hall. The keeper showed him where it had been put out of the way.

"Is the second journey still ours?" he asked.

The keeper stopped rolling her strip.

"What second journey?"

"The owner said there were two cuts for two turns. He told me to come back for the other load after the water rises."

"There are two cuts for two baskets," she said. "One long cut for one journey."

The crewman pointed at the marks.

"He told me the two small cuts meant he could come twice. He has gone to deliver the first. He will want to come again before night."

"Did you hear what he agreed here?"

"I was keeping the bow clear. I heard him say two."

"He said two baskets," the woman answered. She had come back for the mat that covered them. "I paid him for one journey."

"Then why did he tell me two?"

No one answered. I remembered the owner touching each short cut, one after the other. I had been pleased that he seemed to understand. I had not asked him to repeat what each cut meant in his own words.

The keeper laid the strip flat.

"Do not alter it," she told me when I reached toward it.

"I only wanted to show him the long mark."

"He has seen it."

The crewman picked up the paddle. He did not look like a man trying to steal a berth. He looked like someone who had been sent to do a job and would have to explain why he returned without it.

"Will you stop us if we come back?" he asked.

"Raya will decide whether the step is open after the next water," the keeper said. "No one here agreed to a second journey. Bring your owner to speak for what he believes he heard."

"He is on the river."

"Then the question waits for him."

The crewman went down the path with the paddle. I watched him climb into a different small boat that would take him to the bend. He had been told one thing by the owner and another by the people on the bank. Both would expect him to remember their words.

"Should I have spoken sooner?" I asked the keeper.

"You heard the same words I did."

"I saw him touch the marks."

"So did I. We thought we knew what he meant."

She did not put the strip away. She left it on the low table beside the open doorway, with the long cut and the two short ones facing the path.

The woman whose goods had gone upriver returned to her house. Dala took her frame home without a new binding. Raya's stone remained beside the step for the next tide to test. I could not tell whether the small-boat owner would return angry, or whether his crewman had mistaken him, or whether the owner had heard only the part of the bargain he wanted.

I had spent days trying to find a man whose name I remembered from a distant boat. I had found no promise there. Here, beside the landing I could see from the hall, I had watched two people agree to one journey and leave with different accounts of the marks.

The bamboo lay where anyone could look at it. The owner who needed to answer for his reading was already beyond the bend.
