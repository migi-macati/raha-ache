## Attribution and evidence boundary

**Source gate advanced 2 November 2026.** The Spanish text of the **1882 printed edition**, *Colección de documentos inéditos para la historia de España* (CODOIN), vol. **LXXVIII**, printed **pp. 58–61**, was read through the indexed text of the [Ghent-hosted scanned volume](https://www.heuristiek.ugent.be/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/codoin.78.pdf). The relevant passage is the end of Chapter XIII, immediately before Chapter XIV. The PDF's extracted text displays the printed page markers 59, 60 and 61 and the continuous narrative across them. This is **direct collation of the printed Spanish wording**, superseding the earlier secondary-paraphrase-only status. The **page images were not visually inspected** (large-file retrieval limit), OCR-sensitive letters have not been checked against the scan, and Aganduru's underlying seventeenth-century manuscript and informants have not been inspected. Do not claim manuscript-level verification.

Aganduru wrote roughly a century after the events and is **not an eyewitness to 1521**. The account's confidently narrated childhood, royal descent, military numbers, successful return and marriage remain **retrospective claims**, even when their printed wording is now verified. His language labels local vessels as pirates and portrays the eventual Spanish conquest as rightful; this authorial viewpoint is evidence of his framing, not proof of the people or events described.

## Ache's childhood and departure (printed pp. 58–59)

Aganduru says the prince's father died when he was newly born, his mother held government, and her nephew, the king of Tondo and the prince's cousin, encroached on Maynila's lands. The prince complained to his mother after a quarrel with that cousin, sought support from his grandfather Siripada in Brunei, and departed with relatives and local lords. His mother wanted him to remain and tried to dissuade him, then granted permission and gave him gold. These are **Aganduru's assertions**, not corroborated facts about the historical Ache's private childhood or his mother's words.

## Brunei force and encounter (printed pp. 59–60)

Aganduru says Siripada received the prince at court and fitted out **nearly two hundred** junks and galleys and **up to six thousand** Bruneian soldiers. He claims the prince sailed before Juan Sebastián del Cano arrived in Brunei and raided en route to recovering Maynila. The European vessels, having left Brunei, fought this force and captured its flagship. **Do not transfer the asserted fleet size, motive or route into Pigafetta's eyewitness testimony.**

Aganduru attributes the capture and release to **del Cano**, who, on learning of Siripada's kinship, allegedly **declined the prince's offered gold**, restored the captured ship and goods, and requested a pilot for the Moluccas. He says the prince gave freedom to an enslaved **Makassarese pilot** who had passed between owners and then guided the Europeans. He also says the prince promised to treat future Spaniards well in remembrance of the release. This is Aganduru's retrospective account; the named pilot's identity, status and actual transfer are not independently established.

**Conflict with contemporary witness:** Pigafetta reports that the Luzon prince was Brunei's captain-general returning from a punitive attack on **Laoe**, and that **Giovan Carvalho** released him for **gold**. Aganduru instead supplies a Maynila–Tondo restoration mission and a **del Cano/no-gold** account. These are substantive differences in motive, command and payment. The original printed wording confirms the contradiction; it does not settle which narrative is historically correct. Albo's navigational account supports a junk capture and departure but does not validate Aganduru's detailed biography.

## Later return, marriage and political framing (printed pp. 60–61)

Aganduru explicitly identifies the prince as the later **Ladya Matanda** who received Martín de Goiti. He claims the prince returned to Maynila, recovered lands, forced back the Tondo ruler, raided some Tondo settlements, and negotiated peace. He then says Siripada sent a **granddaughter** previously promised in Brunei, **after** learning that the prince held his realm securely. Aganduru describes the bride as the prince's **first cousin** and says the marriage had no offspring. The passage does **not** provide a precise wedding date, an eyewitness marriage record or proof that the described military restoration occurred.

The author immediately connects the reported lack of offspring to a providential justification of Spanish sovereignty. That teleological claim is the chronicler's colonial interpretation, **not an established historical explanation**. The printed text's marriage sequence is clearer than earlier paraphrases, but the marriage's historicity, exact date and kinship remain uncorroborated outside this late account.
