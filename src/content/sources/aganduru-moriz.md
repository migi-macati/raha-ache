## Ache's departure

Aganduru says Ache was left without his father while newly born, his mother governed Maynila, and the ruler of Tondo—Ache's cousin—gradually entered Maynila's lands.

Ache decided to go to Brunei to ask his grandfather for support. His mother tried to dissuade him, then gave him permission and gold.

## Brunei

Siripada received Ache at court.

Aganduru gives the force placed with Ache as nearly two hundred vessels and up to six thousand Bruneian soldiers.

## 1521

Aganduru says Ache's force encountered the surviving European expedition; Ache was captured and identified as Siripada's grandson.

Unlike Pigafetta, Aganduru says del Cano released Ache without accepting the gold he offered, returned his vessel and property, and asked him to remember the courtesy if Spaniards later reached his lands.

Ache gave the expedition a Makassarese pilot in his service, freed him, and sent him to guide them toward the Moluccas.

## Return to Maynila

Aganduru says Ache returned successfully, recovered his lands, forced Tondo back, and negotiated a peace after attacks on Tondo settlements.

Siripada later sent a granddaughter to Ache. Aganduru says the marriage had already been arranged and identifies her as Ache's first cousin.

## Source status

Aganduru wrote later than the 1521 expedition. His narrative contains information absent from the contemporary expedition accounts and should be compared with Pigafetta, Albo, Mafra, and other evidence.

## Source-critical qualification

This account was written in the **seventeenth century**, approximately a century after the 1521 episode. Aganduru Moriz did not witness the encounter. His unusually detailed account of Ache's childhood, maternal regency, the Tondo cousin, Brunei's fleet, return campaign, and marriage is valuable **retrospective testimony**, not independently corroborated biography.

Its release story differs materially from Pigafetta: Aganduru says **del Cano** declined the gold offered by Ache, whereas Pigafetta says **Carvalho** accepted gold. Preserve both as conflicting accounts. The claimed force of nearly 200 vessels and 6,000 soldiers must be attributed to Aganduru, not asserted as a verified muster.

## Edition locator (research verified 12 October 2026)

The accessible **printed edition** is *Historia general de las Islas Occidentales á la Asia adyacentes, llamadas Philipinas*, volume I, Madrid: Miguel Ginesta, **1882**, in *Colección de documentos inéditos para la historia de España* (CODOIN), volume **LXXVIII**. The work is seventeenth-century, but the cited printed edition is nineteenth-century; these dates must not be conflated. The University of Zurich copy was digitized for Google Books. Independent bibliographic confirmation: [Complutense University of Madrid](https://www.ucm.es/religiososenmovimiento/agustinos-recoletos-rodrigo-de-san-miguel) and [Google Books, volume I](https://books.google.com/books/about/Historia_general_de_la_Islas_Occidentale.html?id=jiViHQAACAAJ).

## Modern scholarly use (verified 12 October 2026)

Cesar Adib Majul, “Islam's Advent and Expansion in the Philippines,” in *Proceedings of the International Seminar on Islamic Civilisation in the Malay World* (seminar held in Brunei in 1989; volume published by IRCICA in Istanbul in **1999**), pp. 33–35, retells the Manila–Tondo and Ache/Brunei connections and cites Aganduru Móriz, CODOIN 78, pp. 59–60, together with Pigafetta. [Read the scanned paper](https://isamveri.org/pdfdrg/D302265/1999/1999_MAJULCA.pdf). Majul's discussion is a **modern interpretation and citation trail**, not independent contemporary confirmation of Aganduru's childhood narrative; the primary Spanish passage still requires direct inspection. Majul also proposes broader Bruneian political and religious influence in Manila and Tondo; do not treat every aspect of that reconstruction as a witnessed fact or assume uniform Islamization of local households.
