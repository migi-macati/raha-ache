## 29 July 1521

Pigafetta describes more than one hundred praus approaching in three squadrons, with a comparable number of smaller boats.

The expedition attacked and captured several junks.

In one captured junk was a son of the king of Luzon, described as captain-general of the king of Brunei. Pigafetta says this commander had come from Loue after destroying and sacking it because the city refused obedience to Brunei and instead obeyed the king of Java Major.

Pigafetta says Giovan Carvaio released the captain and junks for gold.

## Comparison

Aganduru Moriz later identifies the prince with the future Ladyang Matanda but gives a different explanation of his mission and release.

## Reliability and identification

Pigafetta was present with the Magellan expedition in 1521. His report of a captured **son of the ruler of Luzon**, serving as Brunei's captain-general, is an early anchor for the Ache reconstruction. **Pigafetta does not name the prince Ache** in that description. The identification is made by comparing sources.

His narrative says the commander had attacked Loue for recognizing the ruler of Java Major rather than Brunei. This is information reported about an earlier expedition, not an event Pigafetta personally observed. Pigafetta credits **Giovan Carvaio (Carvalho)** with accepting gold for the prisoner's release. Contrast Aganduru Moriz, who attributes a refusal of gold to **del Cano**.

The July 1521 encounter and departure sequence has now been checked against James Alexander Robertson's 1906 bilingual edition of Pigafetta's Ambrosian manuscript (English translation alongside the Italian), as reproduced in *The Philippine Islands*, vol. 33, pp. 223–225 and 232–233. This is a checked **edition passage**, not a collation of all surviving French manuscript variants or the later Aganduru account. The precise modern identification of Loue remains open.

## Geographic terminology: Laoe and Java Major

The July 1521 account locates **Laoe** toward the Borneo end facing **Java Major**, but Pigafetta did not visit the reported campaign site. Its modern identification is unresolved. In Pigafetta's subsequent Java description, **Java Major refers to Java**, with places including Tuban, Gresik and Surabaya; it does **not automatically identify the 1521 rival king as the ruler of a surviving unified Majapahit empire**. [University of Hamburg, Pigafetta's Java description](https://www.spaetmittelalter.uni-hamburg.de/java-history/JavaNarratives/Java1521.Antonio.Pigafetta.html).

The account reports three squadrons of more than a hundred praus, smaller accompanying boats, and captured junks in the encounter with the Europeans. These observations should not be converted into a verified size for Ache's earlier Loue expedition. [29 July 1521 entry](https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/29th-of-july-1521/).

## Brunei anchorage and departure chronology: source check, 31 October 2026

**Edition inspected:** Antonio Pigafetta, *Primo viaggio intorno al mondo*, Ambrosian manuscript Italian with James Alexander Robertson's facing English translation (Cleveland, 1906), reproduced in Emma Helen Blair and James Alexander Robertson, eds., *The Philippine Islands*, vol. XXXIII (1906), pp. 223–225 (attack, release, captives), 232–233 (departure and subsequent repair port). [Bilingual primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42884/42884-h/42884-h.htm) (the July 29 passage begins at the displayed page 223; see the immediately following departure and refit passages). A separate modern expedition chronology also labels **29 July 1521** the Brunei departure date: [Philippine Diary Project](https://philippinediaryproject.com/2021/03/05/the-magellan-expedition-and-elcanos-circumnavigation-500-years/).

**What Pigafetta actually says:** On **Monday morning, 29 July**, the Europeans saw more than one hundred praus and hurriedly raised sail, abandoning an anchor. They then attacked and captured junks, seized the Luzon commander and later learned Carvalho had released him for gold. Pigafetta reports a Brunei message explaining the praus and a refusal to return the expedition's men left ashore. He describes remaining prisoners and women taken aboard. After intervening descriptive material on Brunei, the narrative says the ships **left that island**, searched for a repair port, and eventually spent **42 days** near Cimbonbon. The Italian `ne deſſemo ... nela vella` and English `hoisted our sails` establish urgent movement on the 29th; **the passage alone does not timestamp the precise hour the vessels finally cleared every part of the harbor**, nor prove whether they remained visible for a few hours while exchanging messages. Do not misquote it as explicitly saying they were already far offshore at sunset.

**Novel chronology warning RA-HIST-01 (still unresolved):** Book II Chapter 7 depicts the attack and boarding. Chapter 8 keeps a foreign ship visible in the harbor after Ache's release, Jamil's hearing and the darkening sky. Chapter 9, scene 5, asks whether Ruma would sail while the foreign ships are *still outside*, after several separate post-release interviews and commercial events; scene 6 explicitly advances **three further mornings** for an inquiry boat. The novel therefore appears to keep the foreign vessels near Brunei for an extended post-29-July interval. That is difficult to reconcile with the 29 July sailing and the later departure/refit chronology, though a detailed day ledger and independent navigator comparison are still required before editing prose.

**Acceptance:** (1) align II.7–10 scene boundaries to relative days, including when Ache was boarded, held, released and heard by Jamil; (2) distinguish the high foreign ships physically at the harbor from lingering damage, witnesses and missing persons after departure; (3) inspect Albo's log and the Robertson manuscript notes for any finer departure-date constraint; (4) if conflict confirmed, revise only references to ships still visible/anchored days later, keeping the gold dispute, missing people, trauma and Brunei's independent inquiries intact; (5) update chapter-specific Historical Notes after a tested prose correction. No correction to published prose has yet been authorized by evidence alone.
