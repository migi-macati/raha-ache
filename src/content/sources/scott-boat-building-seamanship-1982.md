[Publisher's bibliographic record](https://archium.ateneo.edu/phstudies/vol30/iss3/3/) confirms the 1982 journal article. An [accessible SPAFA Digest reprint](https://www.spafajournal.org/index.php/spafadigest/article/download/467/459) reproduces its text; this project's reading used that reprint, **not** the publisher's original PDF, which was inaccessible during this check. A separate 1981 National Museum *Anthropological Papers* no. 9 edition (38 pages) exists; do not conflate its pagination with the 1982 journal pagination.

**What the article supports.** Scott draws on archaeological hull finds at Butuan and early European descriptions to explain plank-built construction, edge-pegging, lashings, and varied vessel uses. The Butuan evidence is older and from another region: it does not identify the construction of Ache's particular Maynila boat. The discussion of the *caracoa* explicitly concerns a vessel adapted for warfare, not an ordinary merchant cargo carrier; do not transplant its crew arrangement or fighting platforms into Chapter 6's unnamed trading boat.

**Trade and passage.** In the SPAFA reprint around p. 29, Scott discusses intercommunity movement of goods by boat and records the Visayan maritime term *hinakay* for paying freight, hiring a boat, or taking passage. This is evidence for regional maritime commercial vocabulary and practice as interpreted by Scott, **not** evidence that Ache paid a documented fare, used that Visayan word in Tagalog-speaking Maynila, or followed the novel's precise route. Chapter 6's two passenger fares, negotiated labor, jars, basket repairs and first-landing refusal remain fictional scenes.

**Editorial use.** Cite Scott for comparative boat construction and commercial context with these regional, chronological and genre limits. Do not cite him as an eyewitness to Ache or as proof of a specific hull, crew size, contract, sailing date or landing. For Chapter 6 historical notes, identify the 1982 article and the accessible reprint, and keep all specific character transactions explicitly fictional.
