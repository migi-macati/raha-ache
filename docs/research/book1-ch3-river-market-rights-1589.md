# Book I, Chapter 3–4 research: river-market rights and political allegiance

Research pass: 9 October 2026 (Asia/Manila). Status: source-verified note, pending Studio integration.

## Verified primary text

Juan de Plasencia, *Customs of the Tagalogs*, signed at Nagcarlán on 21 October 1589, printed in Blair and Robertson, *The Philippine Islands*, vol. VII (1903), pp. 165–170: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13701/13701-h/13701-h.htm

- **p. 166:** Chiefs **in some villages** held bounded fisheries and sections of rivers used as markets. People outside their barangay or village paid for fishing or trading privileges, while members were exempt. This is the strongest currently verified historical analogue for the novel's disputed landing charges.
- **pp. 165–167:** The account distinguishes land, work, rowing and service obligations within chief-led communities. These are social relationships, not a standardized territorial customs bureaucracy.
- **pp. 169–170:** Plasencia describes a marriage-related departure payment for certain *maharlica* changing barangay affiliation; he also observes movement toward chiefs offering better protection. Do not generalize the fee to all residents.
- **p. 170:** Arbitrators could be selected from other communities for disputes between barangays or chiefs when fighting was to be avoided.

Plasencia wrote decades after Ache's early-sixteenth-century youth and interviewed selected elders for a colonial audience. His report concerns Tagalog practices in Laguna and neighboring areas; it does **not** prove the specific fee, jurisdiction, date, or river posts depicted in Maynila. William Henry Scott's *Barangay* (1994) is a useful secondary cross-check, but its relevant printed pages have not yet been verified for this note.

## Evidence / inference / invention

**Evidence:** Some Tagalog communities by 1589 had bounded river-market/fishery privileges, membership-based exemptions, work obligations, and cross-community arbitration.

**Inference:** A ruler could turn control of a landing into influence over trade, food, boatmen and households; a rival might offer exemptions or protection to draw allegiance.

**Fiction:** Hamid, the alleged double charge, tally board, Ula's testimony, three blue-marked poles, exact landing geography, and the specific Tondo–Maynila collector. The unnamed cousin in Aganduru Moriz must not silently be identified as the historical Lakandula.

## Chapter implications

**Chapter 3:** A collector's claim to a specific privilege is plausible; its legitimacy remains contested. Ula's evidence that Maynila workers cleared the channel challenges the claimed maintenance rationale, not every conceivable market right. Hamid's unloading is a provisional settlement.

**Chapter 4:** The three poles should mark a claimed mooring/market reach, not an abstract border. Show who pays, who becomes exempt, who controls unloading, whose work maintains access, and which families can move their trade. Ache wants removal; his mother fears turning a disputed privilege into violence. Tondo's motives should remain intelligible.

**Chapter 5:** Loss of reliable trade access, protection, revenue or dependent labor should make Ache's departure a concrete choice, not a purely symbolic quarrel.
