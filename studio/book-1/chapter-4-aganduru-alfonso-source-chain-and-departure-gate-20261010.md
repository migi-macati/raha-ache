## Source chain: verified versus unresolved

**Directly checked secondary scholarship:** Ian Christopher Alfonso, “Revisiting the Provenance of the Very Strange 1589 Last Will and Testament of Don Fernando Malang Balagtas,” *Historical Bulletin* 50 (2016), 31–61, especially printed pp. 46–47 and note 69 on printed p. 56. Full issue PDF: https://pssc.org.ph/wp-content/pssc-archives/Historical%20Bulletin/PHA%20HB%202016.pdf .

Alfonso reproduces an English speech *attributed* to Mahomet-ben-Suleiman by Rodrigo de Aganduru Móriz. In that attributed speech, the speaker says that, after his father's death, his mother could not hold Maynila against a powerful **unnamed** Tondo cousin, and **sent him to Borneo**, where he learned war in his grandfather's service. The passage also makes the speaker a claimant seeking to recover his inheritance. Alfonso explicitly observes that Aganduru Móriz **did not disclose the source of this alleged speech**. The speech must not be represented as an authenticated verbatim statement by Ache.

**Important new provenance finding:** Alfonso's note **69** for the quoted speech cites **Austin Craig, ed., *Gems of Philippine Oratory* (Manila: University of Manila, 1924), pp. 14–15**, rather than giving an exact page from the 1882 Aganduru edition. Therefore the currently accessible English wording has at least this citation intermediary. Do not claim we have compared Craig's English wording with Aganduru's original Spanish. Obtain Craig pp. 14–15 and Aganduru, *Historia general de las Islas Occidentales*, vol. I (1882), relevant Book I chapter and pages; compare their wording, attribution, narrative frame, and any omissions before strengthening the historical claim.

**Bibliography verified, passage not yet checked:** Rodrigo de Aganduru Móriz, *Historia general de la Islas Occidentales á la Asia adyacentes, llamadas Philipinas*, vol. I, Madrid: M. Ginesta, 1882, 544 pp. Google Books record: https://books.google.com/books/about/Historia_general_de_la_Islas_Occidentale.html?id=jiViHQAACAAJ . This confirms an edition, not the quoted passage or the source of the speech.

**Newly located Craig facsimile (10 October 2026):** A digitized PDF of Craig's *Gems of Philippine Oratory* (1924) is indexed at https://kahimyang.com/resources/books/gems-of-philippine-oratory-austin-craig.pdf . Search-indexed page 14 shows the heading “THE GRATITUDE OF AN ANCIENT MANILA KING” and credits the attributed speech to “Pigafetta and Aganduru Moriz.” The same indexed passage identifies an unnamed cousin ruling Tonduk and says the mother sent Ache to Borneo. This independently locates Craig's published wording, but the PDF itself could not be opened for full page-image comparison because of fetch/download limitations. **Do not infer Pigafetta recorded this speech or that Aganduru supplied its verbatim wording** from Craig's composite attribution. Compare Craig's pages 14–15 against the 1882 Spanish original before upgrading provenance. Craig facsimile is a located witness, not a verified contemporaneous transcript.

**Genealogy remains contested:** Alfonso conditionally suggests Malangsi for the Tondo cousin, through the disputed Balagtas testament and other genealogical interpretations. This is a hypothesis, not a settled identification. The name Lakandula in current Book I plans must not be presented in Studio research or public Universe as the *historically established* cousin from Aganduru. Distinguish the late-sixteenth-century Sibunao/Bunao Lakandula attested in Spanish-era materials from any earlier fictional antagonist unless a credible genealogy establishes the relationship.

## Consequences for the narrative

The current Chapter 5 outline specifies that Ache **chooses secret departure** for Brunei; the later attributed speech says his mother **sent** him. The tension is worth preserving without converting either uncertain source into hard historical canon.

**Recommended fictional path to test, not yet adopted canon:** Ache decides to leave against his mother's expressed strategy. She discovers enough to intervene and could stop him, but chooses not to: protecting him abroad may also preserve her ability to govern Maynila without precipitating a war. She supplies a contact, safe passage, or a single practical resource while withholding reconciliation. Ache experiences the departure as his own choice; years later he can truthfully or self-servingly say that she sent him. If used, this needs scene-by-scene causation in Chapters 5–6, not an expository patch or an unearned sentimental blessing. Keep the mother capable of fear, pride, miscalculation, and political self-interest; give Ache a plausible motive for reshaping the story of his departure.

**Chapter 4 structural implication:** Its mother-son confrontation now has a concrete material disagreement (Maynila lacks dependable replacement crews and provisions), but the political consequence should remain active at the end of Chapter 4. The merchant's voluntary decision to arrange the next day's rowers through Tondo is the best existing proof. Preserve Sima's independent bargain and Sulad's obligations. In the final reread, check whether the repeated corrections of Ache by clerk, Sima, mother, Sulad, and Sima again still make the chapter feel instructional. If so, tighten the middle rather than add another lecture. No manuscript rewrite is mandatory in this research pass.

**Title decision:** *The Second Crossing* remains provisionally preferred over *Encroachment*: the disputed second cargo movement and Ache's trespass into Sima's decision both justify it. Reassess at publication, and propagate only if adopted. Existing reader-facing titles remain unchanged.
