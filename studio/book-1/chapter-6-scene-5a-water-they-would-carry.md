# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 5A: The Water They Would Carry

*Studio working prose; opening of departure sequence. Ache narrates. This scene establishes conditions, not a completed booking or departure. The trader and negotiations are fictional.*

I returned to the landing with questions written nowhere.

My mother had told me to name the carrier, the first place he meant to land, the food he required, and how I would send word. Sulad had told me to ask about the water and the work. I had repeated the questions until I thought I could remember them without anyone beside me. I had not practiced hearing an answer I disliked.

The trader was crouched beside his baskets, pressing a thumb into a damp seam. He lifted the basket by its handles and held it away from his knees while water dripped from the reeds.

“You again,” he said.

“I have not brought any food.”

“Then you have learned something.”

“I came to ask what you need before you can name a price that means anything.”

“I named a price.”

“For one person to a place you had not named.”

“And you have come to tell me what is wrong with it.”

“To ask whether you know the place now.”

He set the basket down. “The buyer at the next landing sent word. If the goods are sound when we reach him, he will take most of them. If not, I will look farther along the coast.”

“What is the landing called?”

He told me. I repeated the name and asked which bank the boat would approach. He looked at me more closely, as if he had expected a prince's son to be interested only in the farthest shore.

“Why?”

“My mother will not give me anything until I can tell her where I might be put ashore.”

“She is wise.”

“She wants to know how I will send word.”

“Then you must find someone there willing to bring it. I will not promise that my crew can return to Maynila for your letter.”

I nearly said that a message could travel with any boat. The intermediary's brother had taught me what that sentence cost.

“If I cannot find a carrier there,” I said, “I will wait until I can.”

“Will you pay for the waiting?”

“I will have to.”

He smiled without showing his teeth. “Better.”

I pointed to the jars near the bow. “Sulad asked about water. Who carries it?”

“The crew carries it aboard. The owner pays for the jars. Whoever drinks must help keep them safe. If you break one, it comes out of what you owe.”

“And the food?”

“You bring your own until the first landing. You do not take from the crew's pot because you have a fine name.”

“I had not meant to.”

“You had not asked.”

I looked toward the baskets. The men loading them were passing the weight from one pair of hands to another, waiting until each grip was firm. I had learned that much at the fishing bank: pulling first did not make a thing move safely.

“If another man travels with me,” I said, “he knows boats better than I do. Would you charge him as a passenger?”

“What can he do?”

“He mends nets. He knows the river and the fishing boats.”

“Does he know this cargo?”

“No.”

“Then he would learn. And until he learned, he would take space like you.”

“Could he work for part of the price?”

“He could ask me himself.”

I was ready with an answer about his uncle and the net. I kept it behind my teeth.

The trader stood and called to a crewman to move the leaking basket out of the sun. “Come back when you know how many are coming and whose food they will eat. I will know more about my cargo tomorrow. Perhaps there will be room. Perhaps not.”

“When will you leave?”

“When the baskets are sound and the buyer still wants them.”

It was not the promise I wanted. It was a better answer than he had given me on my earlier visit.

I walked away before I could spoil it by offering silver for a place that did not exist. At the end of the landing I stopped and watched the crew move the basket into shade. The wet reeds had left a dark mark on the planks. Someone brought a length of fiber to bind the seam. The trader bent over the repair with the others.

I had thought departure would be a single morning, the boat slipping away while everyone on shore watched. Instead it was baskets that must hold, water that must be carried, a buyer who might change his mind, and people who could refuse me even after I had found the right questions.

I still had to take those answers home.
