# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 3a: A Place in the Shade (Studio prose candidate)

*Continuation of Scene 2. Ache narrates. The displaced crewman receives an explicit, limited care arrangement before any replacement-labor bargain. No illness diagnosis, destination, berth, or sailing agreement is established.*

The woman who had sent the sick man ashore called her crew back from the boat. One of them had already taken his pole. Another was holding the bundle he had left beneath the thwart.

“Give that to him,” she said.

The man with the bundle looked toward the shade. “It will get wet there.”

“Then find a dry place for it.”

He stood still long enough that I thought he meant to argue. Then he carried it to the rack above the landing and set it on the highest board.

The sick man raised his head.

“That is mine.”

“I know,” the crewman said.

“Then leave it where I can reach it.”

The crewman brought it down and put it beside him. A corner of the cloth opened. Inside were a small knife in a sheath and a folded length of cord. The sick man closed the bundle with one hand and lay back.

The woman went to the man who had received the mats. He was checking the bindings again, though he had already been paid for them.

“Can he stay here?” she asked.

The man looked at the sick sailor. “For how long?”

“Until he can stand and work.”

“That could be tomorrow. It could be longer.”

She did not answer.

“I have room beneath the rack until the sun moves,” he said. “After that I need the boards. I have no one to watch him at night.”

The woman looked across the landing. Two houses stood farther up the bank, beyond a path cut by many bare feet. She asked who lived in the nearer one. The mat buyer called to a woman working beside its doorway. She came down without hurrying.

I could not hear everything they said. I heard the price of a meal, then the price of a place to sleep. The woman from the house wanted to know whether the man could walk there. No one could promise it.

Sulad stood beside me with our jar. He had given the sick man water, and the jar was lighter for it. He did not offer more until the man asked.

The boat's leader returned to her crew and opened a small pouch. She counted out payment in the sight of the woman from the house. The house woman took it, then made the leader repeat what had been bought: food that evening, a place out of the rain, and someone to bring water. Nothing beyond the next morning.

“And if he is still ill?” the house woman asked.

The leader looked toward the water. “I will leave word with the mat buyer. If another of our boats comes, he will know whom to ask.”

The mat buyer heard this and shook his head.

“You can leave a name,” he said. “You cannot leave me a promise I did not make.”

She considered him, then nodded. “A name, then.”

The sick man had been listening. “Ask me first,” he said.

The leader went to him. They spoke quietly. He showed her the knife and cord in his bundle and made her put both back. When she rose, he called the house woman over and told her himself that he would come if two people helped him along the path.

I watched them lift him. He put weight on his feet, but not enough to walk without their arms. They took him slowly, stopping once where the path rose above the bank. The bundle went with him.

The leader stayed at the foot of the path until he reached the house. Then she came back to the boat.

“There may be work,” she told me.

I looked at the place the sick man had left.

“Has he agreed that you can take someone else?”

“He has agreed to stay,” she said. “That is not the same question.”

I had been about to ask how far they were going. Instead I waited for her to tell me what the work would be.
