# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 10: Where the Count Ends

The wind eased before the banks closed around us.

The sail hung against its line, and the master had the men take in what they no longer needed. A pole found bottom on our right. Then another reached from the bow. The vessel turned slowly into a creek so narrow that I could hear someone splitting wood behind the trees.

I had watched Daya's covering until my knees hurt. Each time a fold lifted, I called the crewman or waited for him to see it himself. Once he told me there was nothing to report. I kept watching.

At the landing, a man stood on a platform above the mud with a board across his knees. A boy beside him held a cord with knots spaced along it. The man did not come down when the master called. He waited for the tally keeper to climb up and show him her marks.

“Two parcels from Daya,” she said. “One lot. The red-edged one came aboard first.”

The man looked past her at the covering. “I was told to expect one.”

“It was made into two before loading. Both are on my board.”

He examined the marks. “Bring them separately.”

The master sent the other men to lift the covering. The crewman beside me raised its outer fold and held it while the first parcel came out. I moved clear. No one asked me to take an end.

The red edge was still visible. Its wrapping had darkened along one seam, but when the receiving man pressed it, no water came through. He told the boy to put one knot against the first mark. They carried it up the short path and set it on a dry plank.

The second parcel was not where the receiving man expected it.

He looked into the boat. “You said they were together.”

“I said they were counted separately,” the tally keeper answered. “The other is beside the small brown bundle.”

The man holding the covering pointed farther in. A carrier had moved the brown bundle to make a path for the first parcel, and Daya's second lay behind it. Its wrapping was plain. From the landing it looked like any of the other goods.

I saw the tally keeper's mouth tighten. She had made the men say where both parcels stood before we left, but she had not been beside them for every lift.

“There,” I said, and pointed without climbing over the cargo.

The covering man leaned in, found the edge she had described, and called another carrier to help. They lifted the parcel between them. Its bottom had been resting on a dry mat.

The receiving man looked at the tally keeper. “You could have sent me two of anyone's goods.”

“You can open the outer fold,” she said. “Daya's strip is tucked inside the knot.”

He loosened only enough to see it. A narrow piece of the same red cloth showed beneath the plain wrapping. He touched it, closed the fold, and told the boy to move the next knot.

The tally keeper watched him make the second mark before she let the carriers take it up.

I had thought the counting ended when the goods came ashore. It ended only when the man who must answer for them agreed to take them.

The other loads followed. I helped carry water from a pot at the landing to the men who were working, because the master told me I could do that without stepping among the parcels. Sulad emptied the last water from the bottom of the vessel under the crewman's eye. He handed the vessel back before the man had to ask.

The receiving man and the tally keeper counted the goods once more. The master waited below, holding the payment board out of the mud. When the receiving man finally agreed, he made his mark beside hers.

“No complaint on the two?” the master asked.

“The cloth is here. I have not opened what belongs to Daya.”

“Then say what you received.”

“Two parcels. One lot.”

Only then did the master send for the crew's food.

The packets were smaller than those we had eaten at the jar landing. I took mine from the master. Sulad took his from the man who had supervised him. The tally keeper saw both hands receive them and made no new mark. The food was the settlement the master had promised for this leg. He owed us no berth beyond the creek.

We ate on a dry root above the mud while the crew prepared to leave.

“You can come back with us,” the master said when he passed. “You will carry your own jar. There is no work for you on the return, but I have room.”

“Back to the landing where we found you?” I asked.

“Yes.”

I could picture the jar buyer's roof, the woman who had told the truth about my rope, and the place where the larger vessel had waited. We knew how to reach that landing. We knew nothing of the water beyond this creek.

“Is there a boat going farther?” I asked.

The master pointed to the receiving man. “Ask him about goods. He knows what leaves here. I know where I take mine.”

He walked away before I could turn the question into a request he had not offered to answer.

Sulad finished his food. He folded the leaf and tucked it beneath a root rather than letting it fall into the water.

“I would go back if I were alone,” he said.

“Why?”

“We know someone there who has seen us work.”

I looked at him. “But you are not alone.”

“That is why I am still sitting here.”

He stood and took our jar to the water pot. He asked the woman guarding it whether he could fill it. She told him how much she could spare.

The receiving man was tying his board shut when I reached the platform.

“Does anyone carry goods south from here?” I asked.

“Goods go wherever their owners can pay to send them.”

“I am looking for Brunei.”

He stopped winding the cord. “You are looking a long way from where you stand.”

“Is there a boat?”

“There are boats that take goods to a trading anchorage farther along the coast. Sometimes people there deal with merchants from Borneo. That does not mean one of them will take you, or that the same boat will go on.”

“Who is leaving?”

“A man is bringing wax and dried fish tomorrow. If his goods come, he will need them carried to the outer water. You may speak to him. I do not speak for him.”

“Where will he be?”

“Here, if he comes.”

I wanted a name and a promise. He gave me a place to wait.

Below us the master called his crew aboard. The tally keeper was already on the vessel, her board wrapped against the spray. The covering man looked up at me once and raised his hand. I raised mine. Then the boat pushed away, taking with it the one passage we could have accepted without another bargain.

Sulad came back with the jar filled below its neck.

“There may be a man tomorrow,” I told him. “His goods go to an anchorage where people sometimes trade with Borneo.”

“May be?”

“That is what the receiver said.”

Sulad watched the boat turn out of the creek. “Then we have chosen to wait.”

“We can ask the receiver for a place out of the rain.”

“We can ask,” he said. “He can refuse.”

He did not say it to teach me anything. He looked at the mud where our feet had sunk and then at the dry boards above it.

I went back to the platform. The receiving man was carrying his board indoors. I asked whether we could sleep beneath the raised floor, clear of the goods, until the man with the wax came.

He looked at our jar and at Sulad waiting below. “Do not touch what I have counted. Leave before the carriers begin in the morning.”

We put our jar between us beneath the platform. There was room enough to lie on our sides, though not to stretch our legs.

Above us, the man moved Daya's parcels farther from the edge. I heard one scrape across the boards and then the other. The second sound was heavier.

The boat we had come on was already out of hearing. If the man with the wax did not arrive, we would wake at the same creek with less food and no agreed passage.

Sulad lay down facing the open water. I stayed awake long enough to see the last light leave the mud.
