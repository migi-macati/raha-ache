# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 3A: What the House Could Spare

*Studio working prose, first half of Scene 3. Ache's first-person narration. The trader has offered no berth; the captain is still missing. All dialogue and transactions are fictional.*

My mother was counting jars.

She had them brought into the shaded room one at a time, each set on the floor with its mouth toward her. The steward removed the cover, she looked inside, and he put it back. Some jars were full enough that the grain pressed against the cover. Others made a hollow sound when he moved them.

I had expected to find her with the men who came to speak of Tondo. I had rehearsed my request as though she would be sitting among them and I could make it before anyone found a reason to interrupt.

“Wait,” she said when she saw me.

The steward tipped a jar toward the light. A dark line ran down its side. He touched the crack and rubbed his thumb across the grain that had lodged there.

“Do not put that one with the sound jars,” she told him.

“We can bind it.”

“Bind it, then. It still leaks.”

He set it aside. My mother made a mark on a narrow board and asked for the next.

“Is this all ours?” I said.

She looked up. “No.”

“Then why are we counting it?”

“Because it is here.”

The steward carried another jar across the room. I knew better than to ask again while he was working, but the trader's price had been going through my head since morning. It seemed to grow each time I tried to remember it.

“I have found someone who may take me south,” I said.

My mother did not look away from the jar. “May?”

“He carries goods. He knows men who travel farther.”

“Does he know your captain?”

“No.”

“Does he know where you will land?”

“He knows where he will try to sell his cargo.”

“Then you have found a merchant.”

“A merchant with a boat.”

“We have many of those.”

“He would consider a passenger.”

“Consider?”

I told her what he had asked: how many people, how much food, where I intended to go after his first stop. I gave her the price he had named. I did not make it smaller, though I wanted to.

The steward stopped lifting the next jar. He was close enough to hear. My mother noticed.

“Finish the count,” she told him.

He lifted the jar. I waited while she inspected it, and resented him for taking so long.

“You cannot pay that,” she said at last.

“I could if you gave me some grain for the journey.”

“Whose grain?”

I looked at the jars. “From our stores.”

She touched the cracked one with her foot. “That belongs to a merchant who has not yet been paid for it. The two behind you are promised to households upriver. The jars by the door are ours, but I have already told the steward where they must go.”

“How much is left?”

“Less than you think.”

I wanted to say she could change the promises. I had seen her do it. She had sent men to another landing with a word, and they had gone.

“Would you take food from a house you had promised to feed,” she asked, “so a trader might let you sit beside his baskets?”

“He might carry me toward Brunei.”

“He might carry you to the first buyer of his goods.”

The steward returned with the board. My mother looked at the marks, then at the cracked jar.

“The merchants have been asking whether they must use the organizer's crews,” he said.

“I told them to bring me their own offers.”

“They heard that they must bring them. Two men say they cannot trade until you approve their boatmen.”

She stared at him. “That is not what I said.”

“It is what they heard.”

I had never seen her hesitate before the steward. Now she stood with the board in her hand, reading the marks he had made. She had wanted the merchants to name a price for their own crews so the organizer could not claim every paddle. The merchants had heard a new permission they must purchase with their time.

“Tell them they may trade,” she said. “They may hire whom they choose. I asked for offers, not obedience.”

“They will ask why you asked.”

“Tell them I wanted to know what a crew costs when its men are paid.”

The steward nodded, but did not move.

“What else?” she said.

“The young rower from the first crossing is waiting outside.”

“Does he want to speak?”

“He wants work.”

“And his wages?”

“He says he still has none.”

My mother closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them she was looking at me.

“Your trader wants payment before he knows whether he has room,” she said. “Our rower has done the work and cannot get payment afterward. Which bargain would you have me settle first?”

I knew she expected an answer. I could think of none that would help me leave.
