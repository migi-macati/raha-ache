# Book I, Chapter 5 — Scene 5: A Name Without a Boat (Studio working prose)

Ula was sorting shells on a mat when I found her. The small ones lay in one pile, the broken ones in another. She did not look up until I had stepped around the mat twice.

"If you are going to make the light move," she said, "sit down."

I sat.

"Have you come to ask what your mother thinks?"

"I know what she thinks."

"Then why do you look as though you have lost an argument?"

"I have."

"That is a useful beginning."

I told her about the captain we had met upriver. She remembered him. She remembered, too, that he had laughed when I spoke Malay too quickly and answered me faster than I could understand.

"I need to find him," I said.

"Do you know his name?"

I hesitated. I had been so intent on what he knew of Brunei that I had never asked for the name by which his own men called him.

Ula stopped sorting.

"His ship came from the west," I said. "The headman knew him."

"Many ships come from the west. More go away."

"I could send someone to the headman."

"You could."

"Would you?"

She picked up a shell, found a crack along its edge, and set it with the broken ones.

"What would the message say?"

"That Ache of Maynila wishes to speak with the captain who came upriver."

"And what does Ache of Maynila offer him for his trouble?"

"I can pay."

"With what?"

I thought of the grain my mother had ordered tied shut. It had been set aside for one paid crossing, not for every favor I might need on the way to Brunei.

"Not from the stores," I said.

"Good. You have learned something since morning."

"I have silver."

"You told Sulad that last time."

"I have some left."

"Then count it before you promise it."

I pulled the little pieces from my cloth and laid them beside her shells. She did not touch them. She asked how much of it I was prepared to lose if the captain could not be found.

"All of it," I said.

"Then you have not counted carefully enough."

I gathered the pieces again, leaving one on the mat.

"For a message," I said. "Not a passage."

Ula nodded. "That is a price I can ask someone to consider."

"Who?"

"A woman whose brother carries goods upriver. She may be here tomorrow. Or she may have left this morning. I will not promise what she has not agreed to."

I leaned forward. "If she knows where the captain is—"

"Then she will tell you what she knows. She will not know what he will do next."

Outside, a boatman called to someone on the bank. I knew the voice from our landing, though I could not see his face.

"Do not say I am looking for Brunei," I told her.

Ula raised her eyes.

"Why?"

"Because Tondo would hear."

"Tondo hears things because people speak. So does Maynila."

"If the wrong man knows—"

"Then perhaps he will tell your mother before you do."

"She knows."

"That you wish to go. Does she know you are already spending silver to find a captain?"

I had not thought of the difference.

Ula pushed the unbroken shells into a neat line. "I will ask whether the woman can carry your message. I will not tell her it is a summons from a ruler. It is a request from a young man who met a captain and failed to learn his name."

"She will laugh."

"Perhaps. A laugh is cheaper than a lie."

I stood, then stopped. "Will you tell my mother?"

"She sent you to me once because she thought I would teach you to listen. You have come back asking me to keep a secret. That is not the same thing."

"Will you?"

"I have not decided."

It was the worst answer she could have given me, because it was the one she had a right to give.

At the door I heard her begin sorting again.

"Ache," she called.

I turned.

"If the captain agrees to speak, ask where he is going before you ask him to take you there."

I went back toward the river with less silver than I had brought and no nearer to a ship. But someone might now carry my question upriver. For the first time, that seemed like an action rather than a wish.
