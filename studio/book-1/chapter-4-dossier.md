### Canon and chronological position
The **published** Chapter 3, rather than the alternate manuscript in draft PR #15, controls continuity. Ache, Ula and Sulad return to Maynila near sunset. Under the mother's one-season agreement, two clerks compare tally boards. Seven vessels have passed since midday without double collection. The Tondo clerk has also marked crew strokes. He recalls a woman who **came to Tondo last month** seeking a missing husband, and cannot answer immediately when Ache asks who may inspect the board. The Chapter 4 working scene starts at first light the **next day**. It is a new cargo boat arriving, not the same vessel observed across the river at dusk.

Chapter 1 leaves an injured rower and damaged fishing stakes. Chapter 2 contains a long damages hearing, a missed passage with salt and dried fish, Ache's inadequate resources, and Sulad's qualified consent to help repair nets. Chapter 3 adds religious and linguistic differences and a useful but politically ambiguous recordkeeping arrangement. Chapter 4 must not duplicate the Chapter 2 hearing, invent a second lost-buyer plot as its major reversal, or make Ache forget that Sulad has independent obligations.

### Evidence register: direct, later, inferred, invented
**Direct primary comparative source (checked against full transcription on 9 Oct):** Juan de Plasencia, *Customs of the Tagalogs*, Nagcarlán, 21 October 1589; English translation in Blair & Robertson, *The Philippine Islands*, vol. VII. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Philippine_Islands,_1493%E2%80%931898/Volume_7/Customs . In the opening relation, Plasencia says that freeborn *maharlica* followers rowed for chiefs when summoned, and that *aliping namamahay* householders could row for their patrons. He distinguishes these categories and records local river-market/fishery privileges and arbitration between barangays. These are **late-sixteenth-century Tagalog observations**, filtered through a missionary's categories and informants, and cannot establish the exact labor status, port office, legal vocabulary or contract procedure of Maynila around 1500.

**Archaeology (checked museum page on 9 Oct):** National Museum of the Philippines, Pandanan Shipwreck, a mid-fifteenth-century trading wreck off southern Palawan, with diverse Asian ceramics and maritime material. https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/maritime-and-underwater-cultural-heritage/maritime-pandanan-shipwreck-2/ . Supports long-distance commerce in the archipelago, **not** a specific Pasig loading operation, the design of the chapter's boat, or the transfer contract.

**Scholarship to consult, not yet fully read this pass:** Bobby C. Orillaneda, “Maritime Trade in the Philippines During the 15th Century CE,” *Moussons* 27 (2016), 83–100, DOI 10.4000/moussons.3529. Metadata accessible; publisher full text not independently verified.

**Later port testimony:** Anonymous Spanish *Relation of the Voyage to Luzon* (1570), Blair & Robertson vol. III, describes a later political/commercial setting. It cannot be used as an eyewitness account of Ache's childhood. Distinguish Spanish claims and merchants' complaints from independently established facts.

**Retrospective historical narrative — unresolved:** Rodrigo de Aganduru Móriz, *Historia general*, Book I, chapter XIII (seventeenth-century text, nineteenth-century print). Need original Spanish wording and pagination for Ache's mother, Tondo cousin and Brunei connection. Do not identify that unnamed cousin with Lakandula as an established fact; do not equate Siripada with Sultan Bolkiah without separate proof.

**Inferences:** Different people at a landing plausibly possess different labor obligations; river access may confer political leverage; a useful shared record can enable new claims. All three are *plausibility judgments*, not dated proof of a specific c.1500 institution.

**Fictional inventions:** Sima and her household, organizer and sister's son, Tondo and Maynila clerks, paired tally boards, seven-boat count, one-season agreement, disputed second cargo transfer, settlement, individual speech and psychology, and the precise boat movements. Never attribute any of these to Plasencia, archaeological finds, or Aganduru Móriz.

### Geography, water, vessels and physical action
Setting: a fictional working landing in the Maynila–Tondo river/bay trading environment, not a surveyed modern Pasig embankment. The small boat arrives **empty** at the bank. Sima's baskets wait **on shore**. The plan is to load them onto the small boat for onward transfer to a larger vessel farther toward the bay. A river current pushes the empty boat off the landing; a bowline, paddle and willing hands keep it in position. A loose basket binding forces a pause. This physical complication has a cause and a cost: waiting rowers and lost work, and it exposes who knows the cargo best.

Avoid exact tidal hours, a named historical ship class, the geometry of a modern quay, an assumed standardized tariff, and an unsupported claim that every crewman is a freely paid hireling. Check that the direction of the current, handling of bowline and paddle, and the relative locations of bank, small boat and trading ship remain consistent in each paragraph. “Small cargo boat” is a descriptive English gloss, not a verified contemporary type name.

### Everyday life, hierarchy and economic stakes
The organizer must provision crews even if a customer disputes a second journey. One rower is his **fictional sister's son** and therefore has a kin obligation that complicates a simple wage transaction; he still has an independent grievance about what work was promised and whether he was paid. The rower is neither automatically a slave nor an independent modern employee. Plasencia warns against collapsing labor statuses, but the novel's particular kin relationship is invented.

Sima counts baskets, judges bindings, chooses when a vessel is safe to load and negotiates a smaller two-basket shipment. She does not need Ache to bargain for her. Her main irreversible loss is **confidence in Ache's representation**, even if goods can still move. Tondo retains its advantage in assembling crews and may demand clearer witnessed terms in future. A merchant asks about reserving two rowers for tomorrow, but the organizer only promises an answer in Tondo: **no completed booking or lost Maynila contract is shown**. The mother's refusal to exclude Tondo crews is the actual policy consequence, and the merchant's willingness to negotiate across the river signals the commercial pressure. The mother's bargain can narrow an obligation but cannot create replacement crews or food without cost. Sulad's family's fishing stakes still need attention.

Objects with causal roles: paired wooden tally boards and cutting knife (record versus obligation), basket cords and carrying pole (Sima's expertise), mooring line and paddle (labor and river hazard), stakes and net (Sulad's competing obligation). Do not introduce a specific coin, standardized paper contract or later colonial administrative title without evidence.

### Religion, language, writing and material culture
Chapter 3 already stages Ache's Muslim commitment, Ula's rites and Sulad's family charm. Chapter 4 need not force another doctrinal debate into a cargo dispute. Different beliefs may remain present in ordinary gestures or withheld comments. Plasencia's 1589 religious account is later, regionally varied and missionary-filtered; it is not a license to assign Ula a particular ritual office in 1500.

Dialogue is modern English translation for reader access, not a reconstruction of exact early Tagalog, Malay or Brunei speech. Do not present the paired boards as documented indigenous writing or a universal port register; they are physical tallies invented for this novel. Distinguish Ache's household rank from the organizer's control over labor and Sima's commercial authority.

### Seven scene beats and reversals
1. **Next morning:** Ache presses the Tondo clerk about who may see a rower's marks. The clerk wants to help missing families but visibly hesitates when a powerful claimant is imagined.
2. **Boat and cargo:** an empty cargo boat swings in the current; Sima halts loading until terms are clear and demonstrates practical authority.
3. **Contested second transfer:** Sima and organizer recall different conditions. A related rower discloses that his labor was promised without his clear knowledge. Neither organizer nor household is wholly dishonest.
4. **Public escalation:** Ache equates an uncertain service claim with Tondo's political encroachment; Sima rejects his right to speak for her. The organizer's crew leaves for other work.
5. **Limited judgment:** the mother acknowledges that her own tally arrangement counted people beyond its stated purpose. Witnesses can address the disputed second transfer; no ruling supplies food, hands or trust.
6. **Sulad and the limits of substitution:** Ache asks Sulad for a crew, then helps brace fishing stakes. He discovers that his own labor cannot replace Sulad's skill, the damaged net, or his family's prior obligations. No premature public promise or retraction occurs in the current manuscript.
7. **Independent bargain and closing image:** Sima negotiates a limited shipment with another boat owner. Ache apologizes, but she does not restore his role. The clerk asks whether to record the new rowers; Ache tells him to ask the people concerned. She makes the clerk wait.

### Character arc, POV and theme
Ache-dominant first person, approximately sixteen: quick with boats, impatient with ambiguity, embarrassed by visible failure, inclined to substitute protective intent for others' agency. Do not let him speak in fully formed contemporary rights theory. His mother is an effective regent who admits a real administrative mistake and cannot solve every material shortage. Sulad's family obligations remain independent of Ache's wishes. Chapter 3 now contains Ache's uncertain wish for Sulad's attention; Chapter 4 must preserve that asymmetry without inventing Sulad's reciprocal attraction or reverting to the superseded labor-promise argument. Sima's life and interests continue after Ache leaves. The organizer must remain a credible supplier rather than a conspiracy puppet. The Tondo clerk is conscientious, proud and susceptible to pressure.

**Main plot:** encroachment through useful services, information and the ability to allocate crews. **Subplots:** mother/son strategic rupture; Ache/Sulad unequal friendship; Sima's commercial agency; the unresolved missing sailor; consequences of Chapter 1 fishing damage. **Theme:** legitimacy depends on usefulness and recognition, which a ruler cannot simply command. **Historical suspense:** readers may know Ache's later exile and return, but the immediate outcome should turn on believable choices, not a predetermined modern moral lesson.
