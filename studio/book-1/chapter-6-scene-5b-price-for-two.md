# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 5B: A Price for Two

*Studio working prose; follows Scene 5A. Ache narrates. The trader, fare and labor terms are fictional; no confirmed berth, maternal gold transfer or departure.*

I reached the fishing bank after the net had been hauled.

Sulad was rinsing mud from the weights. His uncle had spread the catch beneath a cloth to keep the flies off it. The basket stood well above the water, where I had put it the day before.

“You're late,” Sulad said.

“I went to the trader.”

“You said you'd come for the fish.”

“I did come.”

“The fish came earlier.”

His uncle held out a weight with its cord broken. I took it and sat beside the net. He showed me how the cord passed twice through the knot before he let me try. My first knot slipped. I made the next one tighter, and it held when he pulled it.

Sulad watched without speaking. I wanted him to see that I could learn, which made my fingers clumsy again.

“The trader knows his first landing now,” I said.

“Does he know whether he wants us?”

“He wants to know whether you can work.”

“Then he should ask me.”

“I told him that.”

Sulad rinsed the last weight and laid it on the sand. His uncle looked from him to me.

“You can go and ask,” the uncle said. “The net will still need mending when you return.”

Sulad stood. He did not thank me for bringing the news.

We found the trader counting water jars. A crewman lifted each jar by its neck while the trader checked the mouth for chips. Sulad waited until the crewman had set one down before he spoke.

“I hear you might carry two men.”

“I might carry one,” the trader said. “I have not counted tomorrow's cargo.”

“One of us can work.”

“Which one?”

Sulad looked at me. “He can count baskets.”

The trader laughed. I did not.

“What can you do?” he asked Sulad.

“I know when a line is wearing through. I can mend nets and work a fishing boat. I have not worked this boat.”

“That last part matters.”

“I know.”

The trader gestured toward the jars. “If I take you, you'll help load water and move cargo when we stop. You won't touch a line unless one of my men tells you. You'll bring your own food. The fare is the fare.”

“Then you're charging me to work.”

“I'm charging you to take up space. The work is what keeps you from making trouble in it.”

Sulad crouched beside a jar. A thin line of wet sand clung to its base. He rubbed it away with his thumb, then stood.

“If I carry cargo at the landing, you pay me for that work,” he said. “If I travel as a passenger, tell me what you charge. Don't call me crew when you need hands and passenger when you want silver.”

The trader stopped counting. The crewman held the next jar in his arms and waited.

“I pay men who carry my goods,” the trader said. “I don't pay strangers to learn where to stand.”

“Then I'll pay to travel and do the work any passenger must do. I won't promise more.”

“And if I need the space for baskets?”

“You'll take the baskets.”

The trader looked surprised that Sulad had answered so quickly. I was surprised too, though I should not have been.

“And if there is room for only one?” I asked.

Sulad turned to me. “Then you decide whether to go.”

“Without you?”

“I have a home here. You are the one asking to leave it.”

The crewman lowered the jar. The sound it made against the planks was small and final.

The trader resumed his count. “Come tomorrow after the buyers have seen the cargo. Bring the number of people, the food, and the payment. If there's room, we'll talk about the first landing. If there isn't, find another boat.”

I wanted to ask him to hold two places. I knew what he would say.

As we walked away, Sulad asked, “Have you told your mother what the trader said?”

“Not yet.”

“Then don't tell her I've agreed.”

“Have you?”

“I agreed to ask him.”

He walked ahead, leaving me to follow through the crowd. At the fishing bank his uncle would be waiting with a net still torn in three places. I had learned one knot. I did not know whether Sulad would come tomorrow, or whether I could ask him to wait if the trader found room for only one.
