# Book I, Chapter 9 — Compressed Scenes 10–12: The Missing Piece

The banks came close enough that I could hear wood being split beyond the trees. A man at the bow lowered his pole into the brown water and found the bottom. Another did the same on our right. The master let the sail fall and brought us toward a platform raised above the mud.

I had watched Daya's covering until the backs of my knees ached. Twice the wind lifted a corner, and twice I called the man responsible for it. Now he raised the whole cloth. The receiving man waited on the platform with a board across his knees. Beside him a boy held a cord of knots.

“Two parcels,” the tally keeper said. “Daya's cloth. One lot.”

“I was told one,” the receiver answered.

“One lot made into two. Count what comes ashore.”

The red-edged parcel was easy to find. Its seam had darkened, but the cloth beneath felt dry when the receiver pressed it. He sent it to a plank under the roof and told the boy to move one knot.

The second parcel was hidden behind a brown bundle someone had shifted while making a path for the first. The receiver looked from the plain wrapping to the tally keeper's board.

“How do I know whose this is?”

“Open the knot,” she said.

The carrier lifted the parcel while I stood clear of his feet. A strip of red cloth lay tucked beneath its outer fold. The receiver saw it, closed the wrapping, and made the boy move another knot. The tally keeper waited until he had marked both parcels before she released them.

The master settled his account only after the receiver accepted the goods. Sulad had worked under the water crewman; I had watched the covering. We were each given a packet of food, the payment promised for that leg. We ate above the mud while the boat was made ready to leave.

“There is room if you come back,” the master told us. “No work on the return. You carry your own jar.”

I looked at the boat. I knew its men now. I knew the landing it would return to, the buyer with his jars, and the woman who had told the truth about my mistake with a rope. Going back would cost us a day and take us no closer to anyone who could answer for the water toward Brunei.

Sulad folded the leaf around the last of his meal.

“If I were alone, I would go,” he said.

“Then why stay?”

He looked toward the men pushing the boat off. “Because I have not decided to leave you.”

It was less than I wanted him to say. I had no right to ask for more.

The receiver knew of a carrier who sometimes brought wax and dried fish to an anchorage farther along the coast. He did not know whether the goods would arrive, or whether their owner would take us. The next boat was already easing away from the platform. I could have called after it. Instead I asked for somewhere dry to wait.

“Beneath the boards,” the receiver said. “Keep away from the goods. Leave when the carriers begin.”

We lay with our jar between us. Above our heads Daya's parcels scraped over the planks as the receiver moved them farther from the edge. One sounded heavier than the other. I listened until the creek grew quiet.

By morning the mud had hardened around our footprints. A woman stood on the path with two baskets and a sack that smelled of dried fish. The receiver pointed to her when I asked about the man he had mentioned.

“Her brother has the boat. She owns the wax.”

She heard him and set down her carrying pole. She made Sulad show how he would lift a basket without dragging it along the path. She made me do the same. We carried her goods to the water, where her brother was examining the gathered sail. His boat sat lower than the one that had brought us. I could see planks beneath my feet and water glinting between two floorboards. I could not tell what kind of weather it would bear.

“My fish goes to the anchorage,” he said. “Her wax goes with it. That is as far as I will take you.”

He offered food on arrival for work during the crossing. Sulad agreed to pass the water vessel to the man who emptied the boat's bottom. I agreed to watch the lashings around the wax basket and call if they shifted. The woman put my hand on the cord to show me its place, then moved it away.

“You are to watch it,” she said. “I will mend it if it comes loose.”

She counted eight wrapped pieces of wax before closing the basket. Her brother counted the fish sacks separately. Sulad found a place for our drinking jar away from the loads. He answered the brother's terms himself, and I answered mine.

We left while the receiver was still counting someone else's goods.

For a time the bank stayed near. The woman lifted the corner of the mat over her wax to check for spray. The younger crewman moved between the mast and the fish. Sulad passed the small water vessel aft, keeping his knees clear when the older man turned to throw it over the side. I watched the cord as I had been told.

The first drops left dark spots on the mat. A grey band of rain crossed the water ahead. The brother called for the sail to be taken in. I watched the crew's hands and could not tell which line was doing the work.

The boat rolled. A fish sack slid against the mast, and the younger man put his foot against it. The wax cord lifted from the basket's far side.

“It is moving,” I called.

“I see it,” the woman answered.

She reached across the basket as the young man slipped. His knee struck the floor. The fish sack rolled over his wrist. He cried out and tried to pull free.

I put both hands against the wax basket. It pressed toward the trapped man. If I moved, it would slide farther; if I held it, the woman could reach the cord. I had no hand left to lift the fish.

“His wrist!” I shouted.

“Hold the wax,” the woman said.

Her brother left the steering paddle with the older man and came forward low, one hand against the side. The rain struck so hard that the bank disappeared. Sulad set down his vessel and started toward us, but the older crewman called him back. Water had collected around the young man's feet. Sulad cleared it, making space for the brother to plant his own foot.

“Lift the fish,” the brother ordered.

The woman pulled at the wax cord. It was trapped beneath the basket where my palms held it.

“Let go,” she said.

“The sack—”

“I can see it. Let go.”

I took my hands away. The basket moved. She caught one handle, but the wet covering slipped from the other. A wrapped piece of wax rolled free. It struck a rib of wood and vanished beneath the floorboards, where the water ran dark.

The brother and his sister lifted the sack. The young man pulled out his hand and held it to his chest. He could move some of his fingers. When the woman asked him to move the smallest, he tried and turned his face away.

Sulad was still passing the small vessel aft. It filled too slowly. He reached for our drinking jar.

“That is our water,” I said.

He tipped the clean water over the side and gave the empty jar to the older man. The man dipped it into the water gathering in the boat and threw that water out. Sulad took it back and filled it again. I wanted to stop him. The boat had begun to settle lower where we stood, and I had no better vessel to offer.

The sail lay gathered and wet. The brother returned to the steering paddle. The woman counted the wax beneath its mat.

“Seven,” she said.

She counted again. The young man sat with his wrist against his chest. No one had asked me to lift the fish or open the wax.

“Where did it go?” the woman asked.

“Under the boards. I saw it fall. I did not see it leave the boat.”

She looked at the place I indicated. “Then help me look when the water is down.”

The older crewman raised one loose floorboard and felt beneath it. He found a leaf and a length of cord, both black with mud. I searched where the woman pointed, but the wax was not there. She closed the basket when her brother called for the crewman aft. There was still water to empty.

The young man had a cloth tied around his swollen wrist. I asked whether it hurt.

“What do you think?” he said.

I sat beside the mast. I had wanted to ask whether he would work again. I could not tell him that I had been frightened of losing the wax while his hand was under the fish.

The woman laid out the seven remaining pieces.

“If they pay for seven, I lose one.”

Her brother looked at the young man. “We got his hand free.”

“He was holding the basket,” she said, pointing to me.

“You told him to,” the brother answered.

“And then I told him to move. I know what I told him.”

She wrapped the seven again. The board at the anchorage would record what she delivered. Nothing we said now would put an eighth piece back among them.

The rain eased. A strip of coast emerged through it, broken by trees. Another boat lay farther out with its sail dark against the clearing sky. We approached shallow water slowly enough for the older crewman to use a pole. The younger man tried to rise, but the master told him to remain seated.

“Who will carry the fish?” he asked.

“We have hands,” the woman said. She put a folded mat beneath his elbow.

A man came into the water to take our line. Beneath a roof of woven leaves stood a receiving board. The woman brought her wax there herself.

“Eight loaded,” she said. “Seven here. One came loose when we freed a man's hand.”

“Did it go overboard?” the receiver asked.

“I did not see.”

He marked seven accepted and one missing. The brother gave the same account. Neither named me as the cause. I carried the surviving pieces only when the woman told me to, one at a time, while she watched.

The younger man was helped ashore. Someone knew a woman beyond the fish sheds who tended injuries. He would go to her; no one could say what would become of his wrist.

When the work was done, the brother handed Sulad and me a packet of food each.

“The work we agreed,” he said.

I looked toward his sister.

“The wax?”

“You worked. She lost wax. Those are both true.”

The woman did not look up from the board. I took my packet. It was warm through the leaf.

Sulad held our jar at an angle. A little brown water slid along its bottom. We found a seller by the sheds, who smelled the mouth and made us wash and rinse it before he would fill it. He named a price in food. Sulad offered half his packet; the seller refused. The smaller measure would not last two people if we left that afternoon.

Sulad held his food away from me.

“You may keep yours,” he said. “I chose to empty the jar.”

“We both need water.”

He watched while I put my packet beside his. The seller took both and filled the jar below its chipped rim. We had not eaten since the receiving creek. Now we had water, and no food.

The wax woman came from the shelter carrying her board.

“Seven accepted,” she said. “The eighth will be taken from my payment.”

“Will you say I owe it?” I asked.

“I will say you held the basket when I told you, and moved when I told you. I lost a piece while we freed his hand. I cannot say more.”

“Would you tell another carrier we can work?”

“No. I have seen you on one crossing. I do not know what you will do on another.”

She went back to the shelter. The young man had already been taken beyond the sheds. I could still see the place where he had sat, his uninjured hand curled over the other wrist.

Boats lay at different distances from the shore. One was taking on baskets; another had its covering tied down. I could hear men calling to one another, but not enough to learn where any of them were bound.

Sulad lifted the jar. The chipped rim rested against his palm.

“Which one do we ask?” he said.

I pointed to the boat being loaded. We went together, carrying water bought with the last food either of us had earned.
