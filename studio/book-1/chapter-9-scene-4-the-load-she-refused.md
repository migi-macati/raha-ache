# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 4: The Load She Refused (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 3B at the unnamed second landing. The sick crewman's care is arranged only through the following morning. This scene tests the boat leader's authority over cargo and Ache's temptation to promise Sulad's labor. No berth, route, fare, departure or storm is established.*

The crewman was still shaking the jar when a man came down the path with two baskets on a pole.

He stopped beside the boat and looked at me as if I were the person he had been sent to find.

“Where do these go?”

The woman stepped between us. “Who sent them?”

He named a trader from farther up the bank. She knew the name. I could tell by the way she looked at the baskets before she looked at the man again.

“I told him what I would carry.”

“He says these are part of it.”

“Then he should have brought them when we counted.”

The man set down his pole. The baskets were bound shut with a cord that passed beneath their bases. One leaned against the other, and I saw the pole bend when he lifted the end to free it.

“There's payment for them,” he said.

“Payment does not make my boat longer.”

He laughed, expecting her to laugh with him. She did not.

I moved closer. One of the baskets was smaller than the bundles we had carried that morning. It looked as though I could lift it easily. I had learned to distrust how things looked when they were standing on dry ground.

“Could we put one beside the jars?” I asked.

The woman turned. “Have I asked you to load anything?”

“No.”

“Then leave it there.”

The man with the baskets looked from her to me. “You have another hand now.”

“I have someone who has tied a cord,” she said. “He has not made a crossing with us.”

“He can carry a basket.”

“That is not the same work.”

Her crewman climbed out to examine the new load. He lifted one basket by its binding, then put it down with a short breath. When he pressed the lid, something hard shifted inside.

“Two of these would take the place we cleared,” he said.

The woman looked toward the path. I knew which place he meant. The sick man had gone up it between two people who could scarcely hold him upright. He had not wanted to leave the boat. His belongings had gone with him.

“Then we won't take two,” she said.

“One?” the basket carrier asked.

She had already begun to shake her head, but her crewman touched the smaller basket.

“We might take this one if we move the jars.”

“You told me those jars had to be here,” I said before I could stop myself.

The crewman straightened. “I told you to tie them. I did not ask for your judgment.”

The woman looked at the fastening I had made. It still held. I wanted her to say that my work had been good enough to give me a voice.

Instead she asked the crewman, “Where would you put the jars?”

He pointed to the narrow space beyond the crosspiece.

“And who would reach them if the line caught?”

He bent to show her. His shoulder struck the gunwale before his hand reached the nearer jar. He tried from the other side and found the basket carrier standing where he needed to step.

The woman waited until he moved away.

“We keep them where they are,” she said.

The basket carrier began to retie his load. “I'll tell him you refused the payment.”

“Tell him you brought it after we counted. Tell him I refused the weight.”

He pulled the cord hard enough that one basket scraped against the other. I helped him lift the pole when he was ready. He did not thank me. He set his shoulder beneath it and went back up the path.

The woman watched him go. Then she turned to me.

“You wanted the basket aboard.”

“I thought there was room.”

“You wanted a place for yourself. You thought another basket might buy it.”

I looked at the water beneath the hull.

“I thought I could help carry it.”

“You could. And if it came loose when we turned, you could try to catch it. There are many things a man can try once he has made someone else's boat unsafe.”

Her crewman was watching us. I wanted to tell her that she had refused the baskets herself, that nothing had happened. She was waiting for an answer to something I had already done.

“I spoke before I knew what you were carrying,” I said.

“Yes.”

She walked toward the bow, where the other crewman had been working the line. He showed her a frayed place and asked whether they should cut past it. She told him to bring the sound end to her before he took a blade to anything.

Sulad came over with our jar. He had stayed by the rack through the argument.

“You were going to offer us both,” he said.

“I hadn't.”

“You were looking at me when you said there was room.”

I had been. I remembered the words forming before the woman stopped me: *We can carry it for you.* It would have been an easy thing to say. Sulad had carried the mats without letting them touch the mud. He could do the work. I had no right to make it his.

“I should have asked you,” I said.

“You should have asked her what she needed first.”

He set the jar down where the shade still reached it. The water inside made a low sound against the clay.

“Would you take this boat?” I asked.

“I don't know where it goes.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then ask that before you ask what I will do aboard.”

The woman called for me. I went back, leaving Sulad with the jar.

She had laid the sound end of the bowline across the gunwale.

“Show me where you would make this fast,” she said.

I looked at the worn wood, the wet line, and the place where her crewman stood waiting. This time I did not touch either until she showed me which part of the boat the line was meant to hold.
