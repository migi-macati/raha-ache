# Encroachment

*Studio second-pass draft, 9 October 2026. Fictional reconstruction; not approved for reader-facing publication.*

At first light Sulad went to help mend the fishing stakes. I went looking for the Tondo clerk.

The night before, I had found crew marks beside cargo on the Tondo clerk's board. I wanted to know who might use those marks to demand a man's labor. The clerk had no answer ready when an empty cargo boat approached the landing, riding high at the bow, its crew arguing over which line to throw. Our steward rose to help. The Tondo clerk kept his thumb over the marks he had cut beside yesterday's cargo.

I could have ordered him to lift it.

Instead I asked, “The woman who came to Tondo last month looking for her missing husband. Would you tell her where to find him?”

“I would tell her what I knew.”

“And if I were a man who said her husband owed me two days at the paddle?”

The clerk looked at me. His thumb stayed on the marks.

“I would ask why you needed to know.”

“And if the man asking were a friend of your lord?”

He shifted his thumb, revealing the entire row.

“I would still ask,” he said, a little too late.

“You would ask a raha to explain himself?”

“If you were looking for a missing man, my lord, I would hope you could.”

I did not like his answer. I liked even less that I could not find anything wrong with it.

The boat came in sideways. Its bowline tightened around the post, and a rower jumped ashore before the hull could strike the baskets. The current pulled the stern out again. The man on the line shouted for another hand.

A woman in a faded red wrap stood over baskets stacked on the bank. She tested a binding, set the basket aside and waved the rowers back before they could lift anything.

“That one stays here,” she said. “And keep the boat off the stones. None of the others goes aboard until we agree what this trip costs.”

A rower let go of the carrying pole and took the line instead. Another drew the stern back toward the landing with a short paddle stroke. The empty boat shuddered but held.

I had been studying the tally board. She had been watching who would carry her goods.

“Who commands that boat?” I asked.

She heard me. “When it is mine, I do.”

The clerk's mouth moved. I suspected he was suppressing a smile.

The woman crossed to us. She smelled of sun-warmed rope and dried fish. A narrow strip of woven cloth was tucked beneath her belt, the end darkened by use.

“Are you the young raha?”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps you can settle something before these men carry away my baskets.”

That sounded promising.

I stood.

“I can hear you.”

“I need you to listen first.”

I sat down again.

* * *

Her name was Sima. Her household had hired rowers from Tondo to carry baskets from the landing by small boat to a larger trading vessel lying farther toward the bay. Her own people could move the baskets along the landing, but not all of them could leave their other work to row. She had paid for the first transfer.

The man who had arranged the crew said she had promised another.

“I promised to speak to him again,” Sima told me. “That is not the same thing.”

The organizer was standing beside a pile of coiled lines. He was a broad man with a split nail on one thumb and the patience of someone accustomed to explaining the same thing to people who had not been listening.

“My rowers ate the food I set aside,” he said. “I sent another boat away because I had promised these men to her. If she does not need them, I should have been told.”

“Did she promise you another crossing?” I asked.

“She asked whether they could come again.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” he said. “It is what she asked.”

Sima folded her arms.

“I asked whether they could come again *if* I needed them.”

The organizer pointed to the baskets. “And here are the goods.”

“Goods are not promises,” she said.

A rower near the water laughed and then pretended to cough.

“Ask him what he promised us,” he said, nodding at the organizer.

The organizer frowned. “You ate at my hearth. You know why I kept you.”

“You are my mother's brother,” the rower said. “I will take your boat when you call. But I was told we would row once. If you promise my arms again, I should hear it from you.”

I had assumed the organizer hired his men by the voyage. At least one of them owed him more than a day's work, and still did not know what he had been promised to do.

I turned to the Tondo clerk.

“Your board. Does it show her crew?”

“It shows the men who went aboard yesterday.”

“Does it show what they agreed to do today?”

“No.”

“Then why are we arguing?”

Sima looked at me with the expression my mother wore when I had missed something obvious.

“Because he has fed his men,” she said, “and I still need a way to move my goods.”

A carrying pole slipped against a basket's binding. Sima caught the load before it fell, then made the younger porter set it down and retie the cord. The rower at the bow shouted that he could not hold the boat against the landing much longer. Sima told him to ease it back into the current until they were ready. She would rather make the vessel wait than lose her goods.

I glanced toward the water. Two small boats were taking turns alongside the trading vessel. Their crews knew when to wait and when to move. The larger ship did not pause its work because Maynila and Tondo disagreed over whose men had been promised.

The organizer said, “I can still send them. I have not refused.”

“Then send them for today's work,” I said, “and let yesterday be finished.”

Sima shook her head.

“Not until we know what he will ask me to pay.”

“I ask for what I lost by holding them,” the organizer said.

“You ask for a voyage I did not buy.”

The argument had become a knot with two ends, and I could see neither.

* * *

I thought of the men collecting payments in both our names. Of the marks cut beside cargo that could become marks beside people. Of the man across the river who called himself my kinsman while sending clerks to count Maynila's boats.

“You cannot claim her rowers merely because you fed them,” I said.

“I have claimed no one.”

“You have held them against a promise she denies making.”

“I have held *my* crew ready.”

“Ready to serve whom?”

“Whoever pays for their work.”

“Then let her choose someone else.”

He looked at Sima.

“She may. She always could.”

I had raised my voice. Men waiting to load baskets had stopped to watch. The Tondo clerk stood beside his board, no longer smiling.

“Then why,” I demanded, “does your clerk mark down the men who row for us?”

The organizer followed my gaze.

“Ask him. I do not cut his boards.”

“That is convenient.”

He straightened. “You have called me a liar in front of the men I pay.”

“I have called this an attempt to take service that was never promised.”

Sima stepped between us.

“No,” she said.

For a moment I thought she meant the organizer.

Then she turned to me.

“I did not ask you to say that.”

“He is asking you to pay for a journey you never ordered,” I said.

“You are answering him for me,” she said.

Her words reached farther than my accusation had. A porter at the landing looked away. The organizer called his rowers over and told them to take another assignment. Two climbed into the boat. His sister's son stayed by the bowline while they took up their paddles.

“You have not paid us for yesterday,” he said.

“I paid him,” Sima replied, looking at the organizer.

“Then he owes us,” the rower said.

The organizer looked back at him. “Come.”

The young man hesitated, then slipped the line from the post and stepped aboard. He pushed the bow clear with his paddle. The other rowers turned the boat into the current, and the organizer walked after them along the bank.

Sima watched them go. She did not call them back.

I could feel the eyes of the landing on me.

“You said you needed help,” I told her.

“I asked you to listen.”

She turned back to the baskets. Her younger relative was securing them again, although some had already been loosened for carrying.

“I will speak for my own household,” she said. “You may hear us. You may judge what is brought before you. But do not put words in my mouth again.”

* * *

My mother arrived while the work continued. She touched one of Sima's baskets, testing its binding, and then stopped beside our steward.

“I asked you to compare the boats,” she said. “Did I ask you to put every rower on the board?”

“You wanted to know when the same men crossed twice.”

“I wanted to know when the same *boats* paid twice.”

He looked at the Tondo clerk. Neither answered.

My mother rubbed the bridge of her nose. “I should have asked what else you were counting.”

Our steward sent for the organizer. He returned without his crew, still angry enough to make his answers short.

She listened standing beneath the shade, where the two boards lay side by side. Sima spoke first. The organizer answered. Two porters who had waited near the baskets came forward. One had seen food brought to the waiting crew; the other said he had heard Sima ask whether the crew might return.

“Did you hear her promise payment?” my mother asked.

The man scratched his jaw.

“I heard her say she would know after the baskets were counted.”

My mother looked at the organizer.

“You were wise to keep your men ready. You were unwise to call their readiness an agreement.”

“If I let them go hungry while you hear every quarrel, will your household feed them?” the organizer asked.

“No,” my mother said. “I cannot promise that.”

He lowered his eyes, but not before I saw his anger.

“And you,” she said to me, “have made a quarrel between two households into a quarrel between shores.”

“I wanted to prevent a claim based on the board.”

“Then you should have asked what the board proved.”

The Tondo clerk spoke without being invited.

“It proves who boarded the boat. It does not prove who owes another voyage.”

My mother nodded.

“Then let that be said before those who heard the first bargain. Let neither side demand a second transfer on this board alone. If the organizer has spent food and turned away other work, let them hear his claim for that cost separately.”

Sima considered this.

“I will pay for work done,” she said. “I will not pay for words I did not speak.”

“And I will not hold crews idle for a Maynila household again,” the organizer said, “unless someone stands witness to the terms.”

He was entitled to say it. That was what troubled me.

A merchant waiting beside his own baskets caught the organizer's sleeve before he left. He asked whether two of the same rowers could take his cargo the next morning. The organizer said he would answer him in Tondo.

"Then I'll bring the baskets to Tondo's landing at first light," the merchant said. "Tell me there whether your men can take them."

The organizer agreed to meet him, but made no promise about the crew.

No one had ordered him across the river. He had chosen where he expected to find men.

"You heard him," I said to my mother. "Tomorrow's work is going to Tondo. Will you let them keep taking crews from our landing?"

"I will keep the two boards until the season ends," she said. "The marks will show who traveled, not who owes another voyage. And I will not forbid Tondo's men from taking work here."

"Then they will bring their own terms."

"They already do. If I send their crews away, the cargo may follow them. Which of our boats will take tomorrow's baskets? Whose food will keep the rowers here while they wait?"

I looked toward the fishing stakes Sulad had carried away. "We can find men."

"Find them first. Find what they will eat. I cannot promise a boat with someone else's arms."

"You would leave this shore in their hands?"

"I would keep a shore where boats still come."

She beckoned to our steward. "Go to Tondo's landing tomorrow. Ask what it would take to keep those crews working from our shore. Bring me their terms before you agree to anything."

He glanced at me before he bowed. My mother had sent me to speak for her before. Tomorrow she was sending him.

She turned to Sima.

“Would you accept another crew if we could provide one?”

“Today?”

“If the men are willing.”

Sima looked toward the water.

“I would accept a fair price. From either shore.”

There it was again. Either shore.

I went to find Sulad.

He and his uncle were setting the new stakes where the fishing nets had been damaged. His uncle braced one in the shallows while Sulad worked another into place. More lay on the bank than I had expected.

“Sima still needs a crew,” I said. “Could you take her baskets out?”

“When we finish this, there is the net.”

“I could help.”

Sulad looked at the unplanted stakes. “Then hold that one.”

I took his place. The pole slid in my hands when the water pressed against it. His uncle caught it before it struck the next stake.

“Not there,” the man said, and showed me where to brace my foot.

For a while no one spoke. Sulad set the stakes and his uncle worked them into the bed. The work was slower than the boats passing downriver.

“If I stay,” I asked, “can you go?”

“If you can mend the net as well.”

I looked at the torn mesh lying on the bank. I could not have finished it before dark.

I helped them set the stake I was holding. Another boat passed the landing, carrying baskets toward the ship.

When I returned, my mother was still with the clerks. She saw the mud on my hands and said nothing.

* * *

By late afternoon the two clerks had agreed how to record the disputed crew. Their marks would say who had traveled. If anyone claimed more service, the people concerned would have to be heard.

The seven boats from the day before had not paid twice. The boats arriving now would not pay twice either.

The arrangement still worked.

Across the landing, Sima's baskets waited beneath a covering. She was speaking to another boat owner. When I approached, she stopped speaking.

“I spoke for you,” I said. “I was wrong.”

“Yes,” Sima said. She kept her eyes on the boat owner waiting beside her.

“What can I do?”

“Tell the men what those marks prove. And what they don't.”

“I will.”

She turned back to the boat owner.

“Two baskets first,” she told him. “I will pay for those when they reach the ship. The others wait until your men return.”

“And if they do not return?” he asked.

“Then I still have four baskets to sell,” she said. “You still have a boat to fill.”

He laughed and reached for the carrying pole. Sima took hold of the other end herself.

I stayed long enough to understand that I had not been invited into their bargain.

Beyond them, a crew from Tondo was loading another vessel. Their organizer had found work for his men. Our own steward was still counting the boats. The river carried all of them.

I found my mother beside the tally boards. Across the water, the Tondo crew had already taken another load. On our shore Sima was helping the new boatmen lift the first of her baskets.

The Tondo clerk raised his knife over the wood.

“Shall I mark the men?” he asked.

My mother looked at me. I could have answered. The clerk was waiting for someone important to tell him what to do.

“Ask the men,” I said.

He went down to the landing. Sima saw him coming and set down her end of the carrying pole.

I could not hear her answer over the water. I could see that she made him wait.
