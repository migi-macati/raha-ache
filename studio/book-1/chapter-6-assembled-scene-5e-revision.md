# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 5E: Two Fares (revised assembly candidate)

*Studio-only replacement for the original Scene 5E, not an additional scene. Follows consolidated Scene 5D and precedes Scene 5F-1. Ache narrates. Fares, dialogue, cargo and gold exchange are fictional.*

My mother was mending a torn cloth when I returned. Three stitches crossed the edge at different angles. She had taken out the fourth.

“Sulad wants to go,” I said.

She drew the needle through. “Does his uncle know?”

“He told him himself. His cousin will help with the net, though they don't know whether he can come every morning.”

“Then his uncle is losing something he cannot count beforehand.”

“He has money of his own. Not enough for the fare.”

She put down the needle. I had brought her the trader's landing name, his conditions, and now another man's shortage. I had not brought an answer to what would happen after the first shore.

“Did Sulad ask you to pay?” she said.

“He asked me to tell you why I wanted to.”

“That's different.”

I sat opposite her. The cloth lay between us, with the torn edge turned inward.

“The trader's buyer has taken some of his jars. There may be room for two when the rest is aboard. He will not take payment until he knows.”

“Good. He understands his own cargo.”

“I need the other piece.”

She watched me for a moment. Then she opened the box she had shown me before. My father's bent gold loop lay on its cord. She left it there and unwrapped the smaller piece beside it.

“When your father went away, he said he would try to return before the worst rains,” she said.

“You told me he promised.”

“I told everyone. It was easier than telling them I did not know.”

I had come ready to bargain. I had no answer for that.

She held out the gold. “This is mine to give. The grain belongs to households that have already counted on it. Bulkan belongs to his work here. Do not make either of them your fare.”

I took it, keeping it separate from the first piece in my cloth.

“If there is no room, bring it back,” she said. “If there is room, tell Sulad what you are offering before you pay. He may refuse it.”

“He knows I cannot buy his decision.”

“Knowing and remembering are different things.”

“Will you come to the landing?”

“The organizer's accounts are due. Men are waiting to hear whether we will call their old wages paid merely because they have found new work.”

“You could come after.”

“I could. And they would wait longer.”

She returned to the cloth. I watched her make one careful stitch. She had not forbidden me to go. I wanted her to leave the room anyway.

Sulad was where I had left him, beside the trader's baskets. He had kept his bundle closed. I showed him the two pieces of gold.

“My mother gave me the second,” I said. “She wants you to know what I offer.”

“Your mother's gold, then.”

“Mine to spend now. You can keep your silver if you need it.”

“I said I would pay my share.” He unfolded his cloth and counted the coins. “If you pay the rest, I will know you paid it. I will not owe you my next answer.”

“You don't.”

“Tell the trader.”

The trader was testing the binding of a jar. He let us wait until the crewman had lifted it safely. Only then did he turn to the gold and silver on Sulad's cloth.

“Two fares,” Sulad said. “To your first landing. We bring our own food and water. If you want us to work your buyer's cargo, we bargain for that work separately.”

“You will carry your own jars,” the trader said. “You will move when my men tell you to move.”

“That is not carrying your cargo.”

“No.” He examined the gold, then asked whether my mother could demand it back.

“She gave it to me,” I said.

“And if her men come here saying otherwise?”

“They won't.”

I had meant to sound certain. I remembered what she had told me about my father, and wished I had answered more carefully.

The trader looked toward the space left by the sold jars. “There is room now. I will take the agreed fare. Nothing beyond the landing.”

Sulad counted his silver onto the cloth. I added the gold required to make up the price. The trader counted both in front of us and took them. He gave back what he had not charged. I tied the remainder away; it felt much smaller than it had in my mother's room.

“Bring your food before dawn,” he said. “If the water and the crew allow, we leave at first light. If they do not, you wait. I will not hurry men because you have paid.”

Sulad agreed. I agreed after him.

We walked away from the baskets together. I had paid for a place that could take me only as far as a buyer's landing. Sulad had paid for the same place with his own silver and a share of my mother's gold. Neither of us had paid anyone to carry us farther.
