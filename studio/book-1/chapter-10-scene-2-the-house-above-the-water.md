# Book I, Chapter 10 — Scene 2: The House Above the Water

*Studio prose draft. Follows Scene 1, “A Name Given at the Anchorage.” The cloth buyer, voyage, carrier, landing keeper and dialogue are fictional. The Brunei River's houses raised above tidal water are broadly consistent with Pigafetta's 1521 description, but that account is later than this imagined arrival and does not verify these people, their procedures, this vessel, or Ache's route. Source locator: https://www.loc.gov/item/2021667606/ . Do not treat the passage as a documented itinerary.*

We slept on the hard ground above the fish sheds. Sulad kept our jar between us. Neither of us slept much.

The cloth buyer came the next morning. The fish woman told him my name in front of me, just as she had promised.

He did not bow. He looked at the salt dried on my sleeves and asked whether I could keep rain off a bundle without cutting the cord that held it.

“I have done that work,” I said.

“Did the bundle arrive dry?”

“One did. The other was counted separately.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It arrived.”

He turned to Sulad. “And you?”

“I have carried water and emptied it from a boat. I have carried goods ashore.”

“Were you paid?”

“Yes.”

The man looked between us as if the answers were more useful than my grandfather's name. He had cloth to send with a carrier bound for Brunei. The carrier needed hands to shift the loads at the landing and watch them aboard. We would eat with the crew. There was no silver, no promise of an introduction, and no place reserved for us after the goods were delivered.

Sulad asked where we would sleep during the crossing. The man answered him. I listened. When the master came to inspect the cloth, he repeated the terms to each of us and heard each of us agree.

I wanted to tell the fish woman that I had found a way forward. She was cleaning a knife when we left. She looked up, saw our jar, and said, “Keep that upright.”

That was all.

For the first part of the voyage I was glad to be among people who had no reason to speak of my mother. The master gave me a place beside the cloth, where I could call if water reached the outer wrapping. The bundles had been tied in pairs, their knots left visible so that the receiver could count them without cutting them open. When spray struck the side, I watched it run down the covering and away from the cargo.

I had learned to watch a fold. I had not learned to make the wind stop.

Sulad carried water to the men at work and kept our drinking jar clear of their feet. At night, when the men had finished eating, he sat where the hull made a shallow shelter from the breeze. I sat beside him. He did not ask whether the cloth buyer believed me.

On another day a crewman did.

“Your grandfather is a king?” he asked.

“That is what I came to find out how to say to him.”

The man laughed. “You should know how to say it before you leave home.”

“I know who he is.”

“Then why are you working beside his cloth?”

“It is not his cloth.”

The crewman pulled a loose strand from the mat beneath him and rolled it between his fingers.

“Someone at the anchorage said a prince was going aboard,” he said. “I thought we had missed him.”

I wanted to answer as I would have answered a man in my mother's hall. There was no hall here, and the master was listening from the stern.

“Then you have found him,” I said.

The crewman looked at the cord I had been told to watch.

“Keep an eye on that, prince.”

I did. The next time the covering shifted I called the man responsible for it. He put it back without looking at me. The crewman who had laughed said nothing more.

We passed banks where the trees grew close to the water, and stretches where I could see nothing but a low line of land. I learned the sounds that meant a man was taking in a line, and the sounds that meant I should move out of his way. When I tried to help with a heavy bundle without being asked, the master told me to put it down.

“You know where it belongs,” he said. “You do not know what is beneath it.”

I waited until the man assigned to it lifted the other end. We moved it together. My palms burned afterward. I kept the skin from tearing by doing less with my hands and more with my eyes.

One evening Sulad told me he had spoken to a man who had been to Brunei before.

“What did he say?”

“That goods go to one landing and people to another. It depends on whom you know.”

“Does he know anyone near Siripada?”

“He knows a man who receives jars.”

“That is not near enough.”

“It is nearer than anyone we knew yesterday.”

I thought of the fish woman saying my name slowly so she would not give it to the wrong man. It had reached a crewman before it reached anyone who could help me. I had been afraid that no one would hear it. Now I wondered how many people had.

When the master first pointed ahead and called the place Brunei, I was crouching over the cloth. I had expected to stand for that moment.

Houses rose above the water on posts. Between them lay narrow spaces where boats moved in and out. Farther along the bank, roofs stood among trees on higher ground. I could hear voices from a landing before I could distinguish any face. A small boat crossed in front of us carrying baskets covered with leaves. Its paddler shouted at our master, who answered and changed our course a little.

There was no single gate. I had imagined one because I had imagined a single house.

The master brought us alongside a platform where men were already waiting for the cloth. One of them held a board on his knees. He counted the pairs as they came ashore and had the knots shown to him before each bundle was carried under cover. The master counted with him. Neither accepted my count until the last bundle lay on the planks.

Sulad helped carry the empty water vessels. When the men were paid in food, he received his share and I received mine. We had completed the work that brought us here. The master was finished with us.

“Who can take me to Siripada?” I asked him.

He pointed beyond the landing. “Ask the man who keeps the arrivals. I deliver cloth. I do not bring petitioners to the ruler.”

The man he indicated sat beneath an awning with two others. People approached him, spoke, and sometimes waited while he sent a boy along the bank. He was examining a piece of cord when I came forward. Sulad stood at my side with our jar.

“I have come from Maynila,” I said.

The man did not look up. “For trade?”

“To see Siripada.”

“Who sends you?”

I gave him my mother's name and the house she governed. He set down the cord.

“Who carries her word?”

“I do.”

“That is your word.”

“I am her son.”

“Who here can say so?”

I turned toward Sulad. He met my eyes and then faced the man.

“I came with him from her household,” he said. “I cannot speak for his grandfather.”

The man listened to that more carefully than he had listened to me.

“Has your mother sent a message ahead?” he asked.

“I do not know.”

“Have you brought one?”

“No.”

He spoke quietly to one of the boys beside him. The boy went toward the houses on the higher bank.

“You will wait,” the man told me. “I can send your name. I cannot decide whether the house will receive you.”

“And my companion?”

“Is he part of your household?”

I opened my mouth to say yes. Sulad had carried my jar, slept beside me, and stayed when a boat could have taken him back. But he had never agreed to become mine.

“He came by his own choice,” I said.

“Then he must say what he wants.”

Sulad shifted the jar into his other hand. “I want to remain here until he knows whether he is received. I can work for my food if there is work.”

The man pointed to the shade at the far end of the platform. “Wait there. Do not enter the houses beyond the landing without being called.”

We went where he pointed. I could see boats coming in beneath the posts, people passing goods through openings in the floors, and children calling to one another across the water. No one stopped to look at us. I had come far enough to see the place my mother had named, but the people in it still had their own business.

Sulad set our jar between his feet.

“Did you mean to call me your man?” he asked.

“For a moment.”

“Why didn't you?”

“Because you would have told him otherwise.”

“I would.”

I watched the boy who had carried my name disappear among the houses. I did not know whether he would find anyone willing to hear it. The water rose against the posts beneath us, touching each one in turn.

For the first time since leaving Maynila, there was no boat left for me to ask.
