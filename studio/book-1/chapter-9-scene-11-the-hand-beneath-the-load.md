By morning the mud had hardened around our footprints.

The receiving man had already brought out his board. He stood over us until we moved our jar from beneath the platform. A woman was waiting on the path with a basket on each side of a carrying pole. The baskets were covered with leaves, and the smell of dried fish came from a sack at her feet.

“You said there might be a man,” I told the receiver.

“There might have been.” He pointed to the woman. “Her brother has a boat. She has the goods.”

She heard him. “And I have a name,” she said, though she did not give it.

Sulad lifted the jar before I could reach for it. The woman looked at our hands, then at the space beneath the platform where we had slept.

“Can you carry without dropping what belongs to someone else?”

“We can carry what you show us,” Sulad said.

She set the pole down. One basket held small wrapped pieces that smelled of smoke and tree resin. The other was tied across its mouth. She opened neither for us.

“Wax,” she said. “The fish is his. The wax is mine. They are not to be paid for as one load.”

Her brother arrived while we were helping take the baskets to the water. He had a broad scar on one shin and a voice so quiet that I twice asked him to repeat himself. His boat was lower than the one that had brought Daya’s cloth. I could see its planks, a mast, the gathered sail, and a little water shining between two floorboards. I could not see how far it would go before the next landing.

The woman told him that we wanted to travel.

“Where?” he asked.

“Toward Brunei,” I said.

He looked at the receiving man, who was watching from the platform. “My goods go to an anchorage. Ask again there.”

He had room for us if we helped. I was to sit near the covered wax and call when the lashings shifted. Sulad was to pass water to the man who emptied it over the side. The woman showed us where our jar could stand. She made me put my hand on the cord around the wax basket, then took it away.

“You are watching,” she said. “If it loosens, call me. You have not been asked to mend it.”

The brother asked whether we would do that work for food on arrival. Sulad answered for himself. I did the same. There was no silver in the bargain and no promise beyond the anchorage.

We pushed away before the receiving man finished counting the next load.

For a while there was little to watch. The water was brown near the bank and darker where the channel widened. The woman kept her wax beneath a mat, raising one corner to see whether spray had reached the wrappings. Her brother stood aft with the steering paddle. A younger man worked near the sail. The other crewman showed Sulad how to pass him a vessel of water without leaning across his knees.

The woman caught me looking at the horizon.

“Watch what you were given,” she said.

The first drops fell before I could answer. They were large enough to make separate dark spots on the mat. The woman called to her brother. He told the man at the sail to bring it in. I watched their hands and could not tell which movement mattered most.

A line of rain crossed the water ahead of us. It was not yet over the boat. The brother turned the steering paddle, and the hull rolled enough to make the fish sack slide against the foot of the mast.

“Hold that,” the woman told the younger man.

He put his foot against the sack and reached for its binding. The woman bent to pull the wax mat farther down. I saw the cord at the far side of her basket lift from its place.

“It is moving,” I called.

“I see it.”

She reached across the basket. At the same moment the younger man lost his footing. He went down on one knee beside the fish. The sack moved again, this time over his wrist.

He cried out once. His other hand struck the floor.

I was nearest the basket. If I let go of its rim, it would slide toward him. If I held it, the woman could reach the binding, but the fish sack was still pressing on his hand.

“His hand,” I shouted.

The woman looked over her shoulder. “Keep that basket where it is.”

The rain reached us hard enough to hide the bank. I put both palms against the wax basket and felt it push back. The woman called to her brother. He left the steering paddle to the older crewman for a moment and came forward low, holding the side as he moved.

“Lift the fish,” he ordered.

I could not lift it while I held the wax. Sulad had been passing water aft. He set the vessel down and came toward the young man, but the older crewman called him back.

“Stay where I can see you!”

Sulad stopped. Then he took the vessel again and emptied the water gathering by the young man’s feet. It gave the brother a place to put his own foot without slipping.

The woman pulled at the wax binding. The basket shifted under my hands. She needed the cord from beneath it, and I was pressing the basket onto the cord.

“Let it move,” she said.

“The fish—”

“I know where the fish is. Move your hands.”

I took them away. The wax basket slid. She caught one handle, but the wet mat slipped from her other hand. A wrapped piece of wax rolled free and struck the side. I saw it pause against a rib of wood, then disappear into the water pouring across the floor.

The brother and the woman lifted the fish sack together. The younger man pulled his wrist out and held it against his chest. His fingers moved, though he would not let anyone touch them.

“Get down,” the brother told him. “You have no work standing.”

The woman turned on me. “What did you do with the wax?”

“I held it until you told me not to.”

“Did you see the piece go?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

I pointed to the place where it had vanished. The water there was already running out through the opening beside the floorboard, or so it seemed to me. I could not find the piece.

The woman knelt and counted what remained. The brother called for another vessel to empty water. Sulad passed his to the older crewman and took a smaller one from beneath the bench. He worked without looking at me.

The sail was down enough that it no longer struck above us. The boat still rolled. The brother had returned to the steering paddle. Rain beat against the mat, and the woman kept one hand over the exposed wax while she counted with the other.

“Seven,” she said. She counted again. “There were eight.”

The younger man sat with his injured wrist against his body. The fish sack lay where they had moved it, its binding wet but still closed.

I looked at the woman. “I saw it fall.”

“That does not tell me who pays for it.”

The older crewman called for more water to be passed. Sulad reached for the jar we had brought from the creek. He looked at me first, then at the small vessel filling too slowly in his hand.

“Our drinking water,” I said.

“It is a jar,” he answered.

He tipped it out before I could say more and passed it to the older man. The man filled it from the bottom of the boat and threw the water over the side. The jar came back empty. Sulad took it again.

I could see the bank only when the rain thinned. It was still there, low and dark, but farther away than I had thought. The brother did not turn toward it. He kept the boat's head where he wanted it and called for the water to be cleared.

The woman put the surviving wax back beneath the mat. She tied the cord herself. When she had finished, she looked at my hands.

“Keep them where I can see them,” she said.

I held them on my knees. Sulad passed the jar again, and the younger man watched his swollen wrist while the boat took another roll.

The woman had lost one piece of wax. I had seen where it went. No one had yet said whether that made the loss mine.
