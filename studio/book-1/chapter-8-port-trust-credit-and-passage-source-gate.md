# Book I, Chapter 8 — Port trust, credit and passage: source gate

**Research pass:** 18 October 2026 (Asia/Manila). **Status:** Studio research, not reader-facing historical fact or an approved plot event.

## Source inspected

Tina S. Clemente, “Chinese Trade in Pre-Spanish Philippines: Credit, Hostage and Raid Regimes,” *JATI* 17 (2012), 191–206, especially pp. 192–194, 197–198. Full publisher-hosted PDF inspected: https://ajap.um.edu.my/index.php/jati/article/download/5952/3665/13076 .

**What the article supports:** Clemente analyzes accounts attributed to Zhao Rugua (1225) and Wang Dayuan (1349), distinguishing Ma-yi's described barter-on-credit from San-su/San-tao's more guarded exchange. The latter account describes bargaining aboard ship and hostages during shore exchange; these are **specific reported arrangements**, not interchangeable universal Philippine customs (pp. 193–194). Clemente also discusses local distribution networks and chiefs' role in enforcing trade arrangements (pp. 193, 195–197).

**Geographic caution:** The article explicitly records scholarly disagreement over the identification/location of Ma-yi (p. 192–193). It cannot locate Ache's **unnamed** first landing or prove the institutions there. The article's game-theoretic payoffs are modern analytical models, not indigenous period terminology or evidence that every exchange used formal credit.

**Route source now checked directly (18 October 2026):** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (written 1512–1515), trans. and ed. Armando Cortesão, Hakluyt Society (1944), **vol. I, printed pp. 133–134** (McGill digitization, PDF pp. 263–264 when counting from 1): https://public-content.library.mcgill.ca/digitization/136385.pdf#page=263 . Under `[PHILIPPINES]`, Pires reports that the **Luções** carried merchandise to Borneo and then came to Malacca; he also describes Bornean purchases of gold and foodstuffs in Luções lands and exchange among surrounding islands. The preceding **Borneo** section (printed p. 133) describes Malacca–Borneo monsoon-dependent voyages; do not convert its month-long legs or Pires's approximate ten-day Luções–Borneo distance into Ache's sailing schedule. This confirms Clemente's secondary report at the level of **regional commercial contact**, not a documented Maynila-to-Brunei passenger itinerary, a direct voyage from Ache's unnamed landing, or proof that all Luzon vessels traded via Borneo. Pires's political/religious descriptions reflect an early Portuguese observer and cannot settle local governance or identities for the novel. The accessible 1944 English edition is a translated historical primary account, not an inspected Portuguese autograph.

## Narrative application / gates

- In Chapter 8, make trust and enforceability visible through a **named counterparty, identified goods/services, consent, timing, witnesses, and a plausible consequence for nonperformance**. These are design decisions informed by the source, not a reconstruction of one specific historical contract.
- If a buyer refuses deferred payment or requires security, give the refusal a concrete local reason. Do not import San-su hostage-taking into this unnamed fictional port merely because the source describes it elsewhere.
- The buyer's lead is not a ship's acceptance. Ache has spent his silver; he cannot pledge Sulad's work, Mulaw's name, or Nima's sister's cargo.
- A possible agreement may be for limited provisioning or a specific next leg, with the destination and weather uncertainty separately negotiated. Do not grant a guaranteed Brunei route.
- The Pires passage is now checked in Cortesão's translated edition; continue to distinguish his region-wide observations from fictional local practice. Before a detailed bargain, seek other primary accounts of payment, shipboard obligations and port mediation, and distinguish pre-1521 evidence from later colonial descriptions.
