# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 7: The Measure of a Hand (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 6 aboard the unnamed jar boat. The crew completes its one-leg delivery and pays the promised meals. Ache and Sulad begin seeking the next carrier; no onward berth, named port, Brunei arrival, or storm is established.*

The woman told me to look beneath the covering.

I had been watching the fastening on the nearest jar. I looked at her first, to be certain she meant me, then lifted the edge she pointed to.

“Where is the water going?”

A little had gathered along the folded cloth. It ran toward the bundles when the boat leaned, then back again when we came level.

“Under the jars,” I said.

“And where will it go when there is more?”

I could not see an opening. The covering had been drawn tight enough to keep spray off the bundles, but its lowest fold lay inside the boat.

“Tell him,” she said, pointing to the quiet crewman.

He came without asking why I had called. When I showed him the fold, he lifted it and drew it outward over the side. Water ran off in a narrow thread. He set a small weight on the cloth to keep it from falling back.

“Look again after we turn,” he said.

I watched the weight. The woman had not asked me to move it, and I was determined not to improve anything with my hands.

We kept close enough to the shore that I could see people moving between houses when there were houses to see. In other places the growth came down to the water, and I could not tell whether there was a path behind it. The men worked their poles where the bottom permitted. When the water grew deeper, they changed their work without asking me to join them. I knew less about their boat after watching them for an hour than I had believed I knew while it was tied to the post.

Sulad rested one hand on our jar. He had wrapped his cloth over its mouth again. Each time the hull moved beneath us, his hand moved with it.

“Do you think the woman will take us farther?” I asked.

“She said she would not.”

“She said she had not promised.”

“That is what I heard.”

He leaned forward and pointed with his chin. The weight on the covering had shifted. A corner of cloth was gathering water again.

I told the quiet man. He reached across me, set the fold right, and asked whether I had touched it.

“No.”

“Good. Then you saw what happened.”

I wanted him to sound more pleased. Instead he went back to the bow.

By the time we reached the landing, the woman had made us repeat the jar count twice. A man waiting beneath a roof of leaves came down with another man and a carrying frame. They looked at the boat before they greeted her.

“You have fewer hands,” the first man said.

“I have enough to bring your jars,” she answered. “Show me where you will receive them.”

He pointed to a raised floor a little way beyond the bank. The path to it was dry except where people had drawn water across it in buckets. The woman walked it herself before she ordered the unloading. She pressed her heel into the damp place and came back.

Sulad asked whether the jars were to go all the way to the raised floor.

“Yes,” she said. “That is where he will count them.”

“That is the work we agreed.”

She looked at him. “I remember.”

The quiet crewman loosened the rack. He lifted the first jar with me and took the heavier end. Sulad and the other man carried the next. We made two journeys, then a third. I could feel the jar's weight changing in my arms each time I tried to shift my grip, although nothing inside it moved. The man at the floor would not let us set one down until he had cleared the space where it belonged.

When the last jar stood in its place, the buyer bent to inspect the rims. He ran a finger over the clay beneath one binding, then asked who had tied it.

The woman answered before I could.

“My crew checked it. Is there damage?”

“No.”

“Then count.”

He counted. The quiet crewman counted after him. The numbers agreed. The buyer put a small mark on a piece of wood and sent one of his men for the payment.

I had expected the woman to smile. She looked toward the water instead. Her boat lay farther from the bank than it had when we arrived. The crewman had shifted its line so another vessel could come in behind us.

The buyer returned with the payment. He tried to speak about another load before she had finished counting it.

“Not today,” she said.

“You have a place for more now.”

“I have finished your delivery.”

He looked at me and Sulad. “You have new men.”

“For this delivery,” she said. “They have finished it too.”

She closed her hand over the payment. The buyer looked as though he wanted to argue, then told us to move away from the jars so his people could begin their own work.

We went down to the water.

The woman took out food wrapped in leaves and gave a packet to each of us. Sulad opened his first and inspected it as if he expected to find a different bargain inside. Mine was warm where the wrapping had lain against the side of the pot.

“You have both been fed for what you did,” she said. “There is no silver.”

“We agreed to food,” Sulad said.

“I know what we agreed.”

I looked at the packet in my hand. This was the meal for this delivery. It was not the food Sulad had shared with me at the first landing, and it was not the packet the mat carrier had paid us for unloading her mats. I had learned to count other people's jars more carefully than I counted what we had earned.

The woman sat on the bank to eat. Her men did the same. No one invited us to the boat. We had carried our jar ashore before the last of the cargo.

“Where do you go next?” I asked when she had finished.

“Back for work I know.”

“Do you know a boat going farther?”

“I know boats. That does not mean I know where each one will go tomorrow.”

“Who would know?”

She pointed to the vessels gathered beyond the landing, where the water was deep enough that their owners did not bring them against the bank. Men were passing small loads between one of them and a narrow boat. A woman standing beside the loads kept the count while two others carried.

“The people paying to fill those,” our boat leader said. “Ask them what they want carried before you ask where they sail.”

I looked toward the loads. There were more than I could count without going closer. Some were wrapped in cloth, others in woven coverings. I could not tell what lay inside any of them.

“Would you tell them we worked well?” I asked.

She considered me.

“I would tell them you watched a fastening and reported water beneath a covering. I would also tell them you caught a new rope under a jar rack and made us unload two jars to free it.”

“I did the carrying.”

“You did. That is what I can say.”

I had hoped for a shorter answer.

Sulad finished eating and folded the leaf so that nothing remained caught in it. He looked at the larger vessel, then at the woman.

“Would you take us back if no one will carry us onward?”

“Not on the terms we made today. I have no work for you on the return.”

“I did not ask you to.”

“No. You asked whether I would take you.”

He nodded. She looked down at her hands, then back toward her crew.

“If I have room, I might. But you would have to ask again.”

Sulad thanked her. He stood and picked up our jar.

The quiet crewman came from the boat carrying the short piece cut from the damaged line. He laid it beside the woman. She tested it with both hands and asked him whether the end would unravel. He said he would bind it before they left.

I had thought the new rope was the thing she would remember about me. She had already found another use for what remained of the old one.

At the far end of the bank, a man was arguing over a bundle too wide to pass between two others. The woman counting the loads refused to move either of the bundles already in place. One of the carriers stepped back, leaving the wide one on the ground.

I walked toward them before I could persuade myself that I should wait for Sulad.

The woman with the count looked up as I approached.

“Who sent you?”

“No one. I am looking for work aboard a boat.”

She pointed at the bundle on the ground. “Can you lift that?”

“With another hand.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“Not alone without dragging it.”

She turned to the carrier who had stepped away. “You see? Even a stranger can tell me its width.”

He began to protest. She made him wait while she measured the gap with her hands.

Sulad came to stand beside me. The woman looked from him to our jar.

“Are you together?”

“We travel together,” Sulad said. “We make our own agreements.”

“Then I will ask each of you separately when I have work to offer.”

She told the carrier to take the wide bundle back to the person who had wrapped it. It would have to be divided before it came aboard. The carrier went reluctantly.

I watched the woman mark her count again. I had not earned a place on her boat. I had only given her an answer she could use.

“What are you carrying?” I asked.

“Goods that have owners,” she said. “And people who will want them dry.”

“Where are they going?”

She looked at the vessel waiting in deeper water.

“Farther than this landing. Whether you go with them depends on what work there is, and whether the master will take another mouth.”

I wanted to tell her who I was. The words came so readily that I nearly spoke them.

Instead I asked when the master would return to the bank.

“When he has finished counting his own cargo,” she said. “If you are still here, I will point him out.”

Sulad set our jar in the shade. We sat beside it where we could watch the work without standing in anyone's path. Across the landing, the woman who had carried us was preparing to leave. Her crewman brought the old rope aboard, now bound at its cut end.

I had crossed another stretch of water. I had a meal inside me and no place on the next boat. The people who could take us farther were counting what they owned, and I had begun to understand why they did it before they counted us.
