# Book I, Chapter 5 — Scene 6: What I Could Give (Studio working prose)

By evening the fishing bank smelled of wet fiber and smoke. Nets hung from a frame to dry. A small fire burned beyond them, where Sulad's uncle was turning fish on a split stick.

Sulad saw me before his uncle did.

"You came back," he said.

"I said I would."

"You said you would bring food."

"I asked my mother."

"And?"

"She may pay for one crossing. If there is a crossing worth paying for."

His uncle turned the fish. "Then there is no work yet."

"No."

"Good. I can eat before anyone asks me to row."

He said it without anger. I wished he had sounded angry. It would have given me something to answer.

Sulad walked with me beyond the drying nets. The river had begun to darken, and lights were appearing on the opposite bank.

"I may leave Maynila," I said.

"May?"

"I am trying to find the captain we met upriver."

"The one who made you speak Malay like a child?"

"That one."

Sulad smiled, then stopped. "For Brunei?"

"To ask my grandfather for help."

"Help with Tondo?"

"Help enough that Tondo will have to listen."

"Did your mother send you?"

"She told me to find the captain first."

"That is not the same answer."

"No."

We stood where the tide had left a strip of damp sand. Behind us his uncle called for another piece of wood for the fire. Sulad did not move.

"Will you ask me to come?" he said.

I had meant to ask. I had practiced the words on the walk from Ula's house, words about trust and the dangers of traveling with strangers. I had not practiced what I would say if he asked first.

"Would you?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you want me there or want someone to do what you say."

I felt the sting of it. "I want you."

He looked away toward the boats.

"I mean I want you with me," I said.

"I know what you mean."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Then he picked up a pebble and rolled it between his fingers.

"My uncle needs the boat. The net is not finished. If I leave, someone else must do my share. You cannot promise him grain and call that consent."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I came here this morning and asked for three days of work as though you had nothing else to do. I know now."

He let the pebble fall.

"If there is a passage," he said, "I will speak to him myself. And I will decide whether to go."

"I would not stop you."

"You could make it difficult."

That was true. I had been taught to give orders so often that I sometimes heard a refusal as an insult before I heard it as an answer.

"I will not ask him in your name," I said.

"Good."

"Will you tell me what you decide?"

"When there is something to decide."

We walked back to the fire. His uncle had eaten one fish and left the other where it would stay warm.

"I have silver," I told him.

"So I have heard."

"Not much. Enough to pay for a message, perhaps a little more. If you carry one, I will pay you from my own things."

"After I hear where it must go."

"Yes."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will find someone else."

He took the fish from the stick and offered it to Sulad, who broke off a piece and handed the rest to me.

I had not expected to be fed by the household whose labor I had spent the day trying to borrow.

On the way home, I passed the landing. The steward had returned. He stood beside my mother with a board in his hands, his face pinched from a day on the water.

I stopped where they could not yet see me.

"The organizer will name the crews," the steward was saying. "He wants the merchants to come to him before they ask anyone else. He says he can find men quickly."

"And their pay?" my mother asked.

"He says that can be settled for each journey."

"The first crew has not been paid," she said.

"He says the woman paid him."

"That was not my question."

The steward lowered the board.

I waited for my mother to order him back across the river, or to accept the bargain because we had no better one. She did neither.

"Write down exactly what he asked," she said. "Tomorrow we will ask the merchants what they would pay for a crew of their own choosing."

I could have stepped forward and told her I had found another answer. I had not. I had paid for a question to be carried upriver, and the answer might never return.

I went inside and counted the silver I had left.

It was less than I had imagined when I first spoke of Brunei.

I set it aside anyway.

If a captain came, I would ask for passage. If he refused, I would have to find another way. I would not take Sulad's uncle's boat without his consent, or call the rower's unpaid wages a debt already settled, or tell my mother I had a fleet waiting beyond the sea.

I would go to Brunei if I could.

That was the decision I had. The voyage was still someone else's to grant.
