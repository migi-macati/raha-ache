# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 2: The Empty Place (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. The mat delivery is completed at the unnamed second landing. The carrier's separate food obligation is paid. A possible new voyage is introduced through a crew vacancy, not a guaranteed berth. No destination, historical port, storm, or pursuer is asserted.*

The man from the house returned carrying a coil of cord.

He was older than I had expected. The children who had been turning wood in the shallows followed him, each with a hand on the same long pole. He told them to leave it by the rack, then stopped when he saw the woman in the boat.

“You came before the water turned,” he said.

“You were meant to be here before I did.”

“I was told the upper path was open.”

“And was it?”

He looked at the mud on his feet. “Not where I went.”

The woman did not smile. She asked him to clear a dry place for the mats. He moved the cord from beneath the rack and sent one child for a board. The other child stood watching the bundles as though they might open by themselves.

“Can we carry them now?” I asked.

“When he has somewhere to put them,” the woman said.

The board came. The man laid it across two supports, tested it with his weight, and nodded. The woman went ashore first. She put her hand beneath the edge of the first bundle while I lifted the other end. Sulad took the second bundle with her brother. No one told him where to stand. He watched the brother's feet and kept his end above the mud.

The man unwrapped the first mat, pressed the binding at its corners, and counted the others without unfolding them. He made us turn one bundle so that the wet side of its covering faced out. Then he took the weight from my arms.

“Leave that one,” he said.

I had been waiting to put it down. I did not let go until his hands were under it.

The woman watched him check the second bundle. When he finished, she asked whether he would take responsibility for them now.

“I have them,” he said.

“That was not what I asked.”

He looked at the mats on the rack, then at the water below. “I agreed to take them. They are mine to keep dry.”

He took the payment he owed from his pouch and counted it into her hand. She counted it again, then made him repeat his promise to her brother. Only then did she tell us our work was finished.

My hands had begun to shake. I had carried less than I had at the first landing, but I had been holding my breath whenever the man's fingers went near a loose edge.

The woman reached beneath a folded cloth in her boat and took out a small leaf packet.

“This is for the lifting,” she said, putting it into my hand. “I said you would have food whether or not we sailed. I did not give it to you last night.”

I had nearly asked her for it twice. Now that she had remembered without being asked, I felt foolish for being pleased.

“Thank you.”

“Eat it before it spoils. That is the only advice that comes with it.”

I showed Sulad the packet. He looked at it, then at the water jar still secured beside the stern support.

“Take the jar first,” he said. “Before they want to move the boat.”

We untied it together. The cloth had tightened while it was wet, and my sore fingers could not free the last turn. Sulad pushed the knot toward me instead of pulling at it. I worked it loose, lifted the jar by its body, and stepped ashore. It was ours again, and the woman had no more reason to carry us.

Her brother was already preparing to leave. I had wanted to ask where they would go next, but he was working with a pole that had split near the grip. He cut away a splinter and tested the wood against the bank. I had nothing to offer him that he needed.

At the far end of the landing, men were dragging another boat toward the shallows. It rode unevenly, with one side lower than the other. A man inside passed a pot ashore. Another took out a bundle of cloth, then stopped and sat down hard against the gunwale.

“Is he hurt?” I asked.

Sulad followed my eyes.

“Ask him.”

We went closer. The seated man was breathing through his mouth. He held a cloth against his ribs, but I could see no blood. The others had stopped unloading. One was talking to a woman on the bank, and neither seemed to hear the man ask for water.

Sulad set down our jar.

“May I give him some?” he asked the woman.

She nodded without turning. Sulad poured into the man's cupped hands. He drank and passed the rest over his face.

“Can you stand?” Sulad asked.

“Not while they keep moving the boat.”

The woman came then and told him he was staying ashore. He protested that they needed hands to pull at the next narrow reach. She told him that a man who could not keep his feet beneath him would be another load to carry.

He looked away from her.

I had spent the morning thinking that a boat needed space for two more people. Here was space being made by someone who did not want to leave it.

“Where are you going?” I asked.

The woman studied me. “Not to wherever you are imagining.”

“I have not said where.”

“Then you have saved us both some time.”

One of the men laughed. The seated man did not.

I could have told her that I had carried jars and mats without breaking them. I had already learned how little that proved about a boat moving beyond sheltered water.

“Do you need another hand?” I asked.

“Perhaps. What can you do?”

I looked at the pole beside her boat. Its end was worn smooth where it had been worked against the bottom. I had held a pole before. I had never had to choose where to plant it with a loaded boat turning beneath me.

“I can lift,” I said. “I can keep a load from shifting if someone shows me how it is tied. I cannot take that man's place at a pole without being taught.”

She looked past me at Sulad.

“And you?”

“He is asking for himself,” Sulad said. “I have not asked you for a place.”

The woman glanced at the sick man. He had closed his eyes. His companions were making a place for him on the bank, where he would have shade when the sun climbed.

“Come back when he has been settled,” she told me. “I will know what work remains then.”

“Will there be room for us both?”

“I have not offered room for one.”

She turned back to her crew.

I went to where Sulad stood with the jar. He had kept enough water for us. I held out the food packet, and he took it while I adjusted the cloth beneath the jar.

“You could have said you knew the work,” he said.

“I don't.”

“You used to say it anyway.”

We sat beside the rack, away from the mats we no longer had the right to touch. Sulad opened the packet and gave me the larger piece. I gave part of it back. Across the landing, the sick man lay under a strip of shade while the others decided what could be taken aboard without him.

I could see the empty place in their boat. I could not yet tell what it would cost to fill it.
