# Book I, Chapter 7 — Cloth ownership and landing claims (source note)

**Status:** historical research and scene-design constraints, not public canon. **Checked:** 17 October 2026 (Asia/Manila). This is a focused supplement to `chapter-7-strangers-followers-research-and-scene-architecture.md`, not a substitute for it or for the unsaved Scene 3.

## What the sources support

**Women's textile work and commercial agency.** William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (Ateneo de Manila University Press, 1994), Visayas chapter 3, “Trades and Commerce,” describes spinning, weaving, sewing and the marketing of those products as activities undertaken by women, sometimes for hire. The same book's Visayas chapter 7, “Property and Labor,” notes that women sometimes pooled work on abaca and textiles and that manufactured goods could have joint owners. Thus the fictional Nima's claim that the damp cloth belongs to his sister is *socially plausible*, but Scott does **not** attest Nima, his sister, this cargo, or this unnamed landing. The sister must remain an owner with an independent claim, not a disposable reason for men to quarrel. Read against the printed 1994 edition before attaching page-specific footnotes to public prose.

**Limits on leaders' discretion.** Scott's Visayas “Social Organization” discussion connects datu authority to followers, maritime activity, obligations and access to resources; it does not authorize treating a datu as the owner of every follower's cargo or as able to override a separate local buyer or household. Do not import precise sixteenth-century Visayan status rules into an unnamed shore without identifying the community. A boat leader may negotiate, but Nima can refuse to risk another person's goods. The buyer and fisherman may defend already committed landing space. This is an invented dispute consistent with, not established by, the historical context.

**Vessel details.** Scott's 1982 *Philippine Studies* article, “Boat-Building and Seamanship in Classic Philippine Society,” is a verifiable scholarly source for period maritime research, but its landing mechanics and specific pages were **not** inspected here. Do not cite it as proof of the fictional poles, rope, damp covering, mooring post or crew hierarchy. The 1981 National Museum monograph of the same title is a distinct publication; avoid conflating their bibliographic records.

## Scene 3 continuity and dramatic implications

Scene 2 ends with Ache **following Sulad back up the path** after hearing the buyer begin to define terms. Scene 3 must show any return to the landing and account for elapsed light before an intervention there. The host has offered only conditional **one-night shelter under her eaves** in exchange for water and roof work; she has not offered storage, her rope, a mat, or future nights. Her roof was repaired in Scene 2, and the first two jars were delivered (Ache's jar was partly spilled). The visiting boat is still being held off by poles; no cargo has been landed and no mooring right has been granted.

A strong next reversal: Ache spots a genuine material risk and pays or promises something of **his own** without checking whose permission is needed. That expenditure cannot purchase Nima's sister's property, the host's shelter, or the buyer's landing. Sulad may object before or after the cost is incurred; either way, do not erase his dissent by giving Ache an instant reward. Let Nima's sister's absent ownership constrain Mulaw too. Avoid a modern property-rights speech; reveal the distinction through who may move, pay for, dry, sell or refuse to release the cloth.
