# Book I Chapter 5 — working prose (Studio draft, incomplete)

The merchant had brought his baskets down before the sun reached the roofs.

I knew them by the dark reed tied across the handles. Yesterday he had said he would take them to Tondo's landing. I had spent most of the night telling myself that he might change his mind.

He had not.

His porters carried the baskets past our tally boards and down to a small boat. There was no Tondo crew waiting for him. He would have to find one when he crossed, if he found one at all.

Our steward was going with them.

He had a clean cloth folded beneath one arm and a little bundle of food in his hand. My mother had sent him to learn what the Tondo organizer would ask for regular crews on our shore. The steward looked at the baskets, then at me, as though he expected me to be pleased that the work had begun.

"Will you agree to his price?" I asked.

"I will hear it."

"And if he asks to count every man who takes a paddle?"

"I will hear that too."

"You could say no."

"Then your mother would have sent someone who was not supposed to hear him."

He stepped into the boat. One of the porters pushed it clear with his pole. The merchant sat with the baskets at his feet, keeping his hand on the nearest binding.

The boat crossed toward Tondo, small and ordinary. No banner. No escort. Nothing I could point to and call an invasion.

I hated how easily it went.


A young rower stood farther along the bank, near the place where yesterday's crew had cast off without Sima's cargo. I recognized the organizer's sister's son. He had one hand wrapped around a paddle shaft, though there was no boat beside him.

His uncle came out from behind the store sheds.

"I have asked you twice," the rower said.

"I know."

"You told me she would pay."

"She paid me."

"Then give me my share."

His uncle looked toward the boats. "Not here."

"Where?"

"After the next work."

The young man held the paddle across his body. He did not raise it. For a moment I thought he might. Then he set the blade on the ground, hard enough to make the wet sand jump.

The organizer went past him without another word.

I could have called the man back. Yesterday I had tried to settle a quarrel before I understood it. Today I understood enough to know that Sima's payment and the rower's wages were different things.

I still wanted to call him back.

The young man saw me watching.

"Did your mother put that on her board?" he asked.

"No."

"Then perhaps she will not know it happened."

He lifted the paddle and walked toward the fish sheds. I had no answer that would have kept him there.

* * *

Bulkan was beside the damaged outrigger when I found him. He had one foot against the hull while a carpenter tested a new fastening. The carpenter pressed, listened to the wood, and pressed again.

"That boat will not be ready today," Bulkan said without looking up.

"I did not come for that boat."

"Good. I would have had to disappoint you."

"I want men at the landing."

"There are men at the landing."

"Men who answer to us."

Bulkan stood. He had been my teacher long enough to know when I was trying to turn a question into an order.


"How many?" he asked.

"Enough that Tondo's crews stop acting as though this is their shore."

He glanced toward the water. "How many is enough?"

"Two boats. A dozen men."

"Doing what?"

"Watching."

"Watching whom?"

"The men who come to take our work."

Bulkan ran his thumb along the edge of the fastening. The carpenter waited for him to move.

"Yesterday the rowers came because a household hired them," he said. "Would you have your men stop the next crew from coming?"

"Not if they are paid fairly."

"Who will decide that at the water?"

"I will."

"And when you are not there?"

I looked at the hull. A narrow strip of new wood had been fitted along the side, pale against the old planking. The carpenter had done good work. He was waiting to finish it.

"I want them to know we are not helpless," I said.

Bulkan moved his foot so the carpenter could work.

"I can put a boat near the landing," he said. "A man can tell us what he sees. If someone tries to take a vessel by force, that is another matter. But if your men stand there all day while the crews work, they will want food. They will want to know who sent them. And the first time a Tondo rower laughs at them, you will have to decide whether the laughter is worth a fight."

"I would decide."

"I believe you."

He said it so readily that I looked at him.

"I have seen you decide quickly," he continued. "I have also seen the boat afterward."

The carpenter struck the fastening into place. I felt the blow through the ground.

Bulkan had once made me repeat a turn until my hands blistered. I had cursed him for it and then, the next day, found myself making the turn correctly without thinking. I wanted him to be wrong now. I wanted him to be an old man who had forgotten what courage was for.

"Would you stand with me?" I asked.

"If your mother sends me."

"And if she will not?"

He looked at me for a long time.

"I would still come if someone were drowning."

That was not what I had asked.
