## Historical evidence, with strength and limits

1. **Visayan leadership and followers (historical comparison, not direct evidence for this invented encounter).** William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (Ateneo de Manila University Press, 1994), especially the Visayas social organization chapter, describes datu leadership as bound to kinship, personal followers, protection, prestige, and exchange. The timawa category in sixteenth-century Visayan accounts is not interchangeable with the Tagalog maharlika or a modern generic 'free commoner.' Do not transplant an exact later sixteenth-century Cebu structure wholesale to an unnamed early-sixteenth-century boat or community. ## Narrative problem and chosen architecture

**Chapter question:** Can Ache trust a stranger whose loyalty belongs first to his own people, while Sulad has every reason to refuse another uncertain bargain? The scene must test both parties' agency. The ally must be credible even if Ache never returns to help him.

**Working opening:** At the first landing's proposed lodging house, Ache learns that shelter has a price in food, money or labor. Sulad sees the cost before Ache does. A visiting boat with a mixed cargo and several independently minded crew arrives needing to make a repair or negotiate access to the landing. Its leader, provisionally Mulaw, has his own urgent obligation to those aboard. The local host or buyer has reasons to refuse his request; no one is a stock villain. The precise craft, repair, cargo and landing customs require source checking before prose.

**Scene beats (not yet prose):**
- 7A: Shelter request is conditional. Ache cannot purchase a royal exception; the household's occupant or steward has a credible reason to limit access. Sulad offers labor without surrendering his own decision.
- 7B: The visiting boat's problem is first visible as *someone else's* dispute. Mulaw appears with a specific follower who disagrees with him; show leadership as negotiation and obligation rather than universal obedience.
- 7C: **Scene 3A saved** at `96ac1450a0d0c416c878245955a1905ad7cefbc8`: Ache pays one extra pole-handler to steady the visiting boat. The agreed work is completed and his silver is spent, but Nima refuses to land his sister's cloth on the fisherman's ground. The host has refused storage. Ache and Sulad go uphill again. Neither unloading nor onward passage is authorized.
- 7D: **Saved as Scene 4, *The Words He Took Back*.** Sulad challenges Ache's assumption that he will carry baskets; Ache publicly withdraws the unauthorized promise before witnesses. The buyer then reopens a narrower mooring negotiation. There is no separate saved Scene 3B; do not require or invent one.
- 7E: Shared work under a bounded agreement solves the immediate material crisis; the host or buyer receives a meaningful concession. At least one participant remains displeased. Mulaw's followers choose to help for reasons visible in action.
- 7F: Ache and Sulad gain an introduction or *conditional* chance to seek the next leg, **not** a guaranteed vessel to Brunei. Mulaw keeps a conflicting priority that can reappear in Books II–III. Chapter 8 retains its distinct negotiation for supplies and future obligations.

**Character pressure:** Ache is observant but prematurely confident; he must be wrong in a way that inconveniences someone with less status. Sulad's dissent cannot vanish when Ache apologizes. Mulaw is funny or warm in a private moment yet capable of leaving strangers behind to protect his people. Give one follower a name, skill, fear and separate incentive, not a faceless entourage. The local host's caution should be rational.

**Tension with future history:** Readers may know Ache will reach Brunei, but not whether this particular household, follower, bargain or companion survives the encounter. Avoid a false threat that history already settles; build suspense around whose trust is lost, what debt survives and who pays.

## Constraints and next research

- Geography: the first landing is unnamed. Do not equate it with Mindoro, Calamianes or Palawan until a scene and evidence warrant it. A visiting Visayan boat is a fictional possibility, not an established route.
- Language: Ache's uneven Malay and Tagalog do not make him fluent in every Visayan language. Communication can be partial, interpreted or gestural; research vocabulary before embedding period terms.
- Historical framing: Scott's descriptions draw heavily on sixteenth-/seventeenth-century records and lexical sources. Mark backward projection and regional variation. No documentary proof for Ache meeting Mulaw.
- Literature: avoid “hero saves a boat, instantly earns warrior ally”; make cooperation reciprocal, conditional and costly. Do not turn the chapter into an exposition of social classes.
- Verify full texts of Scott's 1982 article and the relevant *Barangay* pages, research the period use of `lakan` versus `datu`, then specify a physically credible boat problem before drafting 7A. Keep Chapter 7 independent of Chapter 6 release timing and preserve the 30 core chapters + Book III bonus 11–12 architecture.
