# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 5: The Sound End (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 4 at the unnamed second landing. A provisional work bargain is discussed, not yet accepted by the full crew. No departure, named port, Brunei-bound ship, or weather crisis is established.*

The woman laid the line across the side of the boat and made me follow it with my eyes.

“What does it hold?”

“The bow.”

“When?”

“When you tie it to the post.”

“And when we are away from the post?”

I looked at the rope. A wet length lay under her foot. The other end passed around a piece of wood darkened by many turns of cord.

“You bring it in,” I said.

“Before it catches beneath us. Now show me where you would make it fast.”

I pointed to the timber I had seen her crewman use. She told me to put my hand there. The wood was smooth along the top and rough beneath. When I ran my fingers around it, I found a split near the underside.

“Is that where you want the rope?” she asked.

“It is where he put it before.”

“He did not ask you to remember him. I asked you to look.”

I took my hand away. The crewman came over and put two fingers in the split. He looked toward the woman, then back at the line.

“It held yesterday,” he said.

“Yesterday we had another man to watch it.”

“He is not dead.”

“I know. I paid for his supper.”

The crewman pulled the rope through his hands. He stopped at a pale place where the outer fibers had worn away. He had shown her that place earlier. I had watched him do it.

“We can cut here,” he said.

“Then what length do we have?”

He drew the sound portion out along the boat. It reached the post, but only if he leaned over the bow to take the turn.

The woman watched him measure it a second time.

“I will not have you hanging out there when the water takes the boat,” she said.

“There is more line beneath the covering.”

“That belongs with the goods.”

“It is rope.”

“It was counted with the goods.”

He let the worn length fall. I could hear someone at the landing beating mud from a mat. Farther up the path, the man with the refused baskets was arguing with another voice. I thought of how much light the woman had already spent saying no.

“Could I fetch a sound line?” I asked.

“From whom?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Then find out what it costs before you offer to bring it.”

The crewman turned away to ask at the next boat. She let him go. He had been working for her longer than I had been standing beside her, and I could see that he disliked being sent to bargain while I watched.

“Where do you go from here?” I asked.

“To the landing where these jars are wanted.”

“Is that near Brunei?”

“I have not been paid to go to Brunei.”

“Do boats leave from there?”

“Boats leave from every place where someone has reason to move something. Whether one will take you is another matter.”

I had hoped she would give the landing a name I knew. She gave me no name at all. Perhaps she had said it earlier to her crew. I had been listening for the wrong thing.

“Do you need someone to watch the jars?” I asked.

“I need someone who will notice when a fastening comes loose and tell me before he tries to mend it while we are moving.”

“I can do that.”

“You can say that. I have seen you tie one cord. I have not seen you keep your feet in a loaded boat.”

She stepped into the hull and pointed to the space where the sick man had sat. His bundle was gone, and so was the knife I had almost asked to borrow. There was no sign that he had been there except the bare plank.

“He was paid for work already done,” she said. “What he is owed remains his. If I take another hand for the next carrying, that hand earns only the next carrying.”

“I don't want his share.”

“Good. There isn't a share to give you.”

She showed me where I could sit without blocking the crewman's reach to the jars. I crouched there. The boat moved under me, though no one had touched it. I put out a hand and caught the crosspiece.

“You would watch the bindings,” she said. “You would lift when I tell you. You would not take a pole unless the man using it placed it in your hands and told you what to do. You would eat with us after the goods are received. No silver. No promise beyond that landing.”

“And if a jar breaks?”

“If you break it by doing something I forbade, you will answer to its owner. If the boat strikes a stone, I answer for the boat. I will not pretend the two are the same.”

It was more of a bargain than anyone had offered me since we left the first shore. I wanted to accept before she found another hand.

“Who else must agree?” I asked.

“The men who must make room for you. And the buyer must not send another load.”

Sulad had come close enough to hear the last part. He stood on the bank with our jar against his leg.

“What would you have me do?” he asked her.

She looked at his hands. The mark left by the mat bindings was still visible near his thumb.

“I have not asked you to do anything.”

“I know. I am asking.”

She pointed toward the bundles. “At the next landing they must be carried without dragging. I may need another pair of hands there. A man who cannot help while we are afloat still takes up room and water.”

“I can carry them. I won't say I can work a pole.”

“Would you take food for that?”

“Food for my work,” he said. “Not from his portion.”

She considered the bundles, then the place where he might sit.

“If the crew agrees to the weight, you may have the same meal for the unloading. If I cannot take two, I take neither on those terms. I will not have one of you boarding because he thinks the other has bought him a place.”

Sulad nodded. He did not look at me.

The crewman returned without a rope. The owner of the neighboring boat would lend one until the next landing, but wanted a length of new cord in return. The woman asked whether he had agreed to its condition. He said the owner had made him uncoil it and look.

She went to inspect it herself.

I stayed in the place she had shown me, though she had not told me to stay. The jars were still tied. The refused baskets were still ashore. Sulad had his own possible bargain, and neither of us had a place until the crew decided.

From the bank I heard the woman call the price of the borrowed line back to its owner. He called another price in answer. She told him he could keep his rope.

I rose to get out of the boat before anyone mistook my sitting there for an agreement. My foot caught the edge of the crosspiece. The nearest jar trembled against its binding.

I stopped it with my palm.

The woman saw me from the bank.

“Did it move?”

“Yes.”

“Then when you are ashore, tell the man who tied it. Don't make it tighter while he cannot see your hands.”

I stepped out and told him. He came to look, and the woman went on bargaining for the line.
