# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 9: The Place of the Second Parcel (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 8 at the unnamed delivery landing. The larger carrier completes its load and takes Ache and Sulad aboard under separate one-leg work arrangements. The master names an unnamed receiving landing, not a route to Brunei. This is a fictional vessel and voyage; its class, mapped bearing and sailing season are not established.*

The tally keeper finished her marks before she answered me.

“There is a receiving house beside a creek,” she said. “The man there will count Daya’s cloth. If he will not take it, the master must bring it back.”

“Does the creek have a name?”

“People who use it have one. You will not find your way there by knowing what they call it.”

“Is it on the way to Brunei?”

She held her board against her chest. “The cloth is going to its receiver. That is the way this boat is going.”

I wanted to ask again. I had asked the same question of three people who owned things they could afford to move, and each had told me only where those things belonged. I went back to our jar.

Sulad was washing the dust from his palms with a little water poured over the bank. He stopped before the jar was empty.

“Did she tell you?”

“She told me where the cloth is going.”

“Then we know more than we did.”

“We know what she will be paid for.”

He looked up at the larger boat. The narrow one was coming in again, lighter now, with a man sitting on the gunwale to hold it level.

The tally keeper called Sulad over. She gave him a leaf packet and made him open it before she marked her board.

“For the lifting,” she said. “From the owner’s food. You have done the work you agreed.”

Sulad examined the food. He did not eat at once.

“And the passage?”

“The master will tell you himself.”

“I have already been paid for the loading, then.”

“Yes.”

He nodded, and came back to me with the packet.

“Are you going to share that?” I asked.

“No. You have made a different bargain.”

He said it without anger. I had eaten the meal from the last boat. I had no claim on this one.

A shout came from the water. One of the men aboard the larger vessel had lifted a corner of the covering and was pointing toward the stern. The master went down the bank and stepped into the narrow boat. I followed until the tally keeper held out an arm.

“Not you. The last parcels go first.”

Daya had returned. She stood beside the two empty spaces where her cloth had been and watched the narrow boat pull away. When it reached the larger vessel, the master climbed aboard and disappeared beneath the lifted covering. He was there long enough for Daya to put her hands on her hips.

“He should have made that place before they carried my goods out,” she said.

The tally keeper did not answer.

The master came up with one of Daya’s parcels in his arms. I knew it by the narrow red strip beneath the wrapping. He handed it to the man beside him. The man moved two smaller bundles, put the red-edged parcel down again, and pointed to a place farther inside.

“Why are they moving it?” Daya asked.

“They have put it where water can run against the fold,” the tally keeper said. “They are making another place.”

“The second is beside it?”

“Not yet.”

Daya turned toward me. “You wanted work watching the covering.”

“Yes.”

“Then remember what happens when one of my parcels is out of sight of the other.”

“I will tell the man in charge.”

“Tell him before it is wet.”

She looked back at the boat. I did too. I could not see whether the first parcel was dry. I could see only the men making room for it.

When the narrow boat returned, the master came ashore and called Sulad to him.

“There is room,” he said. “You may come as far as the receiving creek. You will carry water to the men working the sail and help empty the water they take from the bottom. You will do it when they tell you. You are not to touch the steering paddle.”

“Who will show me?”

“The man who works beside it. He knows when water needs to be lifted.”

“And the food?”

“You eat with the crew while you do that work. No silver. At the creek we settle what was done and what remains.”

Sulad looked at the boat. “And if we cannot go farther from there?”

“Then you stay where the cloth is delivered. I cannot bargain with the next master for you.”

Sulad said, “I will take that place.”

The master turned to me.

“You will watch the covering and the parcels beneath it. The man I showed you will lift the cloth when it needs lifting. You will tell him what you see. If you reach beneath it without his order, you will be put ashore at the creek.”

“I understand.”

“I heard you say that before.”

He made me repeat what I would do if water collected against Daya’s parcels. I answered him. Then he asked what I would do if the covering came loose while a man was standing on it.

“Call him down before he falls.”

“And the parcels?”

“After the man is safe.”

He gave a small nod. “The crew will tell you where to stand.”

We carried our jar to the narrow boat. Sulad lowered it between his feet and kept one hand on the cloth at its mouth. I sat opposite him, close enough to the gunwale to see the larger vessel’s side rising above us.

From the bank it had looked like a single shape beneath a mast. Beside it I could see the joints in the planking and the places where the wood had been rubbed smooth by hands and loads. A man reached down for our jar. Sulad asked where he would put it before letting go.

“Beside your feet,” the man said. “It is yours to keep from breaking.”

I was glad Sulad had asked. I had been about to pass it up without a word.

The crewman responsible for the covering showed me the narrow space where I could sit without putting my weight on the bundles. He lifted one edge and pointed to the two parcels from Daya’s lot. They lay near each other now, with a smaller bundle between them.

“Two parcels,” he said. “One lot. If that one moves, this one may take its place. Tell me before either comes against the side.”

“Should they not be tied together?”

“No. They must be able to come out one at a time. That is why I need you to watch.”

He set the covering down and showed me where its edge should fall. A strip of cloth lay over the side so that water running down it would fall outside the hull. He made me look from the other end, where a fold had been tucked beneath a crosspiece.

“If the fold turns inward, water comes in with it. You do not pull the whole covering to mend one fold.”

I looked at the two places until I could tell them apart.

Sulad was at the other end of the boat, learning how to pass a small vessel of water to the man who emptied it. The man showed him how to keep his knees clear of a moving pole. Sulad tried once, and the man made him try again.

The tally keeper boarded last. She carried the board inside her cloth and counted the parcels aloud with the crewman before she sat. Daya stood on the bank until the two agreed.

“Two parcels, one lot,” she called.

The tally keeper raised her board. “I have them.”

“You have the count.”

“I have their places too.”

Only then did Daya turn away.

The master went to the stern. A man there held a long steering paddle. Another stood by the mast with his hands on a line. They spoke quietly, and I could not hear what they decided. The narrow boat moved away from our side. Someone called to the bank for the last fastening to be freed.

The vessel did not move at once. The men worked to bring its head clear of the smaller boats. The steersman shifted his paddle. The sail opened a little, fell slack, and opened again.

The landing began to slip behind us.

I watched the covering. Its outer edge lifted and settled. Nothing beneath it moved.

Sulad glanced toward me from his place near the stern. He had finished his food before boarding, and his hands were already wet with the work he had agreed to do. He did not wave. He pointed instead at the jar, and I pulled it a little farther from the nearest parcel.

We passed the last boats at anchor. Beyond them the water widened. I could still see the bank, but I could no longer tell which of the paths led back to the house where we had carried the mats. A gust lifted the edge of the covering near my knee.

I called the crewman. He came, watched it rise once more, and set the fold where he wanted it. He asked whether I had touched anything.

“No.”

“Then keep watching.”

A little water struck the side and fell away. Another gust followed. The sail made a hard sound above us, and the master called for it to be eased. I looked toward Sulad. The man beside him was already showing him where to sit while the crew worked.

I wanted to ask whether this was the beginning of bad weather. I did not know enough to give the wind a name.

Under the covering were two parcels that belonged to Daya, and beside them the goods of people I had not met. The crew knew where those goods were meant to go. I knew only where I had been told to sit, and what to call out if the cloth began to turn inward.

I stayed there as the landing grew smaller.
