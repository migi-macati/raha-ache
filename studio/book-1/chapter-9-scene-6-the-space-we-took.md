# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 6: The Space We Took (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 5 at the unnamed second landing. The leader obtains a serviceable line at a cost, the crew agrees to limited terms for Ache and Sulad, and the boat departs for the next unnamed delivery landing. Neither traveler has passage beyond it.*

The woman came back with a coil over her shoulder. The man who had owned it followed her as far as the bank, still telling her what he had given up.

“You said you had no other use for it,” she told him.

“I said I had no use for it today.”

“Then you should have charged me for tomorrow as well.”

He looked at the coins in his palm. She turned away before he could begin again.

The line was darker than the one she had refused. When she dropped it on the planks, the end struck the wood with a wet sound. Her crewman uncoiled the whole length, ran it through his hands, and bent each stiff place before he said it would serve.

“What did it cost?” the other man asked.

“Enough that I want it brought home,” she said. “Keep it clear of the jars.”

The man who had inspected my fastening earlier held up the damaged line. “And this?”

“Cut the worn part away. The rest is still useful.”

He looked at the new rope, then at the water beyond the bow. Neither line had made the boat ready to leave.

The woman counted the jars aloud. She touched each one with two fingers, and the crewman repeated the count from the other side. There were fewer than I had expected. I had been watching their bindings for so long that I had begun to think of the jars as a wall.

The smaller bundles went under the covering, but she left a gap through which a hand could reach the lashings. The man who had wanted the extra baskets returned without them and stood on the bank to see whether she would change her mind. She did not look at him.

“Nothing more from the buyer?” she asked the crewman.

“Nothing he has sent.”

“That is not the same answer.”

He went up the path to ask. We waited.

Sulad sat beside our jar. He had taken the cloth from its mouth and was looking at the water left inside. The last time I had seen him measure it so carefully, we had been waiting to know whether we could sleep under a stranger's roof.

“You heard what she offered,” I said.

“I heard what she might offer.”

“Would you take it?”

“If she needs the carrying and gives me the meal, yes.”

“And after that?”

“I will decide after that.”

He put the cloth back over the mouth. He did not sound angry. I found that harder to answer than anger.

The crewman returned with word that the buyer had finished counting. He had wanted the boat to wait for another bundle, but would send no more today. The woman made him repeat the last part.

“Now ask them,” she said, pointing at us.

The two men who worked the boat stood near the stern. One had been quiet while the other argued about the rope. The quiet man looked at the place where Sulad and I would sit.

“Two bodies and a jar,” he said.

“We have our own water,” Sulad answered.

“For now,” the man said. “Will you use ours when yours is gone?”

“If we do, you can count it against the unloading.”

The woman watched him. “I offered a meal. I did not offer you the crew's water without measure. We will fill what we need before leaving.”

The quiet man lifted our jar and judged its weight, then set it down. He asked where we had last filled it. Sulad told him. The man sniffed at the mouth and tasted a drop from his finger.

“It will do,” he said.

The other crewman looked at me. “He can sit where you showed him. If he gets in my way when we turn, I put him ashore at the next landing.”

“You will tell me first,” the woman said.

“Then you can put him ashore.”

She waited for the quiet man. He nodded once.

“Both of you may come for this delivery,” she told us. “You do the work we named. You eat when the goods are received. You take your own jar away with you. Nothing else has been promised.”

Sulad asked whether the bundles would be carried from the boat to the buyer's store, or only to the bank.

“To the store if the path is dry,” she said. “If it is mud, we will agree how far before you lift.”

“Then I agree to that work.”

I agreed to mine. The words came out quickly, and I wished afterward that I had made her say the tasks once more.

We filled the crew's water vessels from a place upstream of the landing. The quiet man led us there and watched while we drew it. I carried one vessel and Sulad another. We came back by the narrow path, where the roots stood above the soil and caught at our feet. I wanted to walk faster. The man behind us said the water would arrive at the same speed whether we hurried or not.

By the time we returned, the light had shifted off the stern. The woman had put her coins away. She was examining the covering over the bundles, lifting one edge to see whether it would drain if water came aboard.

“Set yours there,” the quiet man told me, pointing to the place beside the crosspiece.

I did. He passed me the free end of the new rope while he took the other around the bow.

“Keep that out from under the jars.”

I held it against my leg. The line was stiff and wanted to return to its coil. When I shifted to let Sulad pass with the second vessel, it fell across the narrow way. I made a small loop and put it beneath the crosspiece so no one would step on it.

The crewman called for the end. I pulled. It did not come.

I pulled harder. The coil tightened around a foot of the jar rack.

“Stop,” the woman said.

I stopped. The boat moved under us, though it had not left the bank. The crewman came back along the planks and saw what I had done.

“I told you to keep it clear.”

“It was across the way.”

“Then you should have held it.”

He knelt to free the loop. The rack would not lift with the jars in place, and the rope would not pass beneath it while the coil was tight. The quiet man came forward to steady the nearest jar. They took two jars out, set them on the bank, lifted the rack, and drew the line through.

The woman did not speak while they worked. I could hear the rope dragging over the wood, then the short scrape as they set the rack down again.

The man who had sold the line called from farther along the shore, asking whether it had proved too short after all.

“It is long enough,” the woman called back.

The crewman handed me the freed end. “When I say keep it clear, I mean in your hand until I take it. Not somewhere you think is safer.”

“I understand.”

“You understand now.”

The woman inspected the jars after they were returned. She ran her fingers beneath the rack, where I had trapped the rope. Nothing was broken, but she had to count them again, and the men had to test their lashings. A boat waiting beyond us pushed off first. Its stern passed our bow while we were still standing on the bank.

I watched it move into the open water. I had cost us time without dropping anything. I had thought that meant no harm was done.

“Will we lose the light?” I asked.

“We have enough for this passage if we leave now,” she said. “If we do not, we stay. The jars can wait longer than we can see.”

She told the quiet man to take the bow. The other crewman stepped into the stern and checked the steering oar. No one handed me a pole.

Sulad set our jar between us, where it would not roll. I sat in the space the woman had shown me. My knees touched the side of a bundle, but I could still reach the fastening she had assigned me to watch. I pressed one finger against it to learn where the strain lay, then took my hand away.

The woman looked down the length of the boat.

“Count.”

The crewman counted the jars. The quiet man counted the water vessels. Sulad counted our own things: the jar, his cloth, my cloth, the little that remained tied inside it. He did it softly, for himself.

“Line,” the woman said.

The bowman loosened the turn from the post and brought the end aboard. This time I watched his hands. He laid the coil where it could run without catching anything, then sat back on his heels.

The woman gave the order to push away.

The stern moved first. A strip of wet bank showed between us and the place where we had been tied. The crewman used his pole until the water deepened, then brought it in at her word. The woman watched the bundles, the line, the space between us and the next boat. I watched the bindings.

Sulad leaned toward me as the landing began to shrink.

“You should have told him where you put the rope.”

“I thought I could keep it from being stepped on.”

“I know.”

He settled back. I had expected him to say more, and I was relieved when he did not. Then I saw that he had set his foot beside our jar to keep it steady without resting his weight against it. He had been doing that since we boarded. I had not noticed.

Behind us, the man with the refused baskets was still on the shore. He lifted his pole when another boat came close, ready to ask again. The woman did not turn to see him.

I had asked her whether the next landing was near Brunei. She had answered with the price of carrying jars. That was still all I knew about where we were going.

The new rope lay in a neat coil at the bow. It had cost her silver. My mistake had cost the crew part of the afternoon. The sick man's place was no longer empty, but it had not become mine.

When the boat passed beyond the last post, I could no longer reach the bank by stepping out. I kept my eyes on the fastening until the woman told me there was something else to do.
