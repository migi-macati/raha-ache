# Book I, Chapter 5 — Scene 3: The Missing Catch (Studio working prose)

The boat was drawn above the tide line. A length of net hung between two posts beside it, and Sulad's uncle sat beneath the shade with a heap of torn mesh in his lap.

Sulad was separating sound cord from frayed cord. Each piece he saved went into a little coil by his knee.

"I need the boat," I said.

"Everyone needs a boat," his uncle answered.

"For three days. Perhaps less."

"Then I need three days of fish."

"I can pay."

"With what?"

I told him about the merchant crossing to Tondo, about the steward learning the organizer's terms, about crews who might soon find it easier to work for the other shore. Sulad's uncle kept working while I spoke.

"You want us to sit at the landing," he said at last.

"To offer the merchants another choice."

"And while we wait, who brings in our catch?"

I had come prepared to argue about Tondo. I had not prepared an answer about fish.

"The stakes are damaged," I said. "You may lose tonight's catch anyway."

"Then we need the hours before the water turns, not a prince telling us how many days we can spare."

Sulad looked up sharply. His uncle was not looking at him.

"I could bring food," I said.

"From your own house?"

"From the household stores."

"Has your mother said yes?"

"Not yet."

His uncle picked up a section of net whose knots had come loose in a row. He spread it across his knees and waited for Sulad to pass him more cord.

"When she says yes, you can ask me again."

"Just one boat," I said.

"One boat is the one we have."

I looked at Sulad. He did not offer to persuade his uncle.

"You know what is happening at the landing," I said.

"I know what is happening here," he replied.

He held up a length of cord that had been cut too short to join anything. It fell from his fingers into the waste pile.

"I thought you would want to help."

"With what?"

"With Maynila."

"Am I somewhere else?"

I had meant to say something brave. Instead I stood watching his uncle work through the torn mesh, knot by knot, until the silence became worse than any answer.

"Can I do anything?" I asked.

Sulad moved the sound cord away from me.

"You can hold this edge where I can reach it."

I took the net between my hands. Wet fiber rubbed against the tender place on my thumb. He threaded the cord through the opening, pulled it snug, and moved to the next. We worked without speaking for a while.

Once, when I loosened my grip, the mesh sagged.

"Hold it there," he said, bringing my hand back to the place he wanted it.

His fingers were cold from the water. I felt them for only a moment. The small cord he wore for protection was still hidden beneath his cloth.

I kept the net steady.

At last his uncle examined the repaired stretch and laid it with the pieces ready to be hung.

"I could carry a message," he said. "One crossing, if the water is fair. After the net is finished, and if someone pays for the work."

"Only one?"

"One is what I am offering."

"I need more than a message."

"Then you must ask someone who has more to give."

I thanked him. The boat had not moved, and I had gained no crew. But I knew exactly what his offer would cost, which was more than I had known when I arrived.

On the path back I heard Sulad call after me.

"Do not tell your mother I agreed to leave."

"I won't."

"Or that my uncle did."

"I won't."

He waited until I looked at him.

"Ask us when you know what you can offer."

I went home with the smell of wet fiber on my hands and a promise I had made before I knew whether I could keep it.
