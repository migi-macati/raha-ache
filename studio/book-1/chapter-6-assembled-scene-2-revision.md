# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 2: The Space Between Baskets (consolidation revision)

*Studio-only candidate for the continuous Chapter 6 manuscript. This is a substantive compression of the existing Scene 2, not an additional scene. Do not publish or concatenate both versions. The carrier, prices, first landing, and dialogue are fictional; regional maritime trade is the historical context.*

On the fourth morning, Ula sent a child to find me.

He had been told that a man was home and I should bring my question, not my temper. I gave him a piece of fruit for his trouble. He bit it before he reached the next house.

By the time I came to Ula, the man had gone to the landing.

“Did he find the captain?” I asked.

“He found men who had heard of someone like him.”

“Someone like him?”

“Go and ask. I won't improve the answer by repeating it.”

She returned to the shells on her mat. I had called on her twice in the four days since leaving my silver beneath the broken shell. Both times she had been away. I had begun to think she was avoiding me. It was easier than asking why her days should arrange themselves around mine.

At the landing, buyers passed baskets along a line of waiting hands. One basket had been mended with a strip of dark reed. Each man shifted his grip before the next took the weight. The load reached the bank without touching the mud.

The woman's brother stood beside a row of jars. He had a cut near one thumbnail and was trying to pull a splinter from it.

“You're the boy looking for a captain,” he said.

“Did you find him?”

“No. I asked two men upriver. One remembered a Malay-speaking captain bargaining there. Neither knew where he'd gone.”

I had been holding my breath without noticing. I let it out.

“I told Ula we should agree on a price before you went.”

“She told me. I had goods to carry upriver, so I asked while I was there. You owe me nothing.”

He returned to his thumb. I could have walked away with my silver and called the errand a failure. He had spent time asking strangers a question I should have asked myself.

“What would you take for the trouble?”

He looked at me for the first time as if the question might be worth answering. He named a small sum. I offered less. We settled between the two, and he counted the silver before putting it away.

“That doesn't buy another errand,” he said.

“I know.”

“Good.”

Ula had said there was something else. I waited until he had pulled the splinter free.

“A trader here carries goods beyond the river,” he said. “He knows men who go farther than he does.”

“To Brunei?”

“Don't ask him that first. He'll think you're asking him to take you there.”

“Isn't that what I want?”

“It isn't what he sells.”

He pointed toward a boat where a narrow-faced man was marking a slat while his crew counted jars. I watched them move one cracked vessel aside and replace it with another. The trader waited for the count to finish before looking at me.

“I hear you take goods beyond the river.”

“When there's a buyer.”

“I need to travel south.”

“How far?”

“To men who trade with Brunei.”

He put down the slat. “I can tell you where I intend to sell these goods. I cannot promise who will carry you after that.”

“Where do you intend to sell them?”

“At the first landing where the price is worth unloading. Perhaps farther if it isn't.”

I wanted a name. He wanted a buyer. Neither of us had come to the landing for the other's purpose.

“Could I go as far as you do?”

“How many?”

“One. Perhaps two.”

“Then you don't yet know what you're asking me to carry.”

“My friend has to decide for himself.”

“That is sensible of him.”

The trader lifted a basket, tested its weight, and set it behind another. “A passenger eats. He drinks. He takes the space where I might put something that pays better. Can he work?”

“I can carry baskets.”

“So can the men who know which baskets belong to which buyer.”

“I can learn.”

“Then learn before you stand on a loaded boat.”

The woman's brother had followed me. He made a sound behind his hand. I did not turn around.

The trader named a fare for one passenger to his first stop. Food and water would be ours to provide. A second passenger would cost more, though less than two separate fares. Even the first price exceeded the silver I had left after paying the messenger.

“What if I bring provisions from home?”

“Whose provisions?”

“Our household's.”

He examined my clothes, then the man beside me. I saw the moment he understood whose household I meant. His face grew careful.

“Ask whoever keeps the stores,” he said. “I won't have a family come after me because a son has given away their food.”

“My mother knows I want to travel.”

“Does she know what you propose to take?”

I could not answer that as quickly.

“I'll ask her.”

“Then ask her. And ask your friend. I'll not set aside cargo space for either of you until I know what's coming aboard.”

“Can I leave something to hold the place?”

“There is no place to hold yet. I haven't finished loading.”

He turned back to his slat. A crewman called out another count. I stood there, waiting for the trader to notice that I had not gone.

“If I return tomorrow?”

“You may find me. You may find an empty landing.”

It was not a threat. It was the nearest thing to a promise he would make.

The woman's brother walked beside me as far as the first houses. His jar was waiting where he had left it, and he had lost enough of his morning to me.

“You should have told him who I was,” I said.

“He worked it out.”

“He might have spoken differently sooner.”

“He did speak differently. Did you like it?”

I remembered the care that had entered the trader's face. I had mistaken care for respect before.

The brother lifted his jar. “I have somewhere to be when my goods come back,” he said. “If you go, make sure the person going with you has chosen to go.”

He returned to the landing. I counted my remaining silver, though I knew what it would tell me. Behind him the crew moved another basket aboard. It filled the space where I had imagined sitting.
