# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 5D: What He Could Leave

*Studio working prose. Follows Scene 5C. Ache narrates. Fictional household negotiation and commercial arrangements; no passage booked or departure yet.*

By the time I reached the fishing bank, Sulad's uncle had hung the mended net between two posts.

It sagged in the middle. A line of new knots ran through the old ones, paler than the rest. Sulad was testing each knot with his fingers. His uncle stood on the other side and pulled when Sulad told him to pull.

“Is it finished?” I asked.

“One more,” Sulad said.

I waited. A fish seller came by and called a price to the uncle, who answered without looking up. They argued over a basket I could not see. The seller went away. Neither man appeared disappointed.

Sulad made the last knot, wet it and drew it tight. His uncle pulled. The knot held.

“Now?” Sulad asked.

“Now we know it holds here,” his uncle said. “We will know the rest when we put it in the water.”

Sulad took a small cloth bundle from beneath the overturned fishing basket. He did not open it.

“Where did you get that?” his uncle asked.

“From the work I did with the fish sellers. You said I could keep what they paid me for carrying.”

“I said you could keep your share.”

“This is my share.”

His uncle put down the net. “You have been saving it.”

“Yes.”

“For this?”

“For something that was mine to choose.”

I looked away. It was easier to watch the water than to stand between them while they spoke of money.

The uncle lifted the net off one post and began folding it. “I thought you would buy a better knife.”

“I may still need one.”

“You may need a roof first.”

“I know.”

“You do not know where that boat will put you ashore after the first stop.”

“Neither does Ache.”

“I asked you.”

Sulad waited until his uncle looked at him.

“I want to go,” he said. “I have wanted to go since before he asked. That does not mean I know what I will find.”

I had been waiting for him to say yes to me. He had said something larger and more troubling: that the wish had been his before I knew it.

His uncle nodded once. “Then you will have to tell the others why the net comes back without you.”

“I told my cousins I might leave. One said he can come on the mornings he is free.”

“And on the mornings he is not?”

“You will have fewer hands.”

“Good. You can see the cost, then.”

Sulad knelt beside the folded net. He laid the bundle on top of it, but his uncle pushed it back toward him.

“Keep your money. I need hands, not a payment that will be gone by the next catch.”

“I was going to pay my fare.”

“I know. You have been counting it under the cloth every night.”

Sulad's face changed. “You knew?”

“I sleep in the same house.”

The uncle lifted the net and carried it toward the boat. Sulad went to take the other end. I caught myself starting forward, then waited for them to tell me where to stand. Neither did.

When they came back, the uncle spoke to me for the first time.

“If he goes, you will not tell anyone I sent him.”

“I won't.”

“You will not tell anyone he owes you for the journey.”

“He doesn't.”

“And if he wants to return at the first landing, you will not stand in his way.”

“I couldn't.”

“You could try.”

I wanted to say that Sulad would never leave me. I had no right to say it, and I was no longer certain it was true.

“He can come home whenever he chooses,” I said.

Sulad looked at me. “You cannot promise me a boat home.”

“No.”

“Then promise only that you won't stop me looking for one.”

“I won't.”

His uncle went back to the net. Sulad stood beside me with his bundle in his hand.

“You have the gold?” he asked.

“One piece. My mother kept the other until I could tell her the fare.”

“Then you must go to her after we speak to the trader.”

“If he still has room.”

“If he doesn't, I have mended a net and you have asked better questions than yesterday.”

We walked toward the landing. I wanted to take his hand, and was glad there were too many people between us for him to see me reach halfway and stop.

The trader's baskets had been moved into two rows. Several jars were gone. Men were checking the bindings on the rest while a buyer pressed his finger against the patched reed.

The trader saw us and held up two fingers.

“Two people?” he asked.

“Two asking,” Sulad said.

“Then ask quickly. The buyer has taken the jars he wanted. I may have space when we finish loading.”

“May?” I said.

“Until the last basket is aboard, yes.”

Sulad opened his bundle. I could see pieces of silver and a narrow strip of cloth folded around them.

“I will pay for myself,” he said. “Tell me the fare to the first landing and what food I must bring.”

The trader named his price. Sulad counted what he had, then counted again. His mouth tightened. He closed the cloth.

“I am short.”

“How short?” I asked.

“Enough that I know it.”

I touched the gold inside my own cloth. Sulad saw the movement.

“Don't,” he said.

“I haven't offered anything.”

“You were about to.”

The trader turned back to his buyer. He had no reason to watch us discover the distance between two small bundles and a boat.

“I can ask my mother what she will lend,” I said.

“Ask what she will give you,” Sulad answered. “If you promise to repay it with work I haven't agreed to do, we are back where we began.”

I looked at him. He was frightened, I thought, though not of the water. His hand was tight around the bundle. I had never seen him hold money as if it might run away.

“Will you wait while I ask?” I said.

“I'll stay here and see what the trader loads. If the space goes, it goes.”

I left him by the baskets. On the path home I passed the fishing bank again. Sulad's uncle was already setting the repaired net into the boat. He worked alone for a time, then a cousin came down from the houses and took the other end.

The net rose between them. It did not look as if it had been mended for the last time.
