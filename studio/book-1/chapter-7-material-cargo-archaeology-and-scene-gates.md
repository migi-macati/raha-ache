## Archaeological anchors near the novel's broad period

**Regional commerce, not a documented itinerary.** The National Museum of the Philippines dates the Pandanan shipwreck off southern Palawan to the **middle fifteenth century**, based on a Yongle-period coin and ceramic typology. Its 4,722 recovered objects include Vietnamese, Thai and Chinese ceramics, glass beads, earthenware, a stove, bronze gongs, iron cauldrons, small cannons and grinding stones. The museum identifies a vessel combining Southeast Asian and Chinese construction elements and links the dominant Vietnamese ceramic cargo to wider regional exchange. This establishes that varied imported cargo moved through Philippine waters before the novel's early-sixteenth-century setting. It does **not** locate Ache's unnamed first landing, prove his route to Brunei, or identify Mulaw's vessel. Source: https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/maritime-and-underwater-cultural-heritage/maritime-pandanan-shipwreck-2/

**Different wreck, different construction.** The National Museum dates the Lena Shoal wreck near Busuanga to the late fifteenth century (the museum also describes the broader late-fifteenth/early-sixteenth-century horizon elsewhere). Recovered cargo includes ceramics from China, Thailand, Vietnam and Burma/Myanmar, tin ingots, glass beads, bronze objects and organic material. Its approximately 24-metre estimated original length and substantial carrying capacity describe **that ship**, not the visiting boat in Chapter 7. The existence of large trade vessels does not authorize giving Mulaw a ship of comparable size or assuming all crews carried porcelain. Sources: https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/our-collections/maritime-and-underwater-cultural-heritage/maritime-pandanan-shipwreck/ and https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2022/01/03/chinese-blue-and-white-porcelain-pouring-vessels-from-the-lena-shoal-shipwreck/ . **Catalog caution:** the first URL's page heading is “Lena Shoal Shipwreck” despite its path mentioning Pandanan; cite the heading and actual content, not the misleading slug.

**Textiles existed; Nima's exact cloth is invented.** The Cultural Center of the Philippines' *Encyclopedia of Philippine Art* describes the Banton burial cloth as abaca warp ikat, roughly fourteenth–fifteenth century, held by the National Museum. It demonstrates skilled precolonial textile production, not the identity, ownership, provenance or pattern of Nima's fictional bundle. It was found in a burial context, so it is especially unsound to use it as proof that a particular decorated cloth was ordinary export cargo at an unnamed landing. The National Museum's Hibla gallery also exhibits Banton cloth fragments and textile-making technologies. Sources: https://epa.culturalcenter.gov.ph/3/82/2141/ and https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/exhibitions/anthropology/hibla/ . **Dating caution:** secondary museum/catalog descriptions differ in assigned ranges; do not assign an exact manufacture year.

**What archaeology cannot establish.** Shipwreck assemblages preferentially preserve ceramics and metals; surviving textile examples often come from exceptional contexts. Absence of cloth in a particular cargo list is not proof that cloth was not traded. Equally, finding regional ceramics cannot justify giving this small fictional boat a cargo of porcelain or assigning its crew a nationality. Treat every such detail as an invented choice unless a source explicitly establishes it.

## Application to the Chapter 7 scene sequence

1. **Scene 2 is a dispute over a cloth bundle and a place to tie**, not a museum reconstruction. Keep its material actions modest: a wet covering, visible folds, poles holding a boat off a post, a buyer protecting space already in use. Do not name a specific ship type, island, dye technique or historic trade route.
2. **Scene 3's costly mistake** should come from Ache's failure to distinguish *moving cargo to prevent further damage* from *authority to use another person's landing or storage*. His silver may buy labor, rope or time if someone actually accepts that bargain. It cannot buy Nima's sister's ownership, the host's rope or her roof. Because Scene 2 ends with Ache following Sulad uphill, show any later return downhill and the elapsed light.
3. **Scene 4's refusal** should make Sulad's objection concrete: whose silver, whose labor, whose promise, and whether he is willing to stay. Let the buyer or Nima rephrase a term that Ache has misunderstood. The audience should see the failed translation through action rather than a lecture.
4. **Scene 5's limited repair** can use ordinary drying, shifting, sheltering or retensioning *only after the responsible parties agree*. A boat's cargo does not become free property merely because it is endangered. At least one party should retain a grievance or debt.
5. **Scene 6's outcome** can be an introduction to another possible trader or a conditional chance to ask for onward passage. Archaeological evidence of regional commerce cannot be used to promise a particular route, captain, fare or Brunei arrival.
