The younger man would not put his hand down.

The woman held out a folded strip of cloth. He took it with his other hand and laid it across his swollen wrist without wrapping it.

“Can you move these?” she asked, touching her own fingers.

He showed her. The smallest finger barely moved. When he tried the others, his mouth opened, but he made no sound.

“Enough,” her brother said. “Keep it still.”

The rain had thinned. Water still gathered beneath the boards, and Sulad passed our jar from hand to hand until the older crewman waved him away. I wanted to take it from him. I had nothing to offer the injured man, and the woman was kneeling over her basket where she had counted seven pieces of wax.

“May I look beneath the boards?” I asked.

She looked up. “Did you see it leave the boat?”

“No.”

“Then don't tell me it is gone.”

I searched the places she pointed out, working from the basket toward the side. A piece of fish scale had caught between two planks. A cord end had wound around the foot of the mast. I found neither the missing wrapping nor the wax inside it.

The older crewman lifted a loose floorboard where the water had gathered deepest. He felt beneath it and brought out a wet leaf, black with dirt. He showed it to the woman. She shook her head.

“Put the board back,” her brother said. “I need you aft.”

I wanted to keep searching. The boat was moving again, though I could not tell how quickly. The brother had taken the steering paddle back, and the gathered sail lay in a wet heap. The woman closed the basket before I had finished looking.

“We counted eight at the creek,” she said.

“I heard you.”

“You saw one loose.”

“Yes.”

“Then you can say what you saw when we reach the anchorage. Nothing more.”

She set the basket beside her and kept a foot against its base. I went back to the place she had assigned me, though there was nothing left for me to hold.

The younger man leaned against a rolled mat. The cloth lay around his wrist now, tied by the older crewman so that his hand rested against his chest. He kept looking at the fingers as though they belonged to someone who had not yet decided what to do with them.

“Does it hurt?” I asked.

He turned his head. “What do you think?”

I had meant to ask whether he could work. I sat down instead.

Sulad came forward with our jar. It was empty except for a little brown water that moved along the bottom. He poured that over the side, rinsed the mouth with the last drops, and stood it between his feet.

“You emptied our drinking water,” I said.

“I emptied the boat with it.”

“We have none.”

“I know.”

The brother called for Sulad to help shift a wet mat away from the fish. Sulad went without asking me to keep the jar. I pulled it toward me. The rim was chipped where it had struck the side in the rain. We had carried it from landing to landing, filling it wherever someone would let us. Now I could put my thumb over the chip.

The woman began counting again. She laid seven pieces in a row and opened each wrapping enough to see the wax. One had a dent at its edge. She touched the dent and put it back with the others. The eighth place on the mat remained bare.

She called her brother.

“There were eight.”

“I know.”

“If the receiver pays for seven, I lose one.”

“I know that too.”

“You told me these two could work.”

“They did work.”

She pointed at me. “He was holding the basket when it moved.”

“You told him to let go,” the brother said.

“Because his hands were on the cord.”

“Because I told him to watch it, not tie it,” she said. “I know what I told him.”

Neither spoke for a while. The brother glanced toward the younger man. “If we had waited for the wax, his hand would still be under the fish.”

The woman folded the mat over the seven pieces. She did not look at me.

“We will count at the landing,” she said. “If there are seven, I will mark seven.”

I had been preparing an answer to an accusation. There was no accusation I could answer. She had lost something and knew exactly what she had lost. I had no piece to put back.

The coast came into view as a low line broken by trees. A second boat lay farther out, its sail dark against the clearing sky. I watched the brother watch it. He did not call to its crew.

When we reached the shallower water, the older crewman took up a pole. The younger man tried to stand. His brother told him to remain seated. He tried again when no one was looking, then sank back with his injured hand held away from his knee.

The woman saw him.

“Stay there,” she said.

“Who will carry the fish?”

“We have hands.”

“Mine was one of them.”

“It still is,” she said, and set a folded mat beneath his elbow. “Leave it where it is.”

The anchorage had no wall or gate that I could see. Boats lay at different distances from shore, and people moved between them in small craft. A man standing in water to his thighs took the brother's line and brought us toward a narrow strip of firm ground. Behind him, several baskets stood beneath a roof of woven leaves.

The woman called for a receiving board before anyone touched her wax.

A man came down from the shelter carrying one. He asked what she had brought. She named the fish first, because it belonged to her brother, and then the wax.

“Eight pieces loaded,” she said. “Seven received aboard after the rain. Count them.”

The man looked from her to the boat. “Was the wrapping broken?”

“One piece came loose when we freed a man's hand. I could not find it after.”

“Did it go overboard?”

“I did not see.”

He marked the board. She watched him make a separate mark for the missing piece. The brother stood beside them and gave the same account. When the receiver asked whether a worker had dropped it, neither named one.

The older crewman and Sulad carried the fish up first. I stayed near the wax until the woman told me to move. Then I helped lift the seven pieces one at a time, without touching their bindings. She followed us to the shelter and watched each one laid down.

The younger man came ashore last. He would not take my offered arm. The older crewman walked beside him and cleared a place beneath the roof. The young man sat with his wrist on his knee, then moved it back against his chest.

“Will he be able to use it?” I asked the brother.

“I don't know.”

“Is there someone here who can look at it?”

“There is a woman who tends injuries beyond the fish sheds. He will go to her when the tally is finished.”

The younger man heard him. “I can walk now.”

“You can wait,” the brother said.

I helped carry the last wet mat to the shelter. The brother came to us with two packets of food wrapped in leaves. He held one out to Sulad and the other to me.

“The work we agreed,” he said.

I looked toward his sister. She was still at the receiving board.

“What about the wax?”

“That is between the owner and what happened. We agreed food for work. You worked.”

I took my packet. It was warm through the leaves. The brother did not smile or ask us to stay.

Sulad opened his and broke off a piece. Then he closed it again and walked to the water seller beside the sheds. I followed with our jar.

The seller tipped the jar to see its bottom. “Where is your water?”

“Gone,” Sulad said. “How much for enough to fill it?”

The seller named a portion of food larger than I expected. Sulad looked at his packet and asked whether half would do. The seller refused. There was a smaller measure, he said, but not enough to last two people if they left that afternoon.

I held out my packet.

Sulad pushed my hand back. “Keep yours.”

“We both drink.”

“You can give yours if you choose,” he said. “Don't give it because I emptied the jar.”

I asked the seller how much water both packets would buy. He showed us a larger vessel and named the amount. Sulad put his packet down first. I added mine. The seller took both, filled our jar below the chipped rim, and pressed a wet leaf into its mouth.

We stood with the jar between us. Neither of us had eaten since morning.

Across the anchorage a vessel was taking on baskets. I could hear several voices, but not enough of their words to know where it was bound. A man came down the beach carrying a roll of cord, and I asked whether any boat here dealt with merchants from Brunei.

“Some do,” he said. “Which boat will take you?”

“I am asking.”

“Then ask the owners.”

He went on without waiting for another question.

The wax woman came out from the shelter. She had folded her board beneath her arm.

“Seven are accepted,” she said. “One is short. They will take its value from what I receive.”

“Do you want me to work for it?” I asked.

She looked at the jar, then at my empty hands. “I don't know what work you could do that would replace it. I also don't know that you caused it.”

“I saw it fall.”

“You said that already.”

She glanced toward the younger man, who was being helped along the path beyond the sheds.

“I told you to watch a cord. You watched it. When I needed the basket moved, you were in the way. Then you moved. I lost the piece while we were freeing him.” She shifted the board under her arm. “If anyone asks, that is what I will say.”

“Will you tell a boat owner we can work?”

“No.”

“Because of the wax?”

“Because I have seen you on one crossing. I don't know what you can do on the next.”

She turned back to her goods. I wanted to follow her and offer something else. The receiving man called her name, and she went to him.

Sulad lifted the jar. The chip rested against his palm.

“We have water,” he said.

“And no food.”

“We had food. We chose water.”

He started toward the line of boats. I followed, keeping my eyes on the people who might answer us.

The younger man was gone from the shelter. The wax woman had seven pieces on her board, and someone would pay her for one less than she had brought. I knew where the eighth had been last seen. That was all I could carry to the next boat.

At the edge of the firm ground, Sulad stopped.

“Which one?” he asked.

I looked across the anchorage. There were boats moving, boats waiting, and men calling to people I did not know.

“Ask the one loading,” I said.

He nodded, but he did not move until I stepped into the wet sand beside him.
