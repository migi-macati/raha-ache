# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 3b: The Knot That Held (Studio prose candidate)

*Continuation of Scene 3a. A trial of work, not a berth or passage agreement. Ache narrates; Sulad has not consented to another crossing.*

The woman took me to the side of the boat where two jars stood beneath a crosspiece. A rope ran over their shoulders and through a hole in the timber. The knot had swollen with water.

“Tell me what is wrong,” she said.

I touched neither jar.

“The one on the outside will strike the plank when the boat leans.”

“Which way will it lean?”

I looked at the water. A boat had passed a little way downstream, and its wash was still coming toward us.

“That way.”

She waited. The wash reached the hull and rocked it toward the bank. The outside jar knocked against the wood. I had guessed correctly, but the sound was sharper than I had expected.

“Fix it,” she said.

I crouched and pulled the rope. It did not move. The wet fibers had tightened into themselves, and the knot was beyond my fingers.

“May I use the knife?”

“Whose knife?”

I looked toward the path where the sick man had gone. His bundle had contained a knife, and I had seen the crew return it to him. I had almost asked for a tool that was no longer theirs to lend.

“Yours,” I said.

“I have one. But you have not told me why you need it.”

“To cut this loose.”

“And then?”

I looked again at the jars. Cutting the line would leave both free to roll.

“I don't know yet.”

She gave me the loose end of another cord.

“Try without cutting.”

The cord was shorter than I wanted. I passed it behind the nearer jar, then under the crosspiece, but when I drew it tight the other jar moved. I had tied them together so that one could pull the other out of place.

I undid it.

A crewman standing above me laughed once. The woman told him to go on with his own work.

I tried again, keeping the first jar against the timber while I worked the cord around the second. The water came back against the hull. My knee slipped on the wet plank, and I caught myself with one hand. The cord loosened. I started over.

By the third attempt I had a turn that held each jar against the crosspiece without pulling the other toward it. I put my weight against the outside one. It moved less than a finger's width.

“Will it hold when we push off?” she asked.

“I can watch it until you do.”

“That is an answer about your eyes. I asked about the rope.”

I pulled the cord again and found where it had begun to slip beneath the old knot. I made another turn and left enough free end for someone to undo it without a blade.

“I think it will hold,” I said. “I would check it after the first hard turn.”

She leaned down and tested both jars herself. Then she called the crewman back to inspect the fastening. He put his hand where mine had been and shook the outer jar.

“It may hold,” he said.

“Then leave it until we load,” she told him.

She looked at me. “You have shown me one piece of work. I have not offered you the man's place.”

“I know.”

“You will need to know more before we leave this bank.”

Sulad was still beside the rack with our water jar. He had watched me work without coming closer. When I met his eyes, he looked at the cord, not at the woman.

I had made one knot she was willing to keep. There was still no agreement for either of us to go.
