# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 1B: The Space Left for Us (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 1A on the mat carrier's boat. The first leg is fictional, its shores remain unnamed, and neither arrival nor another passage is guaranteed. Ache and Sulad owe unloading work before their bargain is fulfilled.*

The woman put one foot on the bank and watched the water move past the mooring pole.

Her brother had already taken up his pole. The other man waited at the bow with the line in his hand. I sat beside the stern support where she had told me to sit, close enough to feel the jar shift whenever the boat moved. The knot stayed tight. I kept one hand over it anyway.

“Why are we waiting?” I asked.

“For the water,” she said.

It was water I could see, running past us as it had run all morning. I looked at Sulad, but he was watching a pale strip of foam catch against a root below the bank. The foam pulled free, turned, and came back toward us.

The woman saw me watching it.

“Now you see,” she said.

Her brother stepped down from the boat. Together they pushed the bow a little farther from the mud. The other man paid out the line, keeping it just tight enough to stop the boat from turning. They did it without calling instructions across one another. I moved to help, and the woman put a hand against my shoulder.

“Stay where I put you. I know how much you weigh there.”

I sat again. My face grew hot, though she had spoken no louder than before.

The tree above the screen was taking the first light. The mat-mender had gone back inside. I wondered whether he had already begun another torn edge, and whether Sulad was wondering the same thing.

The foam passed the root without returning. The woman gave her brother a nod. He stepped aboard, braced his pole against the bottom, and pushed. The man at the bow let the line slip free of the post.

The bank moved.

At first it was only the post drawing away from my knee. Then the screen, the dark place where we had slept, and the path to the woman's house all shifted together. I had wanted the boat to leave for so long that I had imagined a moment when I would know what came next. Instead I had a wet cord beneath one hand and nowhere to put my other.

Sulad sat across from me with his back against the side. He was looking at the bank. I thought of asking whether he regretted coming. His mouth was set in the way it had been when the mat-mender showed him the loose turn. I left him alone.

We followed the sheltered water past houses that faced the bank and a narrow place where two boats waited for one another. The woman spoke to her brother when a shallow patch appeared ahead. He changed the angle of his pole before she finished. I could hear the wood scrape beneath us, then come free.

The mat bundles did not move. One corner of the covering lifted in the wind. I reached for it and stopped when I remembered the blow against my wrist.

“May I hold that?” I asked.

The brother glanced at the woman. She looked over the load.

“Hold it down. Don't pull it toward you.”

I laid my palm on the corner. The woven surface beneath was uneven, and the cord crossed it so tightly that it had pressed a shallow groove into the wrapping. The covering settled. My hand began to ache where the basket cuts had opened the day before.

A small boat came toward us. Its bow was high and almost empty. The man aboard called something to the woman that I could not make out. She answered, and he pointed behind him. Her brother slowed his stroke.

“What did he say?” I asked.

“That a boat is waiting at the next bank.”

“Going where?”

“He didn't say.”

I tried to see past the bend. There was nothing there yet but the backs of trees and a line of smoke.

Sulad shifted his feet to give the brother room to work the pole.

“You heard him,” he said to me. “He didn't say.”

“I heard that.”

“You were about to ask her if it would take us.”

“I might have.”

“Wait until you can see it.”

The woman heard us. She kept her eyes on the water.

“Even if you see it,” she said, “ask its owner.”

I pressed the covering down with the flat of my hand. It did not need so much pressure.

Beyond the bend the bank rose a little. A man stood beside an empty rack, and two children were turning a long piece of wood in the shallows. There were other boats, but I could not tell which one the stranger had meant. One lay tilted on the mud. Another was being unloaded by people who had not looked up as we came near.

The woman told her brother to keep us away from the rack.

“Is this the landing?” I asked.

“It is where the mats are wanted.”

She pointed toward a house set back from the bank. Its shutters were closed. A length of rope hung from a peg beneath the eaves.

“Who will take them?” I asked.

“The person who agreed to pay for them.”

The brother planted his pole and held the boat while the other man took a line ashore. I started to rise. The jar tugged against its knot, and I sat hard enough to jar my teeth.

“Not yet,” the woman said.

“I thought we had to unload.”

“We do. Not onto wet ground, and not before someone comes to claim what belongs to them.”

The man by the rack came over and looked at the bundles. He said the person at the house had gone inland before dawn. He did not know when they would return.

The woman listened, then asked him whether the rack was free for her goods. He shook his head. The children were still working beside it, and a coil of cord lay beneath one end.

I had carried the mats aboard. I knew how to lift them without dragging the ends. For a moment I thought that if I could get them ashore quickly, the woman would be rid of us and we could begin asking about the other boats.

But she was still speaking to the man by the rack. The mats were hers to deliver, and I had been hired to carry them where she told me.

Sulad reached across and pressed two fingers against the loose end of my sleeve.

“Wait,” he said.

I had not moved. He knew me well enough to see the movement before it happened.

The woman turned toward us.

“You said you would unload,” she said. “I will tell you when there is a place to put them.”

Her brother held the boat against the pull of the line. The other man watched the shore. I kept my hand on the covering and waited for someone with the right to receive the mats.
