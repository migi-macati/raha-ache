# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 3B: The Broken Loop

*Studio working prose; continuation of Scene 3A. Fictional scene, not a public chapter. The passage is still unbooked.*

“Let him in,” my mother told the steward.

The young rower came through the doorway without looking at the jars. I recognized him by the pale mark across the back of one hand. He had been at the paddle on Sima's first crossing, the one she had paid the organizer to arrange. The man had taken her silver. The rowers had received nothing.

“I was told there might be work,” he said.

“There is work,” my mother answered. “But I will not call it payment for what you are owed.”

“I did not ask you to.”

“You should ask the man who hired you.”

“I have.”

“What did he say?”

“That the woman paid him.”

My mother set down the counting board. I had heard those same words at the landing the evening before. Spoken by the man who had worked, they sounded worse.

“I can have the steward show you where the jars need moving,” she said. “He will tell you the pay before you begin. You may refuse it.”

The rower looked toward the cracked jar. “If I work today, will the organizer say I have given up the other claim?”

“He may say anything. The steward will mark today's work separately, with your name and the amount.”

“Will he pay me today?”

“Yes.”

“Then I will work.”

He went with the steward. I heard them speaking outside, deciding which jars were safe to lift. The young man had not asked my mother to settle the old debt for him. I had come asking her to make a new one.

She crossed to a low chest and opened it. Inside were folded cloths, a small wooden box and a cord with a broken gold loop tied to one end. She held the loop between finger and thumb. One side had been bent flat; the cord was newer than the metal.

“Your father wore this,” she said.

I had seen it before, but only when she was looking for something else. I remembered trying to put a finger through it as a child and being told not to pull.

“He broke it?” I asked.

“A man stepped on it. Your father had taken it off before going into the water. He was angrier about the missing cord than the gold.”

“Who stepped on it?”

“He did. He forgot where he had put it.”

She smiled, then put the loop in the box. For a moment I thought she would close the lid. Instead she moved the cloth aside and showed me two small pieces of gold wrapped separately.

“This is mine to give,” she said. “Not the merchants' grain. Not the food promised upriver.”

“Will you give it to me?”

“When I know what you are buying.”

I looked at the pieces. I could not tell whether they would cover the trader's price, and I did not want to ask in front of her.

“He will not promise a place,” I said. “He needs to know what cargo he carries.”

“Then learn when he will know. Find out where his first landing is, what you must bring, and how a message will reach me after you leave it.”

“He may be gone before I can ask all that.”

“Then he is gone.”

I made a sound I hoped she had not heard.

She heard it. “If I send you with food and gold and no one knows where you are, I cannot help you when the trader decides his baskets matter more than your journey.”

“He would be right to care about his baskets.”

“He would. You are beginning to listen.”

I disliked how pleased she sounded. “And if Sulad comes?”

“Has he agreed?”

“No.”

“Then do not count his food yet.”

“I would not force him.”

“You have asked him to leave his uncle's work. You may not think that is force. He may have to explain it to his uncle differently.”

I looked away. Sulad had said nearly the same thing, but less gently.

“And Bulkan?” she asked.

“He knows I want to go.”

“That is not an answer either. I need him here while the merchants decide whether they can trust the offers I have asked them to make. I will not send him after you because you are afraid of strangers.”

“I am not afraid.”

“You should be afraid of some strangers. It might keep you alive.”

I looked toward the doorway, where the rower and steward were lifting the cracked jar together. They moved slowly, testing its weight before taking a step. No one had ordered them to hurry.

“Would you rather I stayed?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said.

I had prepared myself for her to say no, or to say she had no choice. The plainness of it stopped me.

“Then why show me the gold?”

“Because I remember wanting to go where my mother could not follow. And because if you leave, I would rather you know what the journey costs before you have spent everything.”

She wrapped the pieces again and put them away.

“Come back when you can name the carrier, the first place he means to land, the food he requires, and how you will send word. Tell me who goes of his own choosing. Do not offer anyone Maynila's stores or its landing in exchange for help from Brunei. Those are not yours to promise.”

I could have argued that my grandfather would send men whether I promised him anything or not. I did not know that. I had not even found a boat.

Outside, the steward called for another pair of hands. I went to the doorway. The rower had stopped to adjust his grip on the jar; he waited until the steward was ready before they lifted again.

I left my mother with her counting board and the unopened box. At the landing I would have to ask the trader more questions. I had wanted to bring him an answer.
