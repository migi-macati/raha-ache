# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 5C: The Gold She Counted (revised assembly candidate)

*Studio-only replacement for Scene 5C, not an additional scene. Follows consolidated Scene 5AB and precedes consolidated Scene 5D. Ache narrates. The trader, fares, household dialogue and gold transfer are fictional.*

Bulkan was at the door, tying a length of cord into loops and pulling them apart.

“Your mother is with the steward,” he said.

“I have the trader's landing.”

“Then tell her before it changes.”

Inside, the cracked jar had been taken away. Two smaller jars stood in its place, each bound around the neck. My mother was asking the steward which household had agreed to carry them upriver. She let him finish before turning to me.

“The trader named his first landing,” I said. I repeated the name and the bank he meant to use. “A buyer has sent word. If the price is poor, he may go farther.”

“And if the buyer takes his goods?”

“Then I must find another carrier.”

“Will his men bring a message back?”

“No. I must arrange that myself.”

She waited. I told her the passengers would supply their own food and water. The crew's pot was not ours. The buyers still had to inspect the cargo before the trader would know whether he could take us.

“Us?” she asked.

“Sulad spoke to him. He has not decided to come.”

“Did you answer for him?”

“No.”

Bulkan stopped working the cord.

“He told the trader that carrying our own jars was one thing,” I said, “and working his cargo was another. He would not pay for passage and then be made crew whenever the trader wanted more hands.”

“What did the trader say?” Bulkan asked.

“He told Sulad to come back after the buyers had finished.”

My mother looked toward the jars. “That is more answer than you brought me yesterday.”

The steward shifted his board beneath one arm. “The merchants have begun hiring boatmen again,” he said. “One still asks whether the organizer may hold men against debts.”

“Have we seen his account?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we cannot say what those men owe him. Bring the account. And keep the rower's new wages separate from the first crossing.”

The rower with the pale mark on his hand had been paid to move our jars. Sima's silver had gone to the organizer for the earlier voyage; it had not reached the rowers. My mother's new record had changed neither fact.

The steward bowed and left. I watched him carry away a board full of other people's names. I had come to ask for gold.

“How much silver have you left?” my mother asked.

I opened my hand.

“And what did you pay to find the captain?”

“I did not find him.”

“I asked what you paid.”

I told her how the woman's brother had asked after the captain while carrying his own goods upriver. He had asked two men, found no one who knew where the captain had gone, and said I owed him nothing. I had offered payment afterward.

“You gave him silver when he had not asked for it?”

“He spent his time asking for me.”

“Did you agree to send him again?”

“No. He said the payment bought no second errand.”

She counted the coins with her eyes. “Then you paid for work already done. You did not buy an answer you wanted.”

I could not tell whether she approved. I had hoped she would ask less about the silver once she heard how little it had achieved.

She went to the chest and brought out the wooden box. Inside lay the broken gold loop on its cord and two smaller pieces wrapped separately.

“Bulkan,” she said, “stay.”

He had already turned toward the doorway.

She unwrapped one piece. “This is mine. The merchants' grain is not. Neither are the men at the landing.”

Bulkan looked at the gold. “And if he sends for us?”

“I cannot leave,” he told me before she could answer. “There are men here who trust my word because I have stood beside them. If I go, they must deal with whoever comes after me.”

“I haven't asked you to follow.”

“You had it in mind.”

I had. I had imagined him arriving at a strange shore as if he could walk away from Maynila without leaving a space behind him.

My mother set the piece of gold between us. “Take this. Do not hand it to a trader to hold a place he has not offered. If he gives you a fare for the number actually travelling, come back and tell me the amount.”

“And the other piece?”

“It stays here until I know what the first will buy.”

I picked up the gold. It was lighter than I had expected.

“If Sulad stays,” she said, “you must decide whether to go without him. If he comes, his decision is his own.”

Bulkan wound the cord around his fingers. “Ask the trader where passengers sit when the cargo gets wet. Ask who watches the jars.”

“Will you come to the landing?”

“No.”

He pulled the loop tight and put it away. My mother had given me enough to ask again. Neither of them had offered to ask in my place.
