# Book I, Chapter 7 — Speech across the landing (research and dialogue design)

**Status:** Studio-only research and craft note; 17 October 2026, Asia/Manila. **Scope:** language, interpretation and bargaining in Scenes 3–5. This is not a claim that any recorded historical person met Ache, Mulaw or Nima.

## What can be supported

- Laura Lee Junker's chapter “Economic specialization and inter-ethnic trade between foragers and farmers in the prehispanic Philippines” (in *Forager-Traders in South and Southeast Asia*, Cambridge University Press) describes linguistically distinct Philippine groups linked through exchange. Its accessible summary supports multilingual contact in broad terms, **not** a specific language spoken at this invented landing or any particular translation practice: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/foragertraders-in-south-and-southeast-asia/economic-specialization-and-interethnic-trade-between-foragers-and-farmers-in-the-prehispanic-philippines/65249FCAB23C4595002DA3DD3CC8138B .
- Julius C. Martinez, “Recovering Translingualism in Precolonial Philippines,” *International Journal of Multilingualism* 20(3) (2023), 1031–1051, DOI 10.1080/14790718.2021.1932909, uses the Laguna Copperplate Inscription to **suggest** precolonial translingual practices. The accessible abstract does not establish how a particular early-sixteenth-century crew communicated: https://www.fachportal-paedagogik.de/literatur/vollanzeige.html?FId=eric_ej1395142 .
- Marlon James Sales, “Translation and interpreting in the early modern Philippines: a preliminary survey,” *Perspectives* 26(1) (2018), 54–68, DOI 10.1080/0907676X.2017.1342668, studies translation in the **Spanish colonial** encounter. Its discussion is relevant to source criticism but cannot simply be projected backward to Ache's fictional setting. An accessible note identifies Malay as a language of commerce, citing Scott (1999, p. 193); verify that underlying Scott passage before asserting its scope or date: https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/translation-and-interpreting-in-the-early-modern-philippines-a-pr/ ; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0907676X.2017.1342668 .
- Birgit Tremml-Werner, “The Global and the Local in Early Modern Manila’s Communication Spaces,” in *Philippine Confluence* (2020), pp. 191–216, DOI 10.1017/9789400603660.009, explicitly cautions that surviving evidence for non-elite multilingual practice is scattered and frequently later than this novel's period. Do not use its Hokkien/Castilian colonial cases as direct proof of an earlier Visayan–Tagalog exchange: https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/8579E91CEA651B0C05074BE447C7FBD3 .

## Consequences for the fiction

**Scene 2 already signals partial comprehension.** The buyer repeats the mooring request for those ashore, Ache understands the buyer but hears the visiting young man's answer only in pieces, and the young man later chooses shorter words for Ache. This is useful groundwork, but it does not establish a common language or reliable mediation for every later bargain. Narrated English may translate dialogue only where the fiction shows who actually understands whom. Ache has some Malay from earlier encounters, but no established fluent command of an unidentified Visayan language. Sulad can be more attentive to work terms and gestures without becoming a magically universal interpreter. The unnamed buyer may have experience mediating exchanges, but must not automatically be assigned any specific linguistic repertoire without an explicit fictional choice.

For Scenes 3–5, distinguish *narrated English translation* from *in-world comprehension*. A compact interruption can show that the visitors initially understand a demand differently, with Nima correcting the interpretation by pointing to the wet bundle and the buyer to the occupied ground. Let the dispute hinge on a real ambiguous term (place to land vs place to store; paid labor vs right to move cargo), without fabricating a period Visayan or Malay word. Show who repeats, rephrases or gestures and whether a statement reaches its intended listener. Misunderstanding should add friction but not erase the separate legal/moral claims of the host, buyer, fisherman and Nima's absent sister.

**QA gate:** Before publishing Chapter 7, annotate in Studio the likely speaker/listener for each crucial offer, refusal and concession; establish the shared medium or mediation in the fiction. Do not assert that every character shares one language, or use modern Tagalog/Visayan expressions as verified sixteenth-century dialogue.
