# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 1A: Before the Light (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Direct continuation of Chapter 8 Scene 6; opening movement only, not a complete Chapter 9 scene. The first landing and next destination remain unnamed. The mat carrier's offer is conditional, Sulad's consent extends one leg, and no new food packet is assumed.*

I woke because Sulad was moving the jar.

He had tied our cloth around its neck. The knot held, but the loop slipped when he lifted it, and he put it down before the base could strike the stones. He had been trying to do it quietly. I watched him loosen the knot and begin again.

“Is it morning?”

“Not yet.”

The screen behind us was dark. The mat-mender had left no lamp burning. Across the water a man coughed, spat, and called for someone to move a pole. I could see the pale part of the sky beyond the boats, but not the tree the woman had pointed out.

Sulad drew the knot tight.

“Will it hold?” I asked.

“You can carry it by the body.”

“I thought she wanted it tied.”

“She wants it not to roll into her mats.”

I put my hand beneath the jar. It was heavier than I expected. The water inside moved against the side, then settled. Sulad had filled it the evening before. We had used some of it, but not enough to make carrying it easy.

He took the packet from beneath his folded cloth. I still had the half he had given me. I had not eaten it. We sat facing the bank and ate without talking. There was too little to make either of us full, and neither of us suggested keeping a portion for later.

The woman came while I was brushing crumbs from my fingers. Her brother followed with a pole over one shoulder.

“You are early,” she said.

“We were told to be.”

“I told you when to be here. I did not tell you the boat was ready.”

She went down to the water and tested the line holding the bow. Her brother stepped aboard. He shifted one of the bundles with his foot, then bent to lift it properly. The boat moved beneath him, and I saw how little room remained between the mat cargo and the side.

“Is there room?” I asked.

She did not answer until her brother had set the bundle down.

“There is room if you sit where I tell you. There is not room for your jar loose at your feet.”

“I can tie it.”

“Show me.”

I brought the jar to the bank. She watched me take the cloth from its neck and pass it around the middle. I made the knot I had used at home when I wanted something to look secure without knowing whether it was.

She pulled once. The cloth slid up.

“Again,” she said.

Sulad knelt beside me. I waited for him to take the jar, but he only held it steady. I passed the cloth beneath the narrower part near its base and crossed the ends above. When she pulled this time, the loop stayed where I had put it.

“You will keep it there,” she said, pointing to the space beside the stern support. “If it strikes the mats, you pay for what it spoils.”

“With what?” Sulad asked.

She looked at him.

“That is a question for him to answer before we go.”

I had no silver left that I could promise. I could have told her I knew people who would pay for me when I reached Brunei. I had no way to make those people hear her complaint, and no right to make her believe they would.

“If it comes loose,” I said, “I will hold it until we land.”

“And if you fall asleep?”

“Then we leave it ashore.”

Sulad looked at the jar. He had carried it up the path and down again, filled it, and tied it while I slept. He did not say that it was his to decide about. I had nearly decided for him all the same.

“Do you want it aboard?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said. “But I can hold it too.”

The woman stepped into the boat and showed us a space beneath the support where the jar could rest upright. Her brother wedged a piece of wood beside it. He made me lift it out and put it back without touching the mats.

“Now the last bundle,” she said.

I went for it. The mat-mender had come to the edge of his screen. He was watching Sulad, not me.

“You have time to stay,” he called.

“Not this morning,” Sulad answered.

“There will be another torn edge.”

“There always is.”

The man nodded and turned back to his work.

When I reached the screen, I stopped beside the bundle. The mat was bound with a strip like the one Sulad had tightened the night before. I put my hands beneath it instead of gripping the edge. At the water the woman's brother waited to receive the weight.

The tree was still dark against the sky. I could not see what lay beyond the next landing. I knew only that we had a place for this load, that it might carry us one leg, and that there would be work to do when we reached the other bank.
