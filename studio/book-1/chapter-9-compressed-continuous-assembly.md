# Book I, Chapter 9 — Continuous compressed pre-crisis assembly (Studio only)

*Assembly of four previously verified compression alternatives. All eleven original scenes and the four alternatives remain preserved. This is an editorial reading copy, not a public chapter; it ends at the larger carrier's departure before the central maritime crisis.*

I woke when the jar touched the stones.

Sulad had caught it before it fell. He set it down between his feet and untied the cloth around its neck. The water inside was still moving.

“Did I wake you?”

“You nearly woke everyone.”

“Then help me.”

The screen behind us was dark. The mat-mender had left no lamp burning, and across the water I could hear a man calling for his pole. Sulad held the jar while I passed the cloth beneath its narrow base. He tested the knot with one hand.

“Carry it by the body,” he said.

We ate the food he had saved from the mat-mender's payment. The portion he had given me the night before was still beneath my cloth. I had thought I might want it more in the morning. I did, but it was no larger for having waited.

The carrier arrived before the tree she had named was light enough to see. Her brother stepped into the boat and moved a bundle of mats. She watched him make room without helping.

“Can we come aboard?” I asked.

“When I know where you will sit.”

She examined the jar. I showed her the knot, and she pulled the cloth upward. It slipped to the neck.

“Again.”

Sulad held the jar steady while I tied it lower. This time the loop held. Her brother showed us a space beside the stern support and made me lift the jar in and out without touching the mats. I scraped my knuckles against the wood.

“If it comes loose, you pay for what it spoils,” the woman said.

I had little left with which to pay. I looked at Sulad. He had filled the jar and carried it down from the house, and I had nearly agreed to leave it behind without asking him.

“We can hold it between us,” he said.

“You can keep it where I put it,” she answered.

Her brother wedged a piece of wood against its base. He drew the cargo covering across the mats and told me to lift the edge while he passed a cord beneath. I pulled when the cloth caught. He tapped my wrist.

“Up. Not toward you.”

I lifted. The rough place let go.

The woman inspected the covering and the space where we would sit. Then she stood on the bank, watching a strip of foam turn beside a root.

“We are ready,” I said.

“The water isn't.”

I watched the foam come back a second time. Her brother planted his pole and waited. When the foam finally passed the root without returning, she nodded. He pushed; the man at the bow let the line run free.

The post slipped away from my knee. The screen where we had slept passed behind the stern. I had imagined leaving would feel like a decision. Instead I had a wet cord beneath my hand and a bundle of mats I was forbidden to shift.

We traveled where the banks sheltered the water. At a narrow reach two boats waited for one another. The brother changed the angle of his pole before the woman finished speaking. I kept my feet where she had placed them.

A corner of the covering lifted. I asked before touching it, then held it flat until the wind eased. A passing boatman called something I could not understand. He pointed toward the next bend.

“What did he say?” I asked.

“That a boat is waiting farther on.”

“Going where?”

“He didn't say.”

Sulad moved his feet to clear the brother's pole.

“Wait until you see it,” he told me.

Beyond the bend was a rack, two children working a long piece of wood in the shallows, and a house with closed shutters. Another boat lay tilted in the mud. None of them looked like an answer.

The carrier told us to stay seated. The person who had agreed to receive her mats had gone inland. A man at the rack did not know when he would return.

“We could unload them here,” I said.

“And leave them in the mud?” she asked.

I had been thinking of getting free to ask about the other boats. She had been thinking of the mats.

Her brother held us against the bank while the man cleared the rack. When the buyer finally appeared, he had mud on his feet and a coil of cord in his hands. He set a board across two supports and tested it before the woman allowed us to lift anything.

I carried one end of the first bundle. Sulad took the second with the woman's brother. The buyer made us turn a wet corner outward, examined the bindings and counted the mats. When he reached for my bundle, I held on until both his hands were beneath it.

“Will you keep them dry?” the woman asked him.

“I have them.”

“That was not my question.”

He looked at the rack, then at the water below it.

“They are mine to keep dry,” he said.

He counted her payment into her hand. She counted it again. Only then did she say our work was finished.

I could feel the cuts in my palms when I reached for our jar. The cloth had tightened while it was wet, and my fingers would not free the last turn. Sulad pushed the knot toward me instead of pulling against it. Together we brought the jar ashore.

The carrier had paid me for carrying the night before. She owed me nothing now. I watched her brother shave a splinter from his pole and wondered whether to ask where they were going next. He was busy, and I had no reason to think their next journey included us.

At the far end of the landing, men were unloading another boat. One passed a pot ashore. Another lifted a bundle, stopped, and sat down hard against the side. He held a cloth to his ribs. No blood showed, but he was breathing through his mouth.

“Is he hurt?” I asked.

“Ask him,” Sulad said.

We went closer. The man asked for water. Sulad set down our jar and asked the woman directing the crew whether he might give him some. She nodded. The man drank from his cupped hands and tipped what remained over his face.

The woman told him he would stay ashore. He argued that they needed hands at the next narrow reach. She said a man who could not stand would be another load to carry.

I looked at the space opening in their boat. It belonged to someone who had not wanted to leave it.

“Do you need another hand?” I asked.

“Perhaps. What can you do?”

I could have told her I knew boats. I knew instead how a mat covering caught on wood, how a jar moved when it was badly tied, and how much easier it was to hold a pole while another person chose where to plant it.

“I can lift,” I said. “I can keep a load steady if someone shows me how it is tied. I cannot replace him at the pole without being taught.”

She looked at Sulad.

“He is asking for himself,” Sulad said. “I haven't asked you for a place.”

“Come back when we've settled him,” she told me. “Then I'll know what work remains.”

“Will there be room for two?”

“I haven't offered room for one.”

We sat beside the rack, away from the mats we no longer had the right to touch. The sick man was lying in a strip of shade while his companions moved his belongings ashore.

I could see the empty place. I could not tell what filling it would cost.

The man they had put ashore called for his bundle before the crewman had carried it three steps.

“Mine,” he said.

The crewman set it beside him. A small knife in its sheath had worked free of the cloth. The man pushed it back inside, together with a coil of cord, and drew the bundle beneath his arm.

The woman who commanded the boat watched him try to rise. He got one foot under himself and stopped. His face had gone the color of the wet wood behind him.

“Lie down,” she said.

“I can work when we reach the narrow water.”

“You cannot reach the boat.”

He lowered himself, angry enough to look away from her. I had thought the space he left aboard was the answer to my question. Now the woman had to find somewhere for him to sleep, and I could see what an answer cost her.

She asked the mat buyer whether the man could stay beneath his rack. He would give them shade until he needed the boards, he said, but he would not watch a stranger through the night. The woman looked up the path and asked who lived in the first house.

A woman came down from it, wiping her hands on her skirt. She listened to the boat leader and asked the sick man whether he could walk. He said yes. When she asked him to stand, he could not do it alone.

“I can take him if two of you bring him to my door,” she said. “There will be water and food tonight, and a dry place until morning. After that we speak again.”

The boat leader counted payment into her hand. The house woman counted it too. The mat buyer refused to promise that another boat would collect the man; he would keep a name to give a passing crew, nothing more.

“Ask me,” the sick man said.

They did. He made them repeat what the money covered, then allowed two crewmen to help him up the path. He kept his bundle. They stopped once where the bank steepened. The leader watched until the house woman opened her door.

Only then did she return to the boat.

“You asked for work,” she told me.

“I did.”

“Show me what you know.”

Two jars stood beneath a crosspiece, held by a wet line that had tightened around them. She pointed to the outer jar. When a passing boat's wash struck the hull, it tapped the plank.

“Stop that.”

I pulled the knot and found it swollen hard. The sick man's knife came to mind before I remembered that he had taken it with him.

“May I cut the line?”

“And let both jars roll?”

I had no answer. She handed me a shorter cord.

I made a turn around the nearer jar. It pulled the farther one away from the wood. I undid it before she could tell me to. On my second try the cord slipped under my knee when the boat rocked. By the third, each jar rested against the crosspiece without dragging its neighbor.

I pressed the outer one with my palm. It moved a little.

“Will it hold?” she asked.

“I would check it after the first turn.”

“That is what you would do. I asked whether it will hold.”

“I think so.”

She called a crewman to test it. He leaned his weight against the jar, then pulled the free end of the cord.

“Leave it for loading,” he said.

The woman nodded, but she did not offer me the sick man's place. I could still see him on the path, his feet scraping between the two men who carried him. He had not given me anything by leaving.

A man came down from the houses with two baskets on a carrying pole. He named a trader the leader knew and said there was payment for another load.

“We counted what I would take,” she said.

“He says these belong with it.”

“Then he should have sent them when we counted.”

The man set down the baskets. Something heavy shifted inside one when its base struck the earth. The crewman lifted it by the binding, grunted, and set it down again.

I looked at the space beside the jars. “Could one go there?”

The woman turned toward me. “Who asked you to load it?”

“No one.”

The basket carrier smiled. “Another hand can make room.”

“A hand doesn't make the hull wider,” she said.

Her crewman thought one basket might fit if the jars moved beyond the crosspiece. He reached to show her, but his shoulder struck the gunwale before his fingers touched the fastening.

“If that cord catches?” she asked.

He tried from the other side. The basket carrier was standing where he needed to put his foot.

“Move,” the crewman told him.

He moved. The crewman tried again, then straightened without speaking.

“We keep the jars there,” the leader said. “Take both baskets back.”

The carrier tied them to his pole with quick, hard pulls.

“I'll tell him you refused his payment.”

“Tell him I refused his weight.”

I helped him lift the pole to his shoulder. He left without thanking me. The leader watched him go, then looked at the jars I had fastened.

“You wanted that load aboard.”

“I thought I could carry it.”

“You thought it might buy you a place.”

I had thought precisely that. The basket was still on its way up the path, and I wanted to point out that nothing had happened. She had refused it before it could harm anyone.

Her crewman found a worn place in the bowline. He called her over and ran the fibers between his fingers. She told him to bring her the sound end before he cut anything.

Sulad came to stand beside me. Our water jar was lighter than it had been when we arrived.

“You were looking at me,” he said.

“When?”

“When you asked about the basket.”

I remembered the words I had almost spoken: *We can carry it.* He had carried the mats without letting them touch the mud. That had made it easy to imagine his hands were mine to offer.

“I hadn't said it.”

“You were going to.”

I did not argue. He set our jar in the remaining shade.

“Would you take this boat?” I asked.

“I haven't heard where it goes.”

“Neither have I.”

“Then find that out. Ask me after.”

The leader called from the bow. Her crewman was holding up a length of line whose pale, rubbed fibers had begun to part. I went to her without asking Sulad to follow.

The woman held the worn bowline where the light passed through its broken fibers.

“Where would you tie this?” she asked.

I pointed to the timber beside the bow. She made me put my hand beneath it. A split ran along the wood, hidden from anyone looking down into the boat.

“It held before,” I said.

“So did that line.”

Her crewman pulled the rope through his hands, stopping where the fibers were pale. He could cut away the damaged part, but what remained would barely reach the landing post. A longer coil lay beneath the cargo covering.

“That one was counted with the goods,” the woman said when he reached for it.

He let it go. I had thought rope was rope. She had counted two kinds, and only one belonged to the boat.

“Where are you carrying the jars?” I asked.

“To the people who paid for them.”

“Would they know a boat going toward Brunei?”

“They might. I have not sold you a journey there.”

She turned the damaged length over once more. “If you want work on this boat, you watch the bindings and tell my crew when something loosens. You lift when they tell you. You do not take a pole because you have seen another man use one.”

“And the sick man's place?”

“He keeps what he earned. His sickness does not make his wages yours.”

I looked toward the path where the men had carried him. The house woman had been paid to keep him through the morning. He would still be there when we left, unless someone came to make another arrangement.

Sulad stood by our jar.

“I can carry the goods at the other end,” he told the woman. “I cannot promise to work a pole.”

“Then don't.”

“What will you give me?”

“Food after the jars are received. If the crew agrees to make room.”

“For my work,” Sulad said.

She nodded. I knew better than to answer for him. We waited while she sent a crewman to ask the buyer whether the two baskets she had refused were the last of his demands. He returned without the baskets and with an answer she made him repeat: there would be no more cargo today.

The woman went ashore to buy a sound line. She came back carrying a coil on her shoulder, the seller behind her counting coins and complaints. The crewman unrolled it from end to end, bending each stiff place with his fingers. He approved it only after he had inspected the whole length.

The two men who worked the boat looked at us, at our water jar, and at the place we would occupy. One lifted the jar and asked where we had filled it. Sulad told him. The man tasted a drop and set it down.

“We can take them for this delivery,” he said.

The other crewman looked at me. “If he stands where I need to work, I will tell you.”

“You will tell me first,” the woman answered.

He did not smile, but he agreed.

She showed us our places. Sulad asked how far the bundles must be carried at the other landing; the answer depended on whether the path was passable. He accepted that limit. I accepted the watching and lifting, and the meal afterward. Neither of us had bought a place beyond the delivery.

We filled the crew's water vessels upstream. The path was rough with roots. I came back with one vessel knocking against my hip, wanting to hurry before the woman changed her mind.

At the boat, the new line lay beside the jars. A crewman passed me its free end while he took the other around the bow.

“Keep it clear,” he said.

Sulad came past with his vessel. I moved aside. The line fell across the narrow way, so I made a loop and tucked it beneath the jar rack, where no one could tread on it.

The crewman called for the end. I pulled. It held fast.

I pulled again, and the loop tightened around the rack's foot.

“Stop,” the woman said.

The man came back along the planks. He saw what I had done before I found words for it.

“I told you to hold it clear.”

“It was in the way.”

“Then hold it.”

The rack could not be lifted with the jars aboard. The men carried two jars ashore, freed the rope, set the rack down again and tested the bindings. The woman counted everything a second time. A waiting boat pushed off while ours remained tied.

Nothing had broken. I watched the other stern recede and understood that I had spent part of the crew's daylight.

The woman looked at the sky. “We leave now, or the jars wait here until morning.”

She told the bowman to take his place. Another man checked the steering oar. No one offered me either job.

Sulad brought our jar aboard and settled it where his foot could steady it. I watched the crewman lay the new rope so it could run without catching. When the woman ordered us off, he brought the line in cleanly. The bank opened between us and the post.

“You could have told him where you put it,” Sulad said.

“I thought it was safer there.”

“I know.”

He said no more. He had put his foot beside the jar before I thought to watch it.

* * *

The covering above the jars held a little water in its lowest fold. When the boat leaned, the water ran toward the cargo.

I told the woman. She made me show the quiet crewman.

He lifted the fold outward. A thin stream ran over the side, and he weighted the cloth so it would stay. When we turned, the weight shifted. I called him again rather than reaching across the jars.

“Good,” he said. “You saw it.”

I had wanted praise for the thing I could do with my hands. He gave it for leaving them still.

We followed the sheltered water. In some reaches I could see houses between the trees. Elsewhere the growth met the bank and I could not tell whether anyone lived behind it. The men worked their poles where they could touch bottom, then changed their handling as the water deepened. I tried to learn from the movements, but each turn showed me another thing I had not noticed.

At the landing, a buyer waited beneath a leaf roof. He looked first at the boat, then at the crew.

“You have fewer hands,” he said.

“I have brought your jars,” the woman answered. “Show me where you will receive them.”

The raised floor stood above a narrow patch of damp ground. She walked the path herself before she allowed us to lift. Sulad asked whether the work ended at the bank or at the floor.

“At the floor,” she said. “That is where he counts.”

We carried the jars in three journeys. The quiet crewman took the heavier end of mine. Sulad worked with the other man. The buyer cleared a place for each jar and would not let us set it down before he was ready. When the last stood in line, he examined the rims and counted. The crewman counted after him.

The numbers agreed.

The buyer marked the tally and paid the woman. He tried to offer another load while she counted the payment.

“Not today,” she said.

“You have new men.”

“For the work that is finished.”

She sent us down to the water with our own jar. When she came after us, she handed Sulad and me a packet of food each, wrapped in leaves. I waited until he had opened his before opening mine. He had earned his packet himself; neither was a share from the mat carrier's earlier payment.

We ate on the bank. The woman and her crew ate nearby. When she rose, I asked whether she would speak for us to another boat.

“I can tell them what I saw,” she said.

“That I carried the jars?”

“That you carried them, watched the covering, and reported the water. I can also tell them you trapped a new rope beneath the rack and made us unload two jars to free it.”

I looked at the cord burns across my palm. She was giving me the account I had earned, though it was not the one I wanted.

Sulad asked whether she would take us back if we found no other passage.

“If I have room, you may ask again,” she said. “There is no work for you on the return.”

He thanked her. He had asked about a possibility, not mistaken it for an offer.

Beyond the landing, a larger vessel lay in deeper water. People passed goods to it in smaller loads. A woman kept count beside a bundle too wide to fit between the others. She told its carrier to take it back and divide it.

I approached. She looked up before I spoke.

“Can you lift that?” she asked.

“Not alone without dragging it.”

“Then you have better eyes than the man who wrapped it.”

Sulad joined me, setting our jar in the shade.

“Are you together?” she asked.

“We travel together,” he said. “We bargain for ourselves.”

She looked from one of us to the other, then returned to her tally. A man farther out called for the next load. She made him wait until the divided bundle came back.

“What are they carrying?” I asked.

“Goods that have owners. People will want them dry when they arrive.”

“Could the master use another pair of hands?”

“When he has finished counting, ask him. First learn what he needs carried.”

The boat that had brought us was preparing to leave. The woman who commanded it stood by the bow while her crewman bound the sound end of the old rope. She had found a use for what remained after the worn part was cut away.

I sat beside our jar to wait for the master. The food was gone, and I had no place on the larger vessel. I knew one thing I could do aboard a boat, and one mistake its crew would hear about if they asked.

The wide bundle came back from the path as two parcels.

The carrier set them beside the tally keeper. A strip of red cloth showed beneath one wrapping. She put her board on her knee and counted again.

“It was one when I took it,” the carrier said.

“Then it was too wide to carry between the others.”

“The cloth is the same.”

“The hands that receive it will be different.” She made a second mark.

Sulad sat beside our jar. We had already eaten the food we earned carrying the jars. The woman who commanded that boat was preparing to leave, and I had watched her crew put away the sound remainder of their old rope. I did not know whether she would tell this tally keeper how I had caught her new one beneath a rack.

A narrow boat grounded near us. A man stepped out without helping to unload it. A pale mark ran from his ear to his jaw.

“Are the goods ready?” he asked.

“Except for the parcel that became two,” the tally keeper said. “Daya is coming to see them aboard.”

“Then we wait.”

I stood. “Are you master of the larger boat?”

“Of the boat. Not this bank.”

“We are looking for passage.”

He looked at our jar. “What can you do for it?”

“Carry a load. Watch a fastening if someone shows me what to watch.”

“Can you fetch it from the water if you drop it?”

“No.”

“Then choose what you offer to carry.”

The tally keeper glanced up. “He knew the bundle was too wide before the carrier tried to force it through.”

The master looked at me again. “What else have you done?”

I could have named the jars delivered without damage. I could have left out the time lost at the bow.

“I put a rope where it caught beneath a rack,” I said. “The crew had to lift two jars to free it.”

“Why did you put it there?”

“I thought I was keeping it out of their way.”

“And now?”

“I would ask where they wanted it.”

He did not say whether that was enough. He turned to Sulad.

“Do you work for him?”

“No.”

“Then what will you do?”

“I will see the work before I promise it.”

The master told the tally keeper to show us the loading. She walked us to the small boat, where men were passing goods toward the larger vessel. The covering aboard had been lifted to make room. I could see the mast and the sail gathered against it, but not how the goods lay beneath the cloth.

“Nothing goes out until I mark it,” she said. “If they call for another load before they are ready, they can wait.”

The master left her to it.

A woman came down the path with nothing in her hands. She knelt beside the divided cloth and opened the edge with the red strip.

“Who made this two?”

“The gap was too narrow,” the tally keeper answered. “I would not let them crush it.”

The woman felt the fold, then closed it.

“I am Daya,” she told the master. “I paid to have this delivered dry. I will not pay for two deliveries because someone has made two parcels.”

“One owner's lot,” he said. “Two parcels to count.”

“And if only one arrives?”

“The receiver will know which hands took both.”

Daya looked at the tally keeper until she showed her the two marks. Only then did she let the carrier lift the first parcel.

I thought of the jar buyer who had counted every rim before accepting our load. Daya had not seen a jar or the lost daylight. Her cloth could be damaged while she stood here, watching other people make decisions about it.

The first parcel went out. A carrier reached for the second, but the tally keeper stopped him.

“Tell me where they have put the first.”

“It has gone aboard,” he said.

“That is not a place.”

The narrow boat returned with a man who had helped stow it. He told her which bundles stood on either side. She made him say it twice. Then she sent the second.

Daya watched the little boat move away. “You wanted work?” she asked me.

“Yes.”

“Then learn which goods will be blamed on you if they arrive wet.”

“I would tell the crewman responsible.”

“Tell him while he can still do something.”

She went to speak with the master. Sulad waited for the next empty carrying frame.

The tally keeper offered him a share of the carriers' food for helping bring the last load down. He asked whether it came from the goods' owner or the crew's stores. When she answered, he accepted the work. He did not bargain for a berth he had not seen.

I helped clear the path without taking either of Daya's parcels in my hands. When the last load had gone, the master crossed to the larger vessel to see how much space remained. We waited beside our jar.

Sulad came back with dust on his palms. The tally keeper handed him a leaf packet.

“For the loading,” she said. “From the owner's food.”

He opened it and looked inside before she marked her board.

“That settles the work here,” he said.

“It does.”

He ate under the shade. I had no part of that packet. He had agreed to the loading without me, and the food was his.

From the bank Daya called toward the larger boat. A man had raised the covering and was moving the red-edged parcel away from the side. The master stood beside him, pointing to a drier place farther in. The second parcel had not yet been set beside it.

“Both must be there when they unload,” Daya told the tally keeper.

“They are counted separately,” the woman said. “The receiver will take them one at a time.”

“And if they are not together?”

“I know where each was put.”

Daya pressed her lips together. It was not the answer she wanted, but she stopped calling.

The master returned when the small boat had come back empty.

“There is room for two,” he said. “Only as far as the receiving creek. A house there takes the goods. I cannot promise you another boat from it.”

“Is that toward Brunei?” I asked.

“The cloth is going to its receiver. That is where I am going.”

I wanted a different answer. He had none to give.

He spoke to Sulad first. There was water to carry for the men working near the sail and water to empty from the bottom when ordered. A crewman would show him where to sit and how to pass the vessel used for lifting it. He was not to touch the steering paddle.

“And food?” Sulad asked.

“You eat with the crew while you work. No silver. We settle at the creek.”

“The loading here is already paid.”

“Yes.”

“Then I agree to the work aboard.”

The master faced me. “You watch the covering. The man assigned to it will show you the folds. You call him if water collects or a parcel moves. You do not reach under the cloth or try to mend a fastening yourself.”

I remembered the new line tightening around the rack's foot.

“If he cannot hear me?” I asked.

“You call again. If a man is in danger, warn him first. Do not decide that a parcel is worth more than his body.”

“I agree.”

“Tell me what you will do if water runs toward the cloth.”

“Call the crewman. Show him where.”

He nodded toward the small boat. “Bring your jar. You keep it safe yourselves.”

We boarded last, after the goods. Sulad asked where our jar would stand before he let the man above take it. The man showed him a place clear of the bundles, and Sulad kept one hand on its neck until we had found our seats.

Close to the larger vessel I could see worn places in the planks and seams I had not noticed from shore. I could not have named the way it was built. A man helped me aboard and led me beneath the lifted edge of the covering.

“There,” he said, pointing. “Daya's two. One with the red edge, one beside the small brown bundle. Not tied together. They have to come out separately.”

He showed me where the outer fold should hang so that water ran away from the goods. At the other end a tucked corner could turn inward.

“If it turns, you call me. Do not pull the whole cloth loose to fix a corner.”

I crouched where he told me. When he lowered the covering, I could still see its edge without leaning on the parcels.

Sulad was farther aft, passing a small water vessel to the man who would supervise him. The man corrected his grip and made him try again. Sulad did not look toward me for permission.

The tally keeper boarded with her board inside her cloth. She and the covering man counted the parcels aloud. Daya stood on the bank until both had agreed.

“Two parcels,” she called.

“One lot,” the tally keeper answered. “Two places known.”

Daya remained ashore.

The master went to the stern, where another man held the steering paddle. A crewman waited by the gathered sail. The small boat pulled clear. Someone called for the last fastening to be freed.

The larger vessel moved slowly between the boats at anchor. The sail opened, sagged, and opened again. I watched the fold above Daya's parcels lift and fall. It lay where the crewman had shown me.

Sulad pointed at our jar. Its base had begun to slide toward the nearest bundle. I moved it back into the space the crew had given us. The tally keeper saw me do it and returned to her board.

Beyond the last anchored boat, the bank drew away. A gust caught the covering near my knee and turned the corner inward.

I called the crewman.

He came, watched it rise again, and put the fold back where it belonged. I kept my hands on my knees.

“Did you move the parcels?” he asked.

“No.”

“Good. Keep looking.”

Above us the sail made a hard sound. The master called to the men working it, and they eased it. Sulad sat low while the crew moved around him.

I did not know whether the wind would grow stronger. I knew only that the fold had turned once and might turn again.

The landing had grown too small for me to pick out Daya or the woman with the jars. The tally keeper still knew where the cloth lay. The master knew where he had agreed to carry it. Sulad knew the work he had chosen. I watched the edge of the covering and waited for the next lift of wind.
