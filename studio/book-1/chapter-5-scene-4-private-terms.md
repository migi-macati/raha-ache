# Book I, Chapter 5 — Scene 4: The Stores (Studio working prose)

The steward had not returned. His place at the tally board was empty, and the day's marks ended halfway down the wood.

My mother was in the storehouse, watching two women turn out a basket of grain. One held it steady while the other searched the bottom with her fingers. They were looking for damp. When the basket was lifted again, a little pale dust fell through the weave.

"Leave that one open," my mother said. "It will spoil faster if we pretend it is dry."

I waited until the women had carried the basket aside.

"I need food for a boat."

"Which boat?"

"One from the fishing bank."

"Whose?"

"Not mine."

She looked at me then. "I had understood that much."

"Sulad's uncle will carry a message across. He wants food for the work."

"To Tondo?"

"Perhaps farther, if we find someone who knows the way."

"Someone you have not found."

"Not yet."

She brushed grain dust from her palm. "How much did you promise him?"

"I said I would ask."

"That is not what I asked."

I looked at the open basket. "I told him I could bring food from our stores."

"And he agreed?"

"He asked whether you had given it to me."

She waited.

"I said no."

"Then he knows the answer before you do."

I felt heat rising in my face. "He offered a crossing. That is more than anyone else has offered me today."

"Your teacher offered to watch the landing."

"One boat. Watching."

"That is what you asked him to do."

"I asked him for men who would stand there."

"To stop the merchant who crossed this morning?"

"No."

"To stop the men he might hire?"

"If they mean to take our work."

She set her hand on the rim of the basket. "A man taking wages for carrying another man's goods is not stealing your shore."

"I know the difference."

"Then tell me whom your men would stop."

I could see Bulkan's carpenter waiting beside the damaged hull. I could hear the young rower's paddle strike the sand.

"The organizer keeps what he owes his own sister's son," I said. "We know it, and the merchant still crosses to hire his people."

"That debt is not yours to erase."

"Nor his to hide."

"No." She let the word stand. "The steward will ask about wages as well as boats. I sent him because the terms matter. He has not come back. Neither of us knows what Tondo will demand."

"And if their price is too high?"

"Then we decide what we can pay."

"And if every merchant goes to them while we decide?"

"Then I will have been too slow."

She said it without looking away. I had wanted her to deny the danger. Her admitting it made the room feel smaller.

"I can go south," I said.

"With Sulad's uncle's fishing boat?"

"With a captain who has been there. I met him upriver."

"When?"

I told her. I told her he spoke Malay and had seen the Brunei court, that he knew men who traded there. I did not tell her he had promised to take me. He had promised nothing.

"Where is he now?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Then you cannot go with him."

"I can find him."

"How?"

That was the question I had been keeping for last. A messenger could ask along the river. A merchant might know which vessels were moving south. Every person who helped would know what I intended, and each could choose whom to tell.

"I have to try," I said.

She picked up a handful of grain and let it run back into the basket.

"You speak as though trying costs only you."

"It is my mother's kin I would ask."

"My kin," she said. "And your grandfather's household is not a place where you arrive empty-handed and make demands because you know the names of the dead."

I looked at her hands. There was grain in the lines of her fingers.

"I thought you would want me to go."

"I want you alive. I want this landing to belong to the people who use it. I want the men who carry our goods to be paid. None of those wants makes the others disappear."

She called one of the women back and told her to set aside enough dry grain for a single paid crossing, should the steward's terms make one necessary. Nothing was to be taken until she gave the word.

"That is all?" I asked.

"That is what I can authorize without promising another household's supper."

"And the boat south?"

"Find the captain. Learn what he asks. Then come to me with something more than a wish."

I wanted to say I would leave whether she allowed it or not. Instead I watched the woman cover the grain. She tied the basket shut carefully, as if it were already going somewhere.

My mother turned back to the tally board.

"You may go," she said.

I had come to ask for food. I left knowing the first thing I would need to find was someone willing to carry my words.
