# Book I, Chapter 9 — Scene 8: What the Cover Held (Studio prose candidate)

*Ache narrates. Continues Scene 7 at the unnamed delivery landing. The larger vessel's master, cargo owner and tally keeper establish separate obligations before considering Ache and Sulad. The vessel's next destination is not identified; there is no departure, confirmed Brunei passage or weather crisis.*

The woman who had been counting bundles told us to wait until the last of them came back.

The wide one had gone up the path in a carrier's arms. When he returned, it was two bundles, each wrapped tightly enough to hold its shape. He put them beside the others and looked at her as if the work of dividing them had settled the matter.

“You brought back two,” she said.

“It was one before.”

“It is two now.”

“The cloth is the same.”

She made him move them apart. Beneath one covering a narrow strip of red showed at the edge. She pressed the wrapping down with her thumb, then took the small board from her belt and marked it.

I had counted the bundles while he was gone. I had been proud to know how many there were. Now there was one more than before, and I did not know whether the woman had counted the cloth, the wrappings, or the number of times a man would have to bend to lift them.

Sulad sat beside our jar. He had eaten his meal more slowly than I had, and there was still a folded leaf in his hand.

“Do you think she has forgotten us?” I asked.

“She told us to wait.”

“That does not mean she will remember why.”

“She has counted everything else.”

A narrow boat came away from the larger vessel. Two men worked it toward the bank. Between them sat a man with a pale mark running from his ear to his jaw. He had no bundle and no pole. When they grounded the boat, he stepped out and walked straight to the woman with the board.

“Are these ready?” he asked.

“All except the one they made into two.”

He looked at the two parcels. “Then you have another piece to count.”

“I have another piece to keep dry.”

The man bent over the red edge. He did not touch it.

“Who owns this?”

“Daya,” the woman said. “She is coming to see it put aboard.”

He stood. “Then we wait for Daya.”

I rose. Sulad stayed seated.

“Are you the master?” I asked.

The man looked past me at the jar, then at the shore behind us.

“Of the boat out there. Not of the landing.”

“I am looking for passage.”

“So is every bundle here. What are you carrying?”

I pointed to our jar and the cloth tied around the few things that remained to us.

“And what will you give the boat for carrying them?”

“Work.”

“What work?”

“I can carry a load. I can watch a fastening if someone shows me which one.”

“Can you swim to fetch a bale if you let it fall?”

“No.”

“Then do not offer to carry what you cannot hold.”

I had answered the question he asked. I could hear that it was not the answer he wanted.

The tally keeper held out her board. “He noticed the wide bundle would not pass between the others. He did not try to force it.”

“That is something,” the master said.

“It is what he did,” she answered.

He asked Sulad to stand. Sulad put down the folded leaf and came forward without the jar.

“Are you his man?”

“No.”

“Then why are you together?”

“We have been taking the same road.”

“Water is not a road.”

“We know that now,” Sulad said.

The master considered him a moment. “What work will you do?”

“Whatever I agree to after I see it.”

The man almost smiled. He turned to the tally keeper.

“Show them the loading.”

She led us to the water's edge. The narrow boat was tied close to the bank, but the larger one remained out where a man could stand in its hull without grounding it. Men on the larger vessel were making space beneath a covering. I could see a mast and the sail gathered against it. I could not tell from shore how the bundles were held below the covering, or how much water lay in the bottom.

“Nothing goes aboard until I have marked it here,” she said. “Nothing comes out of the small boat until they are ready to receive it there. If you hear a man calling to hurry, you wait for my word.”

The master watched her speak. He did not correct her.

A woman approached carrying no goods. Her hands were stained near the nails, and she walked as though the path belonged to her until she reached the two bundles. She knelt beside them, opened the edge that showed red, and inspected the cloth within.

“Who tied this?” she asked.

The carrier raised a hand.

“Who told you to make one into two?”

“The counter.”

“The gap was too narrow,” the tally keeper said. “I would not have him press it through.”

The woman closed the wrapping again.

“My name is Daya,” she told the master. “Those two are mine. I paid to have the cloth delivered dry. I will not pay twice because someone counts it differently.”

“You will not,” he said. “The count will say two parcels, one owner's lot.”

“And if one arrives without the other?”

“You will know whose hands received them.”

He nodded to the tally keeper, who made a fresh mark beside the first. Daya watched until the board was put away.

I had thought that the bundle's width was a small matter, something a man could solve by pushing harder. Now there were two parcels to lose, and a woman who would know exactly what was missing.

Daya turned to me.

“Do you belong to the crew?”

“Not yet.”

“Then why are you standing where they load?”

“I am asking for work.”

“Ask what they will put in your hands before you agree to it.”

She went back up the path. The master waited until she was out of hearing.

“I need someone to watch the covering when we are moving,” he said. “Not to retie the cargo. Not to tell the steersman where to go. If water gathers inside, you call the man responsible for it. If a parcel shifts, you call. Can you do that without putting your hands where they do not belong?”

I thought of the new rope caught beneath the jar rack. The woman who had carried us had told the truth about it. I was glad she had not stayed to hear this question.

“I have failed at that before,” I said.

“Then why should I take you?”

“Because I know what happened when I failed. And because I will tell you if I see it happening again.”

The master looked toward the larger boat. A man there had lifted the covering to make room for another parcel. He was waiting for the narrow boat to come alongside.

“That man will show you the place,” the master said. “He will decide when you may touch anything. You do not replace him.”

“I understand.”

“You understand the words. We will see about the work.”

He turned to Sulad.

“There is lifting between the small boat and the larger one. You can help here. Whether you go farther depends on whether the crew has room after the goods are aboard.”

“Would I earn passage by the lifting?” Sulad asked.

“Not if there is no place for you to sit.”

“Then I will not begin on the promise of a place you cannot give.”

The master looked at the tally keeper. She nodded toward the narrow boat.

“We can take another load ashore first,” she said. “Then we will know what room is left.”

“I will help with that load,” Sulad said, “for a share of the food they give the carriers. We can discuss passage when the count is done.”

The tally keeper told him what the carriers were to receive. He asked whether it came from the crew's stores or from the owner of the goods. She said it came from the owner. Only then did he agree.

I had wanted to say that we should bargain together so neither of us would be left behind. Sulad had made a bargain that would feed him even if the master refused him a place. I was the one who had not asked what would happen if there was no room.

The men in the narrow boat pushed away with the first load. Sulad waited for their return. I stayed by the tally keeper as she marked each parcel that left the bank.

One of Daya's two bundles went out with the next load. The other remained beside us. The master told a carrier to take it as well, but the tally keeper stopped him.

“Not until they tell us where they put the first,” she said.

“They are the same lot,” he answered.

“That is why I need to know.”

We waited for the narrow boat to return. When it did, the man who had received the first parcel told us where he had laid it and what stood beside it. Only then did the tally keeper release the second.

I could have called the delay foolish. The cloth would have been just as dry if we had sent both bundles at once. But the woman had given each a place in someone's memory before letting it leave her sight.

The master came back from the water.

“If the load sits as it should, I can take two more people for the next leg,” he said. “You will each hear your work from the person who commands it. You will eat from the crew's stores while you work. There is no silver, and I promise no passage after the next landing.”

“Where is that?” I asked.

“A place where the goods have a receiver.”

“Will it bring us nearer Brunei?”

“It may bring you nearer a boat that goes there. I will not sell you a destination I have not agreed to sail to.”

I looked at the larger vessel. Its sail was still tied. The men were arranging cargo beneath the covering, and the small boat had another journey to make. We had not left the bank. We had not even been told to board.

“Then I want to know where the goods are to be received,” I said. “Before I agree.”

The master looked at me for a long moment. I was afraid he would tell me to seek another boat.

“Good,” he said at last. “Ask the tally keeper when she has finished. She has the owner's instructions. I have the water to consider.”

He went back to the narrow boat.

Sulad returned carrying an empty frame. He set it down carefully and asked whether I had found a place.

“A possible one.”

“Then we both have possible places.”

“Yours may be different from mine.”

“Yes,” he said. “They usually are.”

The tally keeper called for the last parcel. I helped her clear a path to the water without lifting it. When the carrier passed, I saw the red edge beneath Daya's wrapping. The other half of her cloth was already out of sight.

I knew whose goods they were now. I knew who had counted them, and who would be asked to answer if they came ashore wet. I did not yet know what the water between this landing and the next would ask of us.
