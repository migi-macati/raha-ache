# Book I, Chapter 6 — Scene 4B: The Work He Would Leave

*Studio working prose; follows Scene 4A. Ache narrates. No confirmed berth or unconditional agreement to travel.*

I sat on my heels. Sulad kept working. The cord he wore was tucked beneath his cloth; I caught sight of it when he reached across the net.

His uncle said, “If he goes, I lose hands at the net. His cousins can come some mornings, but they have their own work. This boat cannot follow him down the coast. If he does not return, I will have to find another way to bring in the catch.”

“I could pay someone.”

“You do not know who is free. Neither do I.”

“I could work here until the trader is ready.”

Sulad stopped threading the fiber. “You have never finished a net.”

“I can learn.”

“By the time you learn, the trader may be gone.”

“Then what do you want?”

“I want you to stop speaking as though there is a place waiting for us.”

He said *us* without looking at me. I held on to the word and nearly missed what came after.

“I might go,” he said. “If there is a boat and if I know what work the crew expects. If they want me to leave before we have finished this net, I will not go. If they want me to promise work beyond the first landing, I will ask them myself.”

“And if they refuse?”

“Then I stay.”

His uncle nodded, as though Sulad had said something about the weather.

I could have offered to wait. I wanted to. But the trader had said he would hold no space, and I had no right to promise what he would do.

“I will ask him,” I said.

“Ask him about the water too,” Sulad said. “And who carries it. You heard his price for a passenger. Did you hear what he charges a man who can work?”

“I didn't ask.”

“Then perhaps you should.”

His uncle took the repaired edge from Sulad and stretched it between his hands. One knot slipped. Sulad swore under his breath and reached to mend it again.

“Leave that one,” his uncle said. “I'll do it.”

“You said you wanted it finished.”

“I want it finished properly.”

Sulad stood and washed his hands in the shallows. He came back with water running from his elbows and looked at me as if he had something else to say. His uncle had bent over the knot. We were close enough to speak quietly.

“You told me you wanted me,” Sulad said.

I felt my face warm. “With me.”

“I remember.”

He reached past me for the basket. His fingers brushed mine. I could not tell whether he meant them to.

“I haven't said yes,” he said.

“I know.”

“You keep looking pleased.”

“I lost your fish.”

“One fish.”

“I'm sorry.”

He lifted the basket. “Then come tomorrow and help bring in another. If you're still here.”

I watched him carry it up the bank. I had come expecting an answer about a journey. Instead I had been given work for the morning, and no promise that I would be allowed to miss it.
