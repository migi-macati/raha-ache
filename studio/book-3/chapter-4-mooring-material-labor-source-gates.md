## Verified chronological boundaries

1. **May 1570, direct but interested account.** The anonymous *Relation of the Voyage to Luzon*, in Blair and Robertson, *The Philippine Islands*, vol. III, describes a waterfront palisade and guns at Maynila's gates, and later reports iron, copper, unfinished cannon and clay/wax molds in a storehouse near Sulayman's house. The reporter belongs to the invading party; descriptions of the fortifications are a **1570** snapshot, not evidence that Ache built the same palisade decades earlier. The text does not identify Panday Pira as a workshop owner, date the workshop's founding, or provide a reliable plan of the town. Directly inspected English transcription: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/13616/pg13616-images.html (the relation, printed pp. 74–83). The account also describes tide-sensitive approach and local sounding; these are later evidence for the importance of navigational knowledge.
2. **1589, later ethnography.** Juan de Plasencia's *Customs of the Tagalogs*, in Blair and Robertson, vol. VII, describes some chiefs' defined fisheries and river-market rights. It also says that freeborn *maharlica* owed certain services, including rowing and helping build a chief's house, with food supplied. Other dependents had different, unequal obligations. Therefore **"every worker is a free wage laborer" is an anachronistic simplification**; so is the opposite assumption that Ache may command any worker or any household's wood. The relation is later, mediated, and cannot be projected as a uniform Maynila statute in Ache's youth. Directly inspected English transcription: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13701/13701-h/13701-h.htm (Plasencia's 1589 account, printed pp. 173ff).
3. **Scott's synthesis, carefully scoped.** William Henry Scott, *Barangay: Sixteenth-Century Philippine Culture and Society* (1994), includes distinct sections on Visayan woodworking, housebuilding, boats and labor, and on Tagalog technology and society. The National Museum library catalog verifies the book's organization (https://library.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/cgi-bin/koha/opac-detail.pl?biblionumber=4138). **No passage from the Tagalog chapter or original page-specific claim about this Maynila landing has yet been directly collated for this packet.** Do not import a Visayan joinery term, tool, fastening, tree species or labor rank into Ache's scene as established local usage without checking the relevant pages.

## Physical and political constraints from published canon

Chapter 3 ends with a **sounding marker** driven after a witnessed depth test, explicitly distinct from a **mooring post**. The lower bank is soft, a post needs bracing, and vessels must use the upper post until that work is done. A representative warns that his owner has already supplied wood for the marker and will resist more uncompensated labor. Dala and Pawi are still testing a repaired net frame; not all losses from the grounded fish boat have been settled. The keeper controls the landing's measured accounts; Amaya controls the hall's stores. Sulad remains an independent worker; Harun has left. A messenger/porter asked who would pay him and Ache went to face the owner. **Scene 1 must pick up that question**, rather than restart the conflict as if Chapter 3 had not ended.

No army, captive workforce, permanent navy, palace treasury, guaranteed Bruneian shipment, completed defensive wall, or early Panday Pira gunfoundry has been established. A builder may propose a sheltered store or small timber barrier; its engineering is **fictional reconstruction** pending stronger dated evidence. Use a maker who has practical experience with posts, water and loading, rather than asserting precise historic dimensions, fasteners, timber treatments or construction vocabulary.

## Causal six-scene working plan

1. **The Post That Would Not Hold:** Ache accompanies the porter to an owner who wants to unload now. A crew member shows why the new sounding stake cannot take a line. The upper berth is occupied by another household's cargo. Ache must ask permission to shift loads, negotiate the porter’s pay, and make a public, limited promise of an inspection, not a completed post by tomorrow. End with a named owner insisting on an accountable repair date.
2. **Wood Belonging to Someone:** The easiest timber belongs to a different undertaking. Amaya can refuse diversion of household supplies; Dala can insist the shore work not block fishing access. Different service obligations must be identified rather than flattened into modern employment or compulsory corvée. End with a supplier's concrete refusal or conditional offer.
3. **The Maker's Price:** A fictional craftsperson tests the soil and load, rejects a convenient but unsound arrangement, and bargains for materials, food, helpers and time. Sulad may assist by choice. A promised delivery fails because its actual owner was not consulted. **Do not introduce Panday Pira by name without a defensible chronological source.**
4. **The Water Tests It:** A specific loading or bracing decision fails under a real tide or vessel's pull. Injury or lost cargo belongs to someone with a voice; make its consequence and accountability particular. Ache orders a halt he cannot easily afford.
5. **The Work No One Owed:** An influential owner offers a universal passing charge or service call as a quick fix. Keeper and far-bank witnesses distinguish **landing use** from **mere passage**, as established in Chapter 3. Some households may already owe defined services; their obligations still need to be specified and contested. Ache accepts a smaller work funded through named contributions and agreed service.
6. **What the Bank Could Keep:** Test the repaired berth under a bounded load. Pay or witness the remaining obligation. The structure works at one tide for one class of vessel; it is not an impregnable kota. One powerful owner withdraws and an unpaid claim remains for Chapter 5. Revisit the reader title: *The Making of the Kota* promises too much if only the first defensible berth is completed.
