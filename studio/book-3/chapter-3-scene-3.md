# Book III, Chapter 3 — Scene 3: A Crossing With No Passenger

**Packet:** RA-B3C3-SCENE-03. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 2's agreed warning watches, Banu's paid invitation and the disputed stake still in place. Harun has departed; Amaya controls the hall's stores; no Tondo household has conceded a passage toll. **Historical boundary:** The encounter, terms and dialogue are fiction. Lakandula's name is used as a political reference reported by characters, without asserting a documented meeting, exact office or genealogy.

---

Banu came back with the cloth tied around his wrist.

I had left it beneath a bowl in the hall, where I had told him to find it. He had found it there and brought it back.

"Did the man refuse it?" I asked.

"I have not offered it. He would have thought you sent me to buy his answer."

"Then what did you tell him?"

"What you told me to tell him. A stake has moved. A boat has grounded. The people who use the passage are asked to see what was measured."

"And?"

"He said his boat is not yours to summon."

I waited.

"He said he would send a woman who knows the landing, if she can leave her work. That is all he promised."

Banu untied the cloth and placed it on the bench. A corner had darkened with sweat. I wanted to tell him he could keep it for the journey already made.

"You still have to pay me," he said.

"I know."

"Then find something I can carry home. Cloth is no use to me if every man I show it to asks whose favor I have sold."

The keeper had heard us from the doorway. She went to the store rack, came back with a small basket of dried fish, and set it on the floor.

"From my own share," she said. "Not from the landing repair count."

Banu lifted the basket and tested its weight. He said it would do for one crossing, not for another journey after dark. He took it away before I could promise him anything else.

We were to meet on a strip of bank where neither of the two trial passages reached the shore. It was a place where ferries waited when a boat was already unloading below. No one called it neutral. It was simply too narrow to hold much cargo, and too exposed to the current to make a good place for a store.

The keeper told me to bring my own drinking water.

"Will the men from the other bank bring theirs?" I asked.

"I do not know. I know whose jars are in the hall."

Amaya stood behind her, cutting a strip from a damaged mat. She had been repairing the sleeping place of a child whose family still used the hall.

"The other men may ask for food," I said.

"They may."

"Would you give it?"

"If they are hungry, I will feed them as visitors. You will not take the grain we put aside for the workers and offer it as a pledge for an agreement they have not made."

"I did not ask for a pledge."

"You were looking at the jars."

She went back to the mat. I had been looking at them.

The keeper lent me the old sounding cord on the condition that she carried it herself. Sulad agreed to come only after the man who had hired him to mend a net released him for the afternoon. I offered to pay the difference.

"You have not finished paying Banu," he said.

He went to tell the net owner where he would be. I waited on the steps, watching the two warning men call the deeper passage to a canoe with three children and a load of green leaves. The canoe passed without a charge. The man from the first hearing raised his hand to the woman at the stern; she did not raise hers back.

We crossed in the ferry owner's boat. He charged the usual fare. He would take us to the waiting bank, then continue with his passengers. If we wanted to return after the tide turned, we would have to find another passage.

"Do you expect trouble?" I asked.

"I expect to earn my fare," he said. "I have already lost a morning to your trouble."

He put us ashore where the roots held the mud firm enough for feet. The keeper stepped out first with the cord. Sulad followed, carrying no weapon and no bundle. I brought a small water jar. The ferry owner pushed away before I had set it down.

Two people were waiting. One was the first far-bank representative. The other was an older woman with a paddle worn thin near the grip. She said her household kept boats above the bend and that the stranger in the blue headcloth was her brother.

"Did he send you?" I asked.

"He told me you were asking questions. I came to hear them myself."

She looked at the cord.

"Are you going to stretch that across the river?"

"No. It marks a depth we measured yesterday."

"Then it cannot tell you what the bottom was last season."

"No."

"Good. My youngest son says a cord can settle any quarrel if you pull it tight enough. He has not yet tried pulling one against a flood."

The first representative seemed relieved to have someone else answer me. He offered her a place on the driest root. She declined it and stood where she could see the river.

A third boat came from upriver with two men and a boy. They stopped beyond the roots. The boy carried a bundle of thin sticks and a shallow basket. One man came ashore, said he had been asked to witness the sounding for a boat-owning household farther north, and refused to name who had asked him until he knew who else had arrived.

The woman with the paddle laughed.

"Then you can go back without telling us who sent you."

He looked offended, but gave the name of his elder sister, who managed the household's boats while their father was ill. He had come because she had lost a load at a different bend when a marker was moved without warning.

"Was it this marker?" I asked.

"No. That is why she sent me. She does not want it to happen here."

He set the basket down. It held a broken stake, much older than the one in our passage, with the point worn smooth.

"She told me to show you what a marker becomes when everyone says another man will replace it."

I wanted to ask who had moved that stake. The boy who carried it had already turned away to help his boatmen secure their bow. The object had come to us without the story I needed.

The woman with the paddle asked whether I had called anyone from Lakandula's household.

"I sent no message to him," I said.

"Then do not say the houses of Tondo have met."

"I have not said that."

"Your messenger said you wanted to settle a passage for both shores."

"That is what I want."

"Wanting is easy. The people who answer to Lakandula will ask what you have offered their boats and why you expect their agreement before they have heard the injury."

"Will you carry the question to them?"

"I will carry what I hear. I cannot promise an answer. Nor can I promise that a man you call Lakandula will speak for the house that sent this boy."

The boy, who had been listening, looked up.

"My aunt sent me," he said. "She did not send him."

The man from the boat opened his mouth, then closed it. He had been speaking for the household as if the boy were only there to carry wood. The woman with the paddle asked the boy to repeat his aunt's instruction. He said she wanted the old stake shown, and that no one should agree to a new payment in her name.

"Now we have heard it," she said.

The keeper unrolled the cord on the roots. She showed the marks made at the last sounding and named the two people who had witnessed each one. She did not ask anyone to accept them. Sulad pointed across the water to the place where he had driven the newer stake, still leaning in the shallows.

"The bottom rises before it," he said. "A loaded boat needs room to turn. We measured the turn at that tide."

"Could you move the stake back yourself?" the woman asked.

"Yes."

"Would that make it right?"

"It would put it where I drove it. I cannot say whether that is where it should stay."

She turned to me.

"Then why did you stop people from moving it?"

"Because I wanted the man whose fish was spoiled to see where the boat ran aground."

"And when his fish is gone?"

"He has kept the wet baskets apart. The buyer has counted them."

"Then you have saved the count. Have you saved the passage?"

"No. The two men are warning boats until we can sound it again."

The man from upriver asked how long they would be paid to stand there. I told him they had offered one tide. He asked what happened on the next. I had no answer.

A narrow boat passed the waiting bank. Its steersman saw us gathered on the roots and called out whether the outer passage was open. The keeper answered that the inner line was clear and the outer stake remained disputed. He cursed the lost time and took the longer water.

The woman with the paddle watched him go.

"That is what our people hear," she said. "Not your good reasons. They hear that they must wait because men are arguing over a piece of wood."

I felt myself reaching for a promise. I could say we would pay for a watch every day, or build a new marker before sunset. The keeper had brought a cord and no money. Sulad had given an afternoon that belonged to another man's net.

"What can we try without making a new levy?" I asked.

The woman set her paddle flat on the roots.

"Send one person from each shore to watch a sounding. Let each say where a loaded boat can pass at the next low water. Do not put a new stake in until they have both seen the bottom. If they disagree, leave the old one where everyone can see it and warn the boats again."

The first representative said his house could send a steersman.

"Your house was at the first sounding," she answered. "Send him, but let another house watch too."

The man from upriver offered the boy as his aunt's witness. The boy shook his head.

"I can show where she told me to go. I do not know the bottom."

"Then she must send someone who does," the woman said. She looked at me. "That is not an insult to the boy. He has told us what he knows."

Sulad asked who would handle the pole if the witnesses came. He could do the work for a wage, he said, but the next low water would arrive before the net owner had finished with him.

The keeper said the landing had two workers who knew how to sound the shallows. One was the man who had helped Sulad set the first stake. The other had carried the cord. She would ask them whether they could come and what work they would miss.

"Then the first sounding can be done without Sulad," the woman said.

I looked at him. He nodded. It was a relief to him, I thought, though I could not know whether it was the money or the hours he would keep.

The woman agreed to bring one steersman from her household's boats. The first representative agreed to bring one from his own house. The man from upriver said he would ask his elder sister for someone who knew the shallow turn. None of them pledged another household.

We had a trial to arrange, but the woman had not finished.

"What will you do if your own people moved the stake?" she asked.

"Ask why."

"And after you have heard why?"

"Count the loss. Hear who was warned. Decide what can be repaired."

"Who will decide?"

I opened my mouth. The keeper was looking at the water. She had not come to answer for me.

"The people whose boats use it must hear the finding," I said. "I can answer for what the Maynila landing did. I cannot order your houses to accept my answer."

"That is a smaller promise than the one your messenger brought."

"Then he should carry this one back."

Banu was not there. I would have to find him again, or carry the words myself.

The tide had begun to pull away from the roots. The woman with the paddle lifted her basket and called for her boat. The boy took the old stake from the mud and carried it aboard. He had not agreed to leave it with us.

The first representative offered to take me back to Maynila. I thanked him and refused. I had come in a ferry whose owner wanted his fare, and I would return the same way if I could find one.

The woman watched me walk toward the upper landing where boats sometimes waited.

"Do not call this an agreement for Tondo," she said.

"What shall I call it?"

"A few people who will come and look."

She pushed off before I could ask whether that would be enough.

The keeper followed me along the bank, winding her cord. Sulad had already turned toward the net sheds across the river. He would find a crossing that did not require him to wait for me.

I looked back once. No one had left a pledge on the roots. No one had accepted a fee. We had chosen a tide, a place to measure, and people who might arrive.

When I reached the upper landing, the ferry owner was still on the far side. I sat on a dry root with my jar and waited for a boat that owed me nothing.
