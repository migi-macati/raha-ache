# Scene 2 — The Words She Would Not Carry

Iya put the cord on the table.

It was the same dark fiber I had seen in her hand at the doorway, but there were two fresh knots near one end. Water had made the loose strands swell. She spread them with her fingers until the cord lay straight between the keeper's knife and the strip from yesterday's crossing.

"That is what Nali gave me," she said.

"What do the knots mean?" I asked.

"They mean I must remember two things. I will tell you what they are. Do not ask me to show them to someone else as proof."

The keeper moved her knife away from the cord.

"Then tell us."

"First, the woman with the cane came back by the path Nali allowed. She did not put down a load or ask a man to carry one for her. Second, that permission ended when she reached Nali's yard. My sister did not give the path to every boat that followed."

"That is what we marked here," I said.

"Then why did a man arrive this morning with two bundles and say your mark gave him leave?"

"He may have heard the owner who wanted watchers."

"May have?"

"I do not know who told him."

Iya looked at the strip on the keeper's shelf. It lay beside the older claims, its cut edge turned toward the light. She had seen the keeper cut it after Ranu returned. She had not been here when the keeper set it away.

"Did you make a mark that says Nali refused the next load?" she asked.

"I marked what we heard of Ranu's return," the keeper said. "Iya called across that Nali allowed only that journey. I did not hear Nali herself at this table."

"Then you marked what I said."

"Yes."

"And if I had said it wrongly?"

"Someone who heard Nali would have to correct me."

Iya lifted the cord.

"That is why I came back. Nali says the man with the bundles had heard that the marked crossing was a way open to both banks. She sent him away. He left one bundle on the wet ground while he argued, then took it up again. He did not break another slat. He made her children stop their drying work to stand by the path until he left."

"Did he say who sent him?" I asked.

"He said he had been told at Maynila. Nali asked for a name. He would not give one."

"Did she know his boat?"

"He came on foot from above the bend. She did not see his boat."

I had been imagining a merchant coming across from our landing. The man might have come from another house entirely. The words had traveled farther than the boat that first carried Ranu.

"Then how can you say the permission came from here?" I asked.

"I have not said it did. I have said he claimed it did."

The keeper put her hand on the shelf. She had made the marks in front of witnesses, but the strip had already acquired a meaning no one present had agreed to give it.

"Let me tell you what I can say," she told Iya. "Ranu paid Ilan for one journey from Nali's path to our stones and back. The pole-handler accepted food for his work. Raya allowed one person to use the exposed stones while the water was low. Dala allowed that woman to pass above her net. Nali allowed her own path for that same journey. We did not make a new agreement for other loads."

"Who heard all of those words?" Iya asked.

"Not one person," the keeper said. "I heard the words spoken here. You heard the words on Nali's bank. Some of us heard what you called across."

Iya sat on the low bench. She did not touch the strip.

"I was asked to come because my sister is tired of strangers explaining her own ground to her," she said. "If I take your strip back and tell her it means only one journey, the next man will say I am speaking for Maynila. If I tell the next man Nali said no, he will say I am only her sister. Both things are true. Neither gives him leave."

I wanted to tell her that being Nali's sister made her the right person to speak. But I had heard Dala refuse to carry words she had not witnessed. Kinship could make a person care about a claim. It could also make someone else refuse to believe her.

"Would you take a witness from here?" I asked.

"Who?"

"The keeper."

"Then who hears the people who come to your hall while she is away?"

I had asked the same question yesterday and disliked the answer then.

"Someone else could sit here."

"Who knows what she has heard?"

I looked at the shelf. The older strips were not all mine. Some belonged to people who had brought claims while I was away. I could have held the knife and cut a mark, but I would not know what question the mark answered.

"Could you take the strip without her?" I asked.

Iya looked at me.

"Would you like me to say what you tell me?"

"Only what was agreed."

"Then say it to me."

I repeated the keeper's account. I named the boat, the woman with cane, the old stones and the two paths. I said the passage did not make a charge for passing boats or open Nali's yard to other cargo.

Iya listened until I finished.

"You left out the first time Ilan turned back," she said.

"That was because the current was too strong."

"It was part of what happened. A man who hears only that a woman crossed may think the river can be used whenever he wishes."

"Then tell him the first approach failed."

"And you left out the three pieces of cane the maker bought."

"That was their trade. It did not change the path."

"It changed what Ranu would say the journey was worth. If you ask her to come again, she will remember the cost."

I could feel the keeper watching me. I had told Iya the words I thought mattered to the landing. She was telling me what the people who paid for the journey might remember.

"I did not mean to hide those things," I said.

"I know. That is why I will not carry only your words."

She put one finger on the first knot.

"Nali asked me to tell you that she will hear a person who comes to ask about the path. She will not receive a man who arrives with bundles and says he has already been given leave."

"And the second knot?"

"She wants someone from this bank to hear her say it, so the next time a stranger claims your hall gave permission there will be more than my word against his."

"Would she come here?"

"She came yesterday. It cost her a canoe journey and half a morning. She has a broken rack to mend. You cannot ask her to keep crossing because you do not wish to cross yourself."

The keeper took a blank strip from the shelf. She did not reach for the knife.

"If a person from here goes, I can tell that person what I heard. But Nali must be free to say something different. I will not cut her answer before she gives it."

Iya nodded.

"That is what she asked."

Outside the hall someone shouted for the old stones. A man came to the doorway carrying a narrow basket covered with leaves. He had been told that a woman crossed yesterday and wanted to put a bundle ashore before the water rose. The keeper asked who owned his boat.

"My brother," he said.

"Has your brother agreed to wait while you ask Dala and Raya?"

"He told me to find a landing."

"Then ask him whether he will hold the boat here. The upper step is closed."

The man looked past us toward the shelf.

"Is that the strip for the crossing?"

"It is a reminder of one journey that finished," the keeper said.

"Then why keep it where anyone can see it?"

"So anyone who was there may tell me if I have remembered wrongly."

He frowned. He had expected a mark that would tell him where to put his basket. He had found three people who could tell him what someone else had done yesterday. He went back to his boat without asking to use the stones. We heard him call to his brother and then heard the pole strike the water.

Iya watched him leave.

"He will tell someone the landing turned him away," she said.

"He was not turned away," I answered. "He was asked to find out whether his boat could wait."

"That may be the same thing when the water is moving."

I could not make him wait by explaining the difference.

The keeper set the blank strip on the table.

"Will you tell Nali I have heard her question?" she asked Iya.

"Yes. I was here when you heard it."

"Will you tell her I have agreed to her path?"

"No. You cannot agree for her."

The keeper smiled a little, then stopped. Iya had not been joking.

"And will you carry a message from Ache?" I asked.

"I will tell her you were here. I will tell her you asked me to carry words and that I refused to carry only your account. If you want more than that, find someone who can hear both of us."

I looked at her wet sandals. She had crossed for her sister's sake and had no reason to make a second journey on mine. Amaya had already taken me to a seller whose cloth I could not yet buy. I had promised work on another morning. A crossing would cost someone food, time and a boat, even before a word was spoken.

"Who would you trust to come?" I asked.

"Nali will decide whom she hears. You must decide whom you ask. Those are different questions."

She retied the cord. The two knots remained where Nali had put them.

"Do not send a man who expects me to open the rack path because he carries your strip," she said. "Send someone who is willing to hear me say no."

She rose to go. I could have offered to walk with her to the bank, but that would not make a boat appear. I asked whether she had arranged her return.

"The woman who brought me will take me back when she has sold her fish," Iya said. "I have paid her for both ways. She has not promised to carry anyone else."

I walked with Iya as far as the higher path. From there we could see the boat she had hired, pulled onto dry ground above the turn. Its owner was counting fish into a customer's basket. Iya waited at a distance rather than call her away from the sale.

The keeper came to stand beside me with the blank strip.

"Will you mark that Nali asked for a witness?" I said.

"I can mark that Iya brought the request and that I heard it. I will not mark that someone has agreed to go."

I looked across the water toward Nali's trees. The path was hidden from this side. Iya's two knots were a better reminder to her than our strip would ever be, but they were hers to explain.

"Then keep it blank until we find the person," I said.

The keeper did not cut it. Iya waited for her boat, and the river kept moving between the two accounts.
