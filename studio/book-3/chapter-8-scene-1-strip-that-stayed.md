# Scene 1 — The Strip That Stayed

The keeper had already put her knife away.

Ilan's boat was passing the far side of the turn, its stern lifting and falling in the wake of a larger hull. I could still see the pole-handler standing with his feet apart. He had finished the journey Ranu paid him for. He was going somewhere I had not asked him to go.

"Ask who is willing to go," the keeper had said.

I looked at the strip in her hand.

"You could carry it."

"Then who will stay with the claims here?"

"I could."

"You can hear them. You do not know which marks belong to which witnesses unless I tell you, and I cannot make Nali accept a thing because you carry it."

She turned the strip over. There was one cut for the journey, another for the return witnessed from our bank, and a small notch beside the place where Iya's words had been repeated across the water. It was a poor thing to send without the woman who had made it. The cut for the return did not show the broken rack above Nali's path. The notch did not show the moment Ilan had turned away from the stronger current before trying again.

"Then I will ask Iya," I said.

"She is across the water."

"She can come again."

"If she wishes."

The keeper placed the strip on the shelf with its cut edge outward. She kept it apart from the older one for the disputed two-basket journey. I had watched her do that so often that I could have told a stranger which was which. I could not have told him why the second mark had been made unless I had heard the people who spoke.

Dala was folding her net farther up the path. She had carried it clear of the wet channel before the tide turned. I went to her.

"Would you take a message to Nali if someone paid your passage?"

"No."

"I have not said what the message is."

"You have said I must leave my net."

"Only long enough to cross."

"How long was Ranu's crossing?"

"Longer than we expected."

"Then you know why I said no."

She lifted the net. A knot caught on the edge of her basket and pulled a strand tight. She worked it free with her thumb. I stood beside her with nothing to carry.

"The keeper wants someone to hear the words on both banks," I said.

"She can ask someone who has heard both. I was here when Ranu stepped onto the stones. I did not hear what Nali said when she got home."

"Iya did."

"Then ask Iya. Do not send me to repeat what I did not hear."

Dala moved past me. She was not angry. She had a basket to bring home before the air grew wet, and I had asked her to give me an hour that belonged to her.

At the old landing a man was waiting with a small boat. I knew him by the coil of cord on his shoulder. He had supplied cord when the marker was placed and had argued for paid watchers. His boat carried no cargo. A younger man held it against the higher bank, where the stones were dry.

"I heard a woman crossed today," the owner said.

"One woman with a sample, and she went back."

"Then the step is open."

"The upper step is still closed. Raya let her use the old stones while they were exposed."

He looked at the barrier above the water.

"I have two boats coming with pots in the morning. If I tell them to come here, will your keeper show them the way?"

"She can tell them what was agreed. She cannot open Dala's path or Nali's."

"They do not want Nali's path. They want this one."

"Then they must ask Dala, and Raya must judge the stones when they arrive."

He rubbed the cord against his palm.

"I have told the owners the passage is being watched."

"By whom?"

"By the men who stand here when a boat comes."

"No one has been hired to stand here."

"Then perhaps I will hire them myself."

"You may hire men for your own boat."

"And if they help another boat?"

"That owner can decide what to pay them."

"Every boat will wait for another to pay. I have seen it happen."

He was not wrong about the waiting. I had stood beside a boat while a pole-handler's wrist went red from holding it. I had also heard the same owner propose a passing charge that no one had accepted. I looked at his cord. It was useful cord. He had given useful things before.

"If you hire watchers," I said, "tell the owners who pays them. Do not say the keeper has marked a charge."

"Then give me something I can carry to the owners."

I thought of the strip on the shelf. He would have carried it. He would also have explained it in the words he preferred.

"The keeper can tell you what she marked," I said.

"She has told me before. I need something a man can show when he is asked why he should trust this bank."

"I cannot give him a mark that promises more than one journey."

The owner looked at the boat he had brought.

"Then he may send his pots by another channel. You will have told him why."

He turned away. The younger man let the bow come free of the bank and climbed in. I had not asked their names. I could not say whether the pots would come tomorrow. The owner had brought me an opportunity to make a larger promise, and I had refused it. He had brought his own boat to hear the answer. I watched it go.

The keeper was closing the hall when I returned. She had heard the end of our exchange.

"He may tell people I turned him away," I said.

"He may. He was here."

"He will leave out the part about the fee."

"Then someone who heard it must be willing to say that part."

I looked at her knife, put away beside the shelf. I wanted her to cut one more mark for what the owner had said. She had not been there to hear it. I could tell her, but that would be my account.

"Tomorrow I will find someone to go across," I said.

"Tomorrow you said you would go with Amaya."

I had already begun to forget.

***

Amaya came for me before the sun reached the roofline.

She carried the old cloth with the dark square patch. It was folded into four and tied with a thin cord. She did not ask whether I had slept. She looked at my hands.

"Have you brought anything to pay with?"

"Nothing that would buy a cloth."

"Then you will hear what one costs."

We took the upper market path. A woman passed us with a shallow tray of fish balanced on her head. Behind her two boys were leading a small goat with a fiber cord. They kept stopping to let it bite at leaves. Amaya walked past them without changing her pace. I had to lengthen my stride to stay beside her.

At a house above the market path, a weaver named Tesa was sorting cloth beneath an awning. She had three lengths folded on a reed mat, each held at one end by a smooth stone. A young woman sat beside her winding thread around a spindle. Tesa looked at Amaya's old cloth before she looked at me.

"You want one of that measure?"

"One fit to be used," Amaya said. "He owes it to my household."

Tesa unfolded a length of coarse cloth. It was strong, but one edge had a narrow place where the weave pulled apart under her fingers. Amaya touched it.

"Not that one."

"It is cheaper."

"It will not hold at the edge."

Tesa put it aside without arguing. She brought out another, pale where the light struck it and darker in the folds. Amaya held it against her measure. She tested the threads at one corner and turned it to look at the opposite edge.

"This would do."

Tesa named the price in rice and wax. I had neither amount. I asked whether she would take work instead.

"What work do you do?" she asked.

"I can carry loads from the river."

"Everyone says that until the load is wet."

"I carried jars yesterday."

"Did they reach the house unbroken?"

"Yes."

"Whose jars?"

I told her the basket maker's name. Tesa knew her. She sent the young woman to ask whether the jars had arrived and whether I had kept my word. I stood in the shade while she went. Amaya did not speak for me.

When the young woman returned, she said the basket maker had received the jars, but I had failed to come the day before and another carrier had been paid. Tesa nodded.

"I have bundles coming from the lower market two mornings from now," she said. "If you carry them and help dry the cloth after the crossing, I can count that work toward this piece. It will not pay the whole price. I will tell you the rest when I see what arrives."

"Will you hold the cloth?" I asked.

"Until the next market morning. If someone pays for it first, I will sell it."

"Then how can I promise Amaya that I will bring it?"

"You cannot promise what I have not sold you."

Amaya folded her old cloth.

"I have not asked him to promise another day," she said. "I asked him to see what he owes."

I looked at the length Tesa had laid aside. It was a thing I could touch, unlike the money I kept expecting from the landing. If I worked for her, I would miss another morning at the river. If I stayed at the river, I would have no better answer for Amaya.

"I will come for the work," I said.

Tesa looked at me for a moment.

"Come before the bundles arrive. I will not keep the boat waiting because you have other business."

"I understand."

"You have said that before," Amaya said, quietly enough that only I heard.

We went back along the market path. I wanted to tell her that the work would bring the cloth closer, that I had begun paying in the only way I could. She had seen the price and the seller. She knew I had not paid.

At the hall the keeper was standing beside a woman with wet sandals. I recognized Iya before she turned. She had come across the river without sending for me.

"I waited," Iya said. "The keeper told me you were away."

"I was with Amaya."

"I know. She told me you owed her a cloth."

Amaya looked at the keeper. The keeper held her hands open.

"I told Iya only that you had gone to seek it," she said.

Iya was holding the end of a narrow cord. The fibers were dark where the water had touched them.

"Nali sent me," she said. "A man came to her yard with two bundles this morning. He said your crossing yesterday gave him leave to use the path. She sent him away. He says he heard the permission from a man at your landing."

"Who?"

"He would not give her a name. He said the permission had been marked."

The keeper turned toward the shelf. The strip was still there, untouched since Ranu's return.

I had wanted to be the person who explained what it meant. Iya had already heard a version that made the broken rack's path open to another load. I had been away doing work I owed. For once, the river had not waited for me to come back.

"Will you tell me exactly what Nali said?" I asked.

Iya looked at Amaya, then at the strip.

"I will tell you what she told me," she said. "You may decide whether that is enough."
