# Book III, Chapter 5 — Scene 6: Two Cuts for One Boat

**Packet:** RA-B3C5-SCENE-06. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Scene 5 ends with Ache agreeing to carry the basket maker's finished order when dry, return the buyer's actual payment, and receive only enough fiber to propose a trial binding to Dala. The far-bank jar buyer may have visited Sera; no answer about Mulaw has reached Ache. Raya's leaning stone marks an upper-step edge needing inspection. The lower berth is unbuilt. Dala's damage/lost-catch claim, Raya's separate household compensation, and Ache's cloth debt to Amaya remain open. **Historical status:** All named transactions, tally practices, witness interpretations and labor arrangements are fictional; no uniform historical law of tolls, wages or written contracts is asserted.

The baskets were dry enough to lift, but not all of them were sound.

The maker found the bad rim before I did. She ran two fingers beneath the third basket and turned it toward the light. One strip had risen from the weave where I had helped stack it the day before.

"That was not there when I finished it," she said.

"Did I bend it?"

"You carried the stack. The boy pulled one by its rim. I do not know which of you did this."

Her nephew stood beside the trough with his hands at his sides. I could see him trying to remember how hard he had pulled.

"I can take it out," I said.

"You can carry it with the others. The woman who ordered them may accept it, or she may send it back. Do not press it down and tell her it was always sound."

She tied the four baskets in two pairs with a cord she kept separate from Dala's sample. The boy led us along the path above the bend. He walked fast when he was not carrying anything, then waited for me whenever the baskets struck my knees.

The house had a low gate made of split wood. Behind it stood a roof with no wall on the river side. A woman was arranging small packets on a mat beneath the roof. Her left arm was wrapped in cloth from the wrist to the elbow. She had a shallow wooden tray beside her and a pot of water within reach.

"Your baskets," the boy announced.

She did not look up until I set them down.

"I asked for four," she said.

"There are four," I answered.

"That one is bent."

"The maker saw it. She asked me to show you."

The woman took the basket and pressed its rim against the floor. It rocked on one side. She turned it and found the raised strip.

"I can take three. The fourth goes back."

"Would you keep it until she can mend it?"

"I have no place for a basket I cannot use. My roof is already full."

She pointed to the packets on the mat. They were wrapped in leaves and tied in pairs. A faint smell of wax rose from the one nearest the sun.

"What do you put in the baskets?" I asked.

"Goods that should not sit in water."

"Where are they going?"

"With a boatman who has agreed to carry them. I have not asked you to find him."

I held my tongue. She counted the three sound baskets, set them beside the packets, and took a folded piece of cloth from the tray. She showed it to the boy, then to me.

"Tell the maker I accepted three. This is what I give for three. She may bring the fourth when it is mended, and we will speak about it then."

The boy repeated the number. The woman made him say it again while he was looking at the rejected basket.

"Three accepted. One returned."

"Good. Now take that one away before I put something in it by mistake."

I put the bent basket inside the empty carrying cord. The woman watched me lift it.

"Are you the man who speaks for the upper landing?" she asked.

"Sometimes."

"Then perhaps you can tell me why the boatman says the step is closed."

"Raya is checking the outer edge. The old path is open for small loads."

"My goods are small. He wants to take them after the next low water. I have paid him for one journey, if he can bring his boat alongside without breaking anything."

"What boat?"

"The narrow one that carried the oil jars yesterday. He came to ask me about these packets before he took Lana's jars across."

I recognized the small-boat owner. He had refused to carry Lana's seven jars for the price of one crossing. He would refuse this woman too if the weight or waiting changed.

"Have you spoken to Raya?"

"No. He told me you would know where to find her."

"Raya decides what the step can hold. Dala needs the path beside it. The hall cannot keep your packets while you wait."

"I have my own roof," she said. "I am asking for the length of a carrying path, not a room in your house."

I looked at the three baskets she had accepted. She was already fitting the packets inside them, pressing the leaf-wrapped pieces into the corners so they would not slide.

"How many baskets will go?"

"Two. The third is for the things I keep here."

"Will you take them down yourself?"

"My nephew can carry one. I can walk with the other. The boatman will take them aboard."

She flexed the fingers of her wrapped arm and winced. The movement made me look away.

"Would you like me to carry the other?" I asked.

"Not unless you want payment for carrying. I have paid the boatman. I have not hired you."

"I can help for nothing."

"Then I would have to wonder what you expected afterward."

I had no answer that would sound clean to her. She turned back to her packets.

"Find Raya. If the step is not safe, the goods can wait here. They will not spoil before the next tide."

I carried the bent basket back to the maker. The boy ran ahead to tell her the count. He arrived breathless, and she made him sit before he spoke.

"Three accepted," he said. "One came back."

I put the folded cloth in her hand. She unfolded it and examined the edge for a tear. Then she compared it to the work she had sent.

"That is payment for three," she said. "You brought it all."

"I did."

She put it away beneath a dry bowl, where the boy could not accidentally wet it.

"You have done the carrying. I can set aside the fiber for Dala now."

"Can you make it today?"

"I can begin it after I finish the basket she returned. It will be a short length. Dala must see whether it is fit."

She gave me a little bundle of raw fiber to show Dala what she meant. It was rougher than the finished cord I had imagined. She warned me not to pull it apart before she worked it.

"This is mine until I make it," she said. "Bring it back when you have shown her."

I thanked her and went down to the landing. Raya had lifted the outer timber enough to see the earth beneath it. Her nephew was holding a stone in the place where the leaning marker had stood. She pressed fresh earth under the edge and tamped it with the flat of a small piece of wood.

"Will it hold?" I asked.

"After the next water I will know whether it moved. For now the inner part can take a man with a light load. Not two men passing each other."

"A woman above the bend has two baskets to send. The small-boat owner has agreed to carry them if the step is sound."

"Then he should come and ask me."

"He will."

"Bring the woman too. She knows what is in them."

Dala was near her rack, turning the broken frame so the sun reached its wet side. I showed her the bundle of fiber. She pulled a single strand loose and rubbed it between her fingers.

"It may serve," she said. "But the old binding is soft. I will not put this on until the maker has worked it and I have tried it wet."

"She will make a short length when she finishes a returned basket."

"Then we are still waiting."

"Yes."

She looked toward the upper path.

"Is there another boat coming?"

"A woman wants to send two baskets. The step may take them after Raya looks again."

"Where will they stand?"

"She will bring them from her own house. She needs only the path."

"Not through my rack."

"I will tell her."

"Tell her yourself. Do not tell the keeper I agreed before she comes."

I returned the raw fiber to the maker, then went up to the woman's house. By the time I arrived, the small-boat owner was there. He had one foot on the low gate and was looking at the two filled baskets.

"I said I would carry them if the landing held," he told me. "She says you will find out."

"Raya wants you both to come."

"Then we will."

The woman covered the baskets with a mat. Her nephew carried one and she carried the other, its weight against her unwrapped arm. I offered to take it once. She shook her head. I walked behind them so that no one could say I had brought the goods on the hall's behalf.

At the landing Raya had finished packing the edge. She made the small-boat owner put his foot on the inner timber while his boat remained tied to the old post. He shifted his weight once, then stepped back.

"One man at a time," she said. "Keep the baskets close to the bank. If the edge moves after the next high water, I close it again."

The woman looked at Dala's rack. Dala was standing beside it.

"I can carry these without touching your frame," she said. "Will that path be open when the water falls?"

"After I take my net down," Dala answered. "You may pass when I call. If your people come early, they wait above."

The small-boat owner nodded.

"One journey with two baskets," he said. "I will take them to the buyer she named. She pays me whether he takes them or sends them back. I am not keeping them in my boat beyond the journey."

The woman agreed. The keeper repeated the words while she held a clean strip of bamboo against her knee. She cut one long mark for the journey and two short marks for the baskets. Then she held the strip where the owner and the woman could see it.

"One boat, two baskets, after Dala clears the path and Raya allows the step," she said. "Nothing here promises another journey."

The woman touched the long cut. The owner touched each short one. I watched them both, relieved that they seemed to understand the same thing.

When Dala called, the woman's nephew carried the first basket down. The small-boat owner steadied it from the boat while the nephew stepped back onto the old path. The woman brought the second herself. She did not ask me to carry it. I held the boat's loose line only when the owner told me to, and I returned it as soon as he had secured the load.

The boat moved out from the old post. It took the bend slowly, keeping clear of the sounding stake. I watched the two covered baskets disappear behind the low bank. Their buyer was still upriver. No sale had been made in front of us, and the woman had not promised a share to the landing.

The keeper put the marked strip with the day's other records. Dala lifted her frame back into the sun. Raya went to inspect the timber again. Amaya remained in the hall, where the cloth I owed her had not appeared. The maker still had to finish the binding, and her own buyer had accepted only three of four baskets. None of those things had changed because a boat had left with two loads.

The owner's young crewman came up the path after the boat was gone. He had been sent back to fetch a small paddle left beside the hall. The keeper showed him where it had been put out of the way.

"Is the second journey still ours?" he asked.

The keeper stopped rolling her strip.

"What second journey?"

"The owner said there were two cuts for two turns. He told me to come back for the other load after the water rises."

"There are two cuts for two baskets," she said. "One long cut for one journey."

The crewman pointed at the marks.

"He told me the two small cuts meant he could come twice. He has gone to deliver the first. He will want to come again before night."

"Did you hear what he agreed here?"

"I was keeping the bow clear. I heard him say two."

"He said two baskets," the woman answered. She had come back for the mat that covered them. "I paid him for one journey."

"Then why did he tell me two?"

No one answered. I remembered the owner touching each short cut, one after the other. I had been pleased that he seemed to understand. I had not asked him to repeat what each cut meant in his own words.

The keeper laid the strip flat.

"Do not alter it," she told me when I reached toward it.

"I only wanted to show him the long mark."

"He has seen it."

The crewman picked up the paddle. He did not look like a man trying to steal a berth. He looked like someone who had been sent to do a job and would have to explain why he returned without it.

"Will you stop us if we come back?" he asked.

"Raya will decide whether the step is open after the next water," the keeper said. "No one here agreed to a second journey. Bring your owner to speak for what he believes he heard."

"He is on the river."

"Then the question waits for him."

The crewman went down the path with the paddle. I watched him climb into a different small boat that would take him to the bend. He had been told one thing by the owner and another by the people on the bank. Both would expect him to remember their words.

"Should I have spoken sooner?" I asked the keeper.

"You heard the same words I did."

"I saw him touch the marks."

"So did I. We thought we knew what he meant."

She did not put the strip away. She left it on the low table beside the open doorway, with the long cut and the two short ones facing the path.

The woman whose goods had gone upriver returned to her house. Dala took her frame home without a new binding. Raya's stone remained beside the step for the next tide to test. I could not tell whether the small-boat owner would return angry, or whether his crewman had mistaken him, or whether the owner had heard only the part of the bargain he wanted.

I had spent days trying to find a man whose name I remembered from a distant boat. I had found no promise there. Here, beside the landing I could see from the hall, I had watched two people agree to one journey and leave with different accounts of the marks.

The bamboo lay where anyone could look at it. The owner who needed to answer for his reading was already beyond the bend.
