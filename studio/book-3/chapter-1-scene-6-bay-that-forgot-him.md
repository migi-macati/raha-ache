# Book III, Chapter 1 — Scene 6: The Bay That Forgot Him

**Packet:** RA-B3C1-SCENE-06. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 5's three-night paid lodging agreement. Banu carries Ache's request toward Tondo but has not promised a hearing. Amaya has agreed to send word through her own channels without disclosing Ache's mother's whereabouts. Harun retains ownership of his vessel and may depart after trading. Sulad works independently at the lower nets. **Historical boundary:** The household instruction, rival river interests and conditional hearing are invented fiction; no historically named Lakandula or Sulayman is assigned an early-1520s office.

---

On the first morning I woke in the tool room to the sound of a man trying to open the door.

The wood caught against the floor. He pushed once, stopped, then called through the gap.

"I need the long pole."

I moved my mat aside and lifted the door clear. The man stepped past me, took the pole from the wall and went without asking how I had slept. He was the same worker who had shown me the leak in the roof. He had not expected me to be there after sunrise.

I went outside. The river was low, and the boats nearest the landing leaned toward the mud. The keeper was lighting a fire beneath a pot. She told me to carry water if I wanted breakfast. I took the jar and followed her directions to the well.

When I returned, three men were waiting beside the shelter. They had come from the upper crossing. One held a length of cloth dyed dark at the edge. Another wore a knife with a wooden handle. The third had brought no weapon I could see.

They were speaking with the landing woman. I heard Burné, then my name.

The man with the cloth turned toward me.

"Are you the one who came from across the sea?"

"I am."

"Have you brought soldiers?"

"No."

"Then why do people say you have?"

I looked at the landing woman. She was watching me, waiting to see how I would answer.

"I came on a trader's boat," I said. "It is still at anchor. The owner will sell his goods and leave when he chooses."

The man with the knife pointed toward the river. "We were told a ruler from Burné sent you with orders."

"He gave me a letter."

"Then you have brought orders."

"Not for this landing."

"Let us see it."

I had left the letter wrapped in the tool room. I could have fetched it. I could have held the seal before them and hoped they would be impressed by a name from another shore.

"What would it prove to you?" I asked.

"That you have been sent."

"It would prove that a man across the sea knows I am here. It does not give me your boats."

The landing woman looked at me sharply. The man with the cloth did not seem satisfied.

"Why did you go to the house at the bend?" he asked.

"My mother kept it."

"That house has workers in it."

"I know. I spoke to them."

"Then leave them to their work."

I could hear the keeper moving her pot away from the fire. The people carrying baskets had stopped at the edge of the shelter. I had been ashore only one day, and already my arrival had made the place less useful to them.

"I have asked for a hearing," I said. "I will not take the house while people are living there."

The man with the knife stepped forward.

"Will you say that where the people can hear?"

"Yes."

"Then say it."

He had not called me prince. He had not asked who my father was. He wanted a promise that would keep his work from being disturbed.

I turned toward the baskets and the people waiting beside them.

"I am Ache," I said. "I was born upriver. I have come to ask what became of my family and the place they kept. I have no soldiers. I will not put people out of their house or stop their boats while my claim is heard."

The words sounded smaller than the speech I had practiced at sea. They were also words I could keep.

The landing woman raised a hand. "He has paid for three nights in the tool room. He has promised no more than he has said. If he breaks that promise, tell me before you come here with knives."

The man with the knife looked down at his belt. He moved his hand away from the handle.

"We were told to ask," he said.

"Then you have asked," she answered.

The three men left toward the upper crossing. The people with baskets began moving again. One woman passed so close to me that the edge of her load brushed my arm. She did not apologize. I stepped out of her way.

Isan came from the tool room, rubbing sleep from his eyes.

"What happened?" he asked.

"I was asked whether I had brought soldiers."

"And what did you say?"

"The truth."

"That is sometimes enough for a morning."

He took the water jar from me and went to help the keeper.

By midday Banu returned from the far bank. He found me beside the steps, helping the men lift a boat clear of the mud. He waited until we had set the supports beneath its hull.

"I carried your question," he said.

"What did they answer?"

"First they asked who had sent me. I told them you had. Then they asked whether you had brought men to make your claim. I said I had seen none."

"Will they hear me?"

"They will send someone to listen if the people at this landing also agree. They will not come because a foreign ruler has written your name on a letter."

"When?"

"After the next market day, perhaps. I was given no promise of an hour."

"Who will come?"

"A man who speaks for a house that keeps boats on the far bank. I did not ask him to give you his whole family."

I had hoped for a name I could place among the old rivalries. I knew the river had carried more families and more debts than I could remember.

"Do they still speak of my mother?" I asked.

"Yes."

"What do they say?"

"That she knew how to make men listen. Some liked it. Some did not."

He let the words stand. Then he took a small piece of dried fish from his bag and began to eat. He had carried my question without payment and had come back with the answer. I offered him part of my remaining coin.

He looked at it.

"Keep enough to stay until they come. If you have no food, you will be asking the wrong people for help."

He took one small piece and put it away.

Amaya came down the path while we were speaking. She was carrying a folded cloth wrapped around something hard. The girls from her house had not come with her. She had walked alone.

"I heard you spoke before the landing," she said.

"I said I would not put anyone out."

"I know. Someone came to tell me."

"Will you speak for me at the hearing?"

"I will speak about what I know. You will have to decide whether that helps you."

She unfolded the cloth. Inside lay a piece of old red-painted wood, no longer than my hand. Its edges were worn where it had been cut from a larger post. A hole had been bored near one end.

I knew the color before I touched it.

"The post," I said.

"Part of it. The rest was rotten. Your mother told us to take it down before it fell on someone."

I took the piece. Red paint remained in the grain where the sun had not reached.

"She told you?"

"Before she left the house."

"Where did she go?"

Amaya looked toward the people moving through the shelter.

"I have told you I will not answer for her here. I can tell you what she said to us."

I waited.

"She said the hall must remain open for the work that fed the household. If the roof failed, we were to mend it. If people needed the floor, we were to let them use it. She knew that you were gone. She did not tell us to leave the house empty until you returned."

I looked at the wood in my hand. I had imagined that the people at the bend had taken advantage of my mother's absence. Amaya was telling me that my mother had given them work to do.

"Did she say it was no longer ours?"

"She did not speak about ownership the way you have been speaking. She told us to keep the place alive. There were people who could not take their families away when she went."

"Who heard her?"

"Three people who worked there. One is dead. One has gone inland. I am here."

"Will you say that before the people from Tondo?"

"Yes. And I will tell them who repaired the floor and who paid for the new roof. If you want me to hide that, ask someone else to speak for you."

"I do not."

"Then we can speak."

She held out her hand for the wood. I gave it back. I had wanted to keep it, but she had brought it as evidence of a house that other people had maintained. It belonged with the rest of the things they had saved.

"Can I see it again?" I asked.

"When you come to hear what we have to say."

She wrapped it and turned toward the path. Then she stopped.

"Your mother would not have liked you standing here waiting for strangers to tell you whether you have a place to sleep."

"I have paid for one."

"Then she would have liked that better."

She left before I could ask another question.

Banu watched her go.

"That woman will speak clearly," he said.

"She always did."

"Then listen when she does."

He picked up his bag and went toward the boats bound upriver.

Sulad came back at dusk. He had earned his wage helping lift a hull from the mud. He carried a small piece of dried fish and a handful of rice wrapped in a leaf. He offered me half without ceremony.

I told him about the men who had asked whether I had soldiers, and about Banu's answer from Tondo.

"Will you speak for me?" I asked.

He looked at the river.

"I will say what I saw when we left. I will say what I have seen since we came back. I will not say your mother gave you a house that she told others to keep working."

"She was trying to protect it."

"Then that is what I will say."

I ate the rice. It had gone cold, but I was hungry enough not to care.

"Harun may leave soon," I said.

"He will leave when his trade is done."

"I have three nights here."

"Then we have three nights to learn whether you can stay longer."

"We?"

He looked at me.

"I have work at the nets tomorrow. I will hear what the men there say about the far bank. That does not mean I will fight for you."

"I know."

He nodded once. "Good."

The last light lay across the river. Boats were returning to the shelter, each one finding its place among the others. Beyond them, farther out, Harun's vessel was a dark shape against the bay. I could not see the people on deck. I knew he would be checking his goods and preparing for the next tide.

I had thought of home as a place that would wait for me. It had been a place where people worked, argued, repaired roofs and made promises in my absence. My mother had known that before I did.

I went back to the tool room and put my remaining gifts beside the mat. Tomorrow I would ask the keeper what work she had. I would ask Amaya who else had heard my mother's instructions. I would wait for the man from the far bank without sending for Harun's letter.

I still wanted Maynila. I had not stopped wanting it. But the river had given me three nights and the chance to be heard. I could begin there.
