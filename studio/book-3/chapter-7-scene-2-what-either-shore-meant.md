# Scene 2 — What Either Shore Meant

The man at the stern drove his pole down again. It went deeper than he expected. His hands slid along the wet wood, and the boat turned broadside before he could free it.

"Keep the bow out," I called.

"I am trying."

A woman rose from behind the bundles. She had been sitting on a low plank, her knees pressed against the covered cargo. The movement of the boat made her put one hand on the gunwale. With the other she held the edge of the leaf covering in place.

"Are you the man at the hall?" she asked.

"I was there."

"Then you know why we have come."

Iya gave me a look. I had not heard of this boat until it appeared around the bend.

"Tell me," I said.

The woman lifted one of the leaves. Beneath it were lengths of split cane tied in flat bundles. Some had been scraped pale; others still carried their green outer skin. They were not heavy like jars, but they took up nearly the whole bottom of the boat. If a bundle shifted across the planks, there was little room for a foot.

"I am Ranu. I am taking these to a maker who has asked to see the finer strips. We came from the bank beyond the bend. They told us we could use this landing."

"Who told you?"

"A man carrying two baskets yesterday. He said the people here were hearing boat claims and would let a load pass if we told them whose it was."

I knew of several men who had carried baskets yesterday. I had been one of them. The description was no help.

"Did he say the step was ready?"

"He said the way had been repaired."

"That is true," I said. "It has not yet been cleared after the high water."

The man at the stern worked his pole free. The boat came nose-first toward the old post. I stepped down, meaning to keep him from tying there, and my heel slipped in the wet groove beside Dala's net. I caught the post with one hand. The wood was rough against my palm.

"Do not tie yet," I said.

He planted the pole again.

"Then where do you want me?"

"Where you can hold without touching the step."

"That is not a place."

Iya moved to the edge of the dry path.

"Who gave you leave on our bank?" she asked Ranu.

Ranu blinked.

"I came from there."

"That is not what I asked."

"The woman whose yard we loaded in knew I was taking the cane."

"Was it hers?"

"No. It is mine. I paid for it."

"Did she give you her path?"

"We used the water path beside her yard."

"Which yard?"

Ranu described a low house with a rack of pots beneath its eaves. Iya shook her head.

"That is above my sister's place. You came through the lower path to reach the water."

"The man with the boat took us there."

The pole-handler pulled the pole up and held it across the stern. He had a strip of cloth bound around his right wrist. The knot was wet and tight.

"That is where we loaded," he said. "I did not see a net rack."

Iya held up the split peg.

"Someone broke one."

He leaned forward to look, but the boat swung away before he could make out the wood.

"I did not carry sacks," he said.

"Did you see men carrying sacks yesterday?"

"I saw men carrying all morning."

"Did one tell a girl that a promise made here gave him leave to cross our path?"

"I heard a man say he could take his load by either bank."

"Which man?"

The pole-handler pointed upriver with his chin.

"Near the boats at the bend. He was speaking to someone else. I did not ask his name."

Ranu turned toward him.

"That is what you told me. You said we could land by either bank."

"I said a man had told me that. I did not say the bank belonged to him."

Ranu stared at him for a moment. Then she sat down on the plank, drawing her feet away from the cane.

"I paid you to bring it here," she said.

"You paid for one journey."

"To this landing."

"To this bank," he said. "I was not paid to carry the cane into a house."

"That was never asked of you."

"You asked where I would put the bundles. I said at the landing, if they let us."

"Who are 'they'?"

He looked at me.

I had been ready to say that we would hear the matter in the hall. Ranu had paid for a boat, not a hearing. The pole-handler was keeping the cargo off the mud while we tried to decide whether a stranger's words had promised something no one could give.

"Who owns the boat?" I asked him.

"Ilan. He has another one upriver."

"Did he tell you to come here?"

"He told me to carry Ranu's cane to the Maynila bank. She chose the landing."

"And if the step is closed?"

"He told me to find a safe place or bring the load back. I will not put his boat against rotten wood."

"Is he paying you to wait?"

"He paid me for the journey. We did not speak of waiting."

Ranu looked from him to me.

"How long before the maker goes to her work? She asked me to bring a sample while the sun was still low."

"Which maker?"

"The one who works baskets beyond the hall."

I knew her. I had left her boy without help with the jars. If she had found someone to carry them, she might already have gone to the potter's yard. I could not promise that she was waiting.

"I can send a child to ask," I said.

"At whose cost?"

I had not expected that question. A child sent on an errand might miss his own work. Ranu was right to ask, though she had asked as if there were a price for everything.

"I can go," I said.

"Then the boat will still be here when you return."

The pole-handler shook his head.

"Not if the water rises further. I can hold it a little while. Then I must take it into the slower channel."

"Can you put the bundles on the old landing?" Ranu asked.

"Not without someone to receive them."

Iya bent to inspect the wet groove where I had slipped. It ran along Dala's net line. There were fresh footprints in it from my own heel and from the people who had passed earlier.

"Nor without Dala's leave," she said.

"Who is Dala?" Ranu asked.

"The woman whose net you can see," I answered.

Ranu leaned to look. The leaf covering slipped from the bundle beside her. She caught it and tied it down with the loose end of a cord.

"I have bought the cane. I have hired the boat. I have come to the bank. Must I now buy a path from someone whose name I did not know?"

"You must ask her whether you may use it."

"And if she says no?"

"Then we look for another way."

"Who is paying for that?"

I had no answer. Iya watched me long enough that I felt she could hear the one I wanted to give: that I would put the matter right, because the landing was mine to make useful. I could not make Dala's ground mine by wanting the boat to stay.

The pole-handler began moving the boat sideways into the quiet water near the old landing. His shoulders tightened under his shirt. Ranu took one bundle in her lap to keep it from sliding.

"Do not let the bottom touch," he said. "There is a stone under the mud."

He was speaking to the boat as much as to us.

Iya stepped back from the water.

"On our bank," she said, "my sister's path was used without her leave. Someone told her daughter the hall had given permission. Here a boat arrives because someone else said either bank could be used. It is a good way for a man to be welcome wherever he wishes to walk."

Ranu looked at the broken peg.

"Do you say we broke that?"

"No."

"Then why show it to us?"

"Because the words that brought you here may be the words that brought them through our rack."

The pole-handler stopped working the pole. For a moment the boat drifted toward the post. He caught the current again before it struck.

"I heard the words," he said. "I did not say they were true."

Iya looked at him.

"Will you say that to my sister?"

"If she comes here."

"She is at work. You have a boat. You could go there."

"I have been paid to come here."

"Then the man who paid you has more claim on your day than the woman whose rack was broken."

Ranu's face changed. She had been looking for a place to unload; now she heard herself accused of buying the right to ignore another household. She set the bundle down carefully.

"I did not pay him to break anyone's rack," she said. "I paid him to carry cane."

"I know," Iya answered.

"Then ask me what I am willing to do before telling me what I have done."

Iya was silent. The two women faced one another across the water, one standing on the bank, the other in a boat that had nowhere to land.

Ranu lifted a short length of cane from the top bundle.

"This is the sample. I can carry it in my hands. I do not need a path wide enough for the whole load. If your sister has a broken rack, I will look at it when I return. But I will not say I owe for it unless I learn who struck it."

Iya studied the cane.

"That is fair," she said. "And I will not say your boat struck it when I have not seen that."

I had been thinking of sending for Raya and Dala. Now I had a smaller question to answer. A woman with a single piece of cane wanted to step onto our bank. She had offered to carry it herself. The pole-handler could bring her to the old landing, but the water had covered the lower stones and Dala's net lay across the shortest path. I could not tell from where I stood whether there was dry ground beyond the post.

"Let me ask Dala," I said.

Ranu looked at the sky.

"How long?"

"I do not know."

She laughed once.

"Then I will decide how long I can wait."

The pole-handler pulled his cloth binding tighter. A narrow red mark showed where the knot had pressed his wrist.

"I can hold until the shadow of that tree reaches the old post," he said. "After that I go to the bend. If she wants to stay, someone else must bring her ashore."

Ranu glanced at the tree. The shadow was already moving over the wet ground.

"Will you take me back if we cannot unload?"

"That is the journey Ilan told me to finish."

"With the cane?"

"With whatever remains in the boat."

She looked down at the bundles. It would cost her a morning to bring them back, and perhaps another to try again. I had thought the new landing would bring work to Maynila. It had brought a woman whose work was already slipping away while I stood beside a post.

Iya wrapped her broken peg and tucked it into her waist. She did not offer to carry Ranu's sample. She had come to ask for an answer, and her own work was waiting across the river.

"Go to Dala," she told me. "I will stay where I can hear what the boatman says."

I started up the path. Behind me Ranu asked the pole-handler to repeat exactly what Ilan had told him before they left. I heard him begin, then lost the words as I passed the hanging net.

Dala was crouched beside a stake, drawing a needle through wet mesh. I stopped before stepping over the line.

"There is a woman in a boat with a piece of cane," I said. "She wants to carry it up to the maker. May she pass here?"

Dala pulled the needle free. She looked toward the water, then at the groove my heel had cut.

"Is she the one who made that?"

"I did."

"Then repair it before you ask me to let someone else through."

She put the needle back through the mesh. The boat waited beyond the post. I could hear the pole-handler counting his breaths as he held it against the current.
