# Book III, Chapter 3 — Scene 2: The Man Who Was Not Sent

**Packet:** RA-B3C3-SCENE-02. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Immediately follows Scene 1's displaced stake, grounded fish boat and competing far-bank claims. The keeper still collects the agreed Maynila landing share; no fee has been agreed for merely passing through. **Historical status:** Fictional negotiation, participants and dialogue. No documented early-sixteenth-century meeting with Lakandula is claimed.

---

The stranger would not come beneath the shelter.

He stood where the path met the dry bank, far enough from the broken boat that no one could accuse him of helping to free it. His headcloth was faded blue. He had tied it twice at the back so the ends would not fall over his eyes.

Banu waited beside him.

"Will you speak here?" I asked.

"Not while that man says his loss belongs to my house."

The carrier with the broken pole had set his wet fish on Amaya's rack. He turned at the sound of the stranger's voice.

"Your people use this water."

"So do yours."

"My boat was put on the mud."

"I can see that."

"Then you can see why I came."

The stranger looked at the keeper instead of me.

"Who set that stake?"

"People from both sides watched the sounding," she said. "Sulad drove the wood. The owners agreed to try two passages."

"Which owners?"

She named the boat-owning house that had sent a representative to yesterday's hearing, and the Maynila owners who had taken part. The stranger waited for another name. When she had finished, he asked again.

"Which owners?"

"Those who were there," she said.

"My people were not."

The carrier lifted his hands.

"Your people have a boat on this river."

"We have boats farther up. We come down to sell. We were not asked to put our name on a marker."

The keeper asked whether the stranger owned the boats himself. He said his sister's household kept two, and that he was sent to learn why one had been turned away from the outer passage before sunrise. He had not come to accept a toll, settle the fish claim or promise another house's cooperation.

"Then why did you say you spoke for your bank?" I asked.

"I did not."

"You said I must learn who may answer for it."

"Yes. You have already called a few owners the whole bank. I came because that is what men say before they begin collecting from people who never agreed."

I felt the carrier watching me. I had asked for a hearing because I wanted to know who moved a stake. Now I was being asked who had given me the right to ask.

Banu rubbed the dust from his feet with the edge of his cloth.

"I told him there was a quarrel," he said. "I did not tell him you had ordered his boats to pay."

The stranger turned to him.

"You said a man at Maynila was hearing claims."

"That is what I said."

"And you said the man had been given a place in the old hall."

"That too."

"You did not say he was given our passage."

"No."

I looked at Banu. He had carried my words twice now, each time returning with less certainty than I had hoped for.

"Will you carry another question?" I asked.

"After you decide what it is."

The stranger folded his arms.

"I can answer a question about my sister's boats. I cannot give you the people who live above our landing."

He had come to deny an authority I had not claimed aloud. Yet I had already spoken of witnesses from both banks, as if there were only two households to summon.

The steersman called from the river. The boat had risen enough for them to slide a round pole beneath the bow. They were trying to turn it toward the inner passage. Sulad told them to wait for the tide to cover the outer edge of the mud. One man wanted to pull now, while they could still stand beside the hull. The others wanted to spare the bottom planks.

The keeper went to them. She left me with the stranger and the carrier.

I asked the stranger how his sister's boats usually passed the lower landing.

"With the tide. We use the outside water when the loads are heavy. When it is low, we wait or take the inner way if no one is coming out."

"Who tells you which is open?"

"We look."

"Then you know the stake."

"I know there was a stake. I do not know who told you to move it."

"I did not move it."

"Then who is keeping it there?"

I had ordered that no one touch it until witnesses came. To the stranger, the stake was still mine because I had spoken about it as if it could be kept by my word.

"We are keeping it for people to see," I said.

"Which people?"

"Those whose boats use the passage."

"That is better."

He said it without approval. He was measuring whether I would make the next claim larger.

The man from the first hearing arrived while we were speaking. He had come in a small canoe, without cargo. When he saw the stranger, his face tightened.

"Who sent you?"

"My sister's house."

"I was here yesterday."

"I heard."

"Then you know what we agreed."

"I know what you agreed. We did not."

The first representative looked at me.

"Did you send for him?"

"No. Banu told him of the dispute."

Banu shook his head.

"I told him what happened. He decided to come."

The first representative pointed toward the fish boat.

"That carrier works for a man who uses our landing. We can hear his claim. We did not ask another house to come and undo yesterday."

"Your agreement was to try two passages," the stranger said. "That is not a right to mark all the water."

"It was not all the water."

"Then why does your stake turn our boats?"

The first representative began to answer, but the carrier interrupted.

"Your stake turned my boat."

The first representative looked at the grounded hull and then at the man carrying its loss. He had not seen the morning's approach. His mouth closed.

I knew how he felt. Yesterday he had been the voice from the other shore, the man whose acceptance had helped bring me into the hall. Today another man had arrived to tell him his voice was smaller than it sounded.

The keeper returned and told us the boat could be freed at the next rise. She had agreed to lend the men two poles and a line, to be returned before dusk. The carrier asked whether the fish buyer would still be waiting. The keeper said she was on Amaya's rack counting baskets and would not leave until she had a witness to the wet ones.

The first representative said he would take the carrier's account to his house.

"And will your house pay?" the stranger asked.

"I have not heard what happened."

"Then do not say you will settle it."

The first representative took a step toward him. I moved between them before either had raised a hand.

"Neither of you saw the stake move," I said.

"Neither did you," the stranger answered.

"I know. We have Sulad's sounding and the hole he found. We have the steersman's account. We can ask who worked on this passage after the last tide."

The first representative nodded. The stranger did not.

"And if you learn one of your people moved it?"

"Then that will be part of what we hear."

"Part of it?"

"The loss, too."

He looked toward the rack. Dala had come to speak with the fish buyer. Pawi stood beside her with the net frame they had been unable to set. The boy was watching the men in the water as if he might be allowed to help when the hull rose.

The stranger pointed at them.

"Do they have to wait for the houses to finish arguing?"

"No," I said.

"Then let them speak first."

I brought the men down to the rack. Amaya had made the buyer lay the dry baskets on one side and the wet ones on another. She had put a folded mat between the fish and the ground. The carrier had counted each basket aloud while she watched. The buyer had opened two and set aside the fish that smelled of mud.

Dala stood with her hands on her hips.

"I lost this tide," she said before I asked her anything. "My boy was ready to set the frame. The boat was in the way."

The first representative asked whether she meant to charge the carrier for the missed catch.

"I do not know yet," she said. "I know where the boat was. I know what I could not do. I will not put a price on fish I never caught because someone wants the account finished today."

The stranger looked at her with interest. He had come to challenge the authority of one house. Dala was challenging the ease with which all of us could turn her morning into a number.

The buyer named the baskets that had arrived wet. The carrier repeated where the boat grounded. The steersman, called from the river, described his approach and said he had followed the visible stake. The keeper confirmed the last agreed sounding but would not say when the stake moved.

Isan came from the market carrying a strip of cloth he had been asked to deliver. He stood beside me while the accounts were repeated. The first representative used a trade word for a charge that Isan had heard used for unloading goods. The stranger used it for a payment demanded from a passing boat.

"Are they speaking of the same thing?" I asked him quietly.

"No," Isan said. "One means a share when goods are brought to the steps. The other means a charge for going through the water."

"Tell them."

"You can tell them," he said. "They understand you. I will say which word each man used if someone later claims he heard another."

I asked the keeper to state the agreement made yesterday. She did so: a share for using the Maynila landing, collected with witnesses, and no charge merely for passing. The first representative said his house had accepted that trial. The stranger said his sister's house had accepted nothing, but had no reason to object to a passage that remained free.

"Then we have no quarrel over a passing fee," I said.

"We have no new fee yet," the stranger answered. "That is a different thing."

He had been watching my words as carefully as the keeper watched her cord.

The first representative wanted us to move the leaning stake back to Sulad's old measure before the next tide. The stranger objected that the old measure had never been witnessed by his sister's people. The carrier wanted the passage made safe immediately. Dala wanted the outer boat cleared so she could work. Each demand was reasonable until I tried to satisfy all of them at once.

I asked the keeper whether we could mark the dangerous place with a floating branch for one tide while the stake remained untouched.

"We can warn boats from the steps," she said. "I will not promise that a branch will stay where we put it."

The first representative offered to send a man from his house to stand watch. The stranger said his sister would send one too, but only if the warning did not become another way of claiming the passage. They agreed to stand on opposite sides of the steps, where each could see the other.

The keeper made them repeat what they would do. They would call to approaching boats, show the deeper line and leave the disputed stake in place until the next sounding. They would not demand money. They would not turn a boat away merely because its owner had refused yesterday's agreement.

It was the first thing the two men had agreed upon.

I asked whether they would meet again after the tide, with the people whose boats had been delayed.

The first representative said he could bring the men who had authorized him yesterday. The stranger said he would bring his sister, or someone she chose, and would ask the other households above the bend whether they wanted to send witnesses.

"When?" I asked.

"After they have heard what you want," he said.

"I want a rule that keeps the passage open."

"You want to make the rule."

He said it as if I had asked for something I did not yet understand.

Banu offered to carry the invitation if I would give him the words in front of both men. He wanted to know whom to approach, what was being asked, and who would pay him for the crossing. The keeper said the landing could pay a messenger from the repair share only if the owners agreed the journey served the landing. The first representative said his house would pay half. The stranger refused to pledge his sister's money without asking her.

"Then I will pay the other half," I said.

Banu looked at me.

"With what?"

I had a small piece of cloth left from the gifts I brought from Burné. I told him where it was and what it might be worth. He said he would take it when he had finished, if it was still there.

He made me repeat the invitation.

I asked for a meeting about the displaced stake, the two passages and the loss to the carrier, buyer and people kept from work. I said the people who had accepted yesterday's trial could attend, and those who had not accepted it could come to state their objections. I did not ask anyone to recognize me as master of the river.

The first representative listened. The stranger listened. Neither gave me the satisfaction of saying I had spoken well.

Banu went to find a boat.

When the men had returned to their work, I found the keeper washing mud from her measuring cord. She spread it over the rail to dry.

"Will they come?" I asked.

"Some will."

"Will that be enough?"

"Enough to begin. Not enough to speak for people who stay away."

I watched the two men take their places beside the steps. One stood where the outer passage opened. The other stood farther up, near the turn toward the inner water. They were not friends. They did not need to be friends to call a warning to a boat.

A woman carrying firewood came down the path and asked which of them had the right to stop her crossing. Both men pointed toward the keeper.

The keeper told her she could pass.

The woman went through without waiting for either of them to agree.

I had spent the morning trying to discover who spoke for Tondo. I had learned how many people could refuse to be spoken for. The meeting I had asked for would be harder than the one that had given me my place in Maynila.

I went back to the hall to find the cloth I had promised Banu. Before the tide turned, I wanted it ready.
