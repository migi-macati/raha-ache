# Book III, Chapter 5 — Scene 4: The Cargo That Could Leave

**Packet:** RA-B3C5-SCENE-04. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Scene 3 ends with Dala giving Ache a sample of her damaged net binding for the basket maker to inspect. No cord has been sold, and Sulad has returned to independent paid boat work. The repaired upper step remains subject to Raya's high-water inspection and can take only a small vessel and separate loads. Sera's possible inquiry about Mulaw is still conditional. **Historical status:** Lana, the jar trade, prices and handling agreements are fictional; no standard Maynila toll or historical commodity valuation is claimed.

---

The basket maker had no cord that Dala could use that morning.

She laid the frayed sample on her palm, turned it over with the point of her blade, and pulled a loose fiber free. Then she took down the coil I had been watching. Its strands were stiff and smooth. She bent a short length between her fingers and showed me where it held the bend.

"This will cut into that old binding when it swells," she said. "You need something that can take water with the rest of it."

"Can you make it?"

"Yes. Not before I finish the baskets I have promised."

"How long?"

"Ask me after the next market day."

I wanted to offer her my fish, but I had eaten it with Sulad. I had nothing left in my cloth except the damp sample.

"Will you keep this?"

"She told you to bring it back if I could not use it, didn't she?"

I nodded. The woman put it in my hand. Her knife was already moving through the bamboo before I reached the path.

By the next low water, Dala had found a way to brace the damaged corner without tightening it. She showed me where she had set a small piece of wood across the split. It kept the frame from falling when she lifted it, but she would not put the net under strain.

"She can make binding," I said. "After her other work."

"Then we wait."

"Could I help with the frame meanwhile?"

"You can hold it while I take the old pieces apart. That will not make it ready for the water."

I held the frame. She loosened the temporary brace and took it away. When she set the wood on the ground, the corner sagged again. I had wanted the visit to the basket maker to count as progress. Dala had a frame that still could not catch fish.

A call came from the upper path. The keeper was waving to someone beyond the hall, not to me. I kept my hands where Dala had placed them until she told me to let go.

"Go see what has come," she said. "I will send when I need you."

At the landing a broad boat waited out from the bank. Seven dark jars stood in two rows beneath a covering. The vessel rode low at the stern, where a woman was arguing with a man in a narrow canoe. She wore a strip of red cloth at her wrist and held one jar lid in her hand.

The man in the canoe was the small-boat owner who had helped test Raya's step. His bow was pointed toward the old upper post, but he had not tied there.

"She wants to move her jars in my boat," he told the keeper. "I can take two at a time. I won't carry all seven for the price of one crossing."

The woman held up the lid.

"I am Lana. I have buyers here. They told me there was a sound step."

"There is one," I said.

She looked at me, then at the edge of the bank.

"For a boat my size?"

"No. The upper post is for the smaller vessel. Raya has to see it after high water."

"Then why did they send me here?"

"Who sent you?"

"A man who bought oil from me before. He said I could bring it to Maynila now."

"Did he say where you could put it?"

"He said there was a step."

The keeper went to find Raya. Lana turned the lid in her hands. Its rim had been rubbed smooth by use, but the jar it belonged to was covered and I could not tell whether the seal beneath it was sound.

"Can you wait for the maker?" I asked.

"I can wait until the tide turns. Then I have to decide where to sell."

Her crewman had put a hand against the larger boat's side to steady it. He looked toward the far bank, where a row of roofs stood above a broad strip of dry ground. I recognized the landing to which Sera had taken her fish.

Raya arrived with her nephew and a short stick. She tested the upper step from the bank, then knelt and reached beneath it. The small-boat owner watched her fingers disappear into the mud.

"Has it moved?" he asked.

"Not enough to close it for a single load," she said. "But no one is to stand a jar on the outer edge. The line stays on the old post. I will not answer for the larger boat."

Lana pointed at her seven jars.

"Two at a time?"

"Only if this man agrees to carry them. I am judging the step, not his boat."

The small-boat owner looked at Lana.

"Two jars for each trip, paid whether you sell them or not. If the buyer makes me wait, he pays for my waiting or you do."

"How much?"

He named a portion of oil. Lana shook her head.

"That is enough to feed a household for a week."

"Then find another boat."

She lowered the lid and studied the jars. Her crewman spoke quietly to her. I could not hear his words. She answered without looking at him.

"One trip first," she told the small-boat owner. "Two jars. If the buyer takes them, we can speak of another."

"And if he doesn't?"

"I pay for that trip."

He nodded. The keeper asked them to repeat the terms while she marked them on a fresh strip. She left a space beneath the marks for the second trip, but did not fill it.

I wanted to tell Lana that Maynila could take all seven if she gave us time. The sight of the empty space on the keeper's strip stopped me. She had agreed to one crossing, and I had no place to put even the first two jars.

The buyer came while the crew were lifting the first jar. He was a narrow man with a basket on his back and a young assistant carrying a folded mat. He pointed to a patch of earth above the step.

"Set them there. I want to see the seals."

Dala's rack stood beside that patch. Her sister had moved it back after the tide test, and the frame lay under its shade.

"That is where I work," Dala called.

The buyer looked at the patch again.

"I only need the ground until I have opened them."

"Then ask me how long I need it."

He came to her. I followed, hoping they might find a way to use the space for a few moments. Dala was kneeling with her broken frame across her legs. She showed him the brace she had taken out.

"Can you put your mat here without touching this?"

He tried to lay the folded mat between the path and the rack. It overlapped the place where Pawi had set the sound cord.

"Move that," he said.

"It belongs here."

"Then where do you expect people to trade?"

"I expect them to ask where I fish."

The buyer stood. He did not insult her. He looked toward the hall instead.

"Can I use the floor inside?"

Amaya had heard him. She came to the doorway and looked at the two jars now resting in the small boat.

"What is in them?"

"Oil."

"Are they sealed?"

"I need to open them to know."

"Then I will not take them among the dry cloth and grain."

"I can pay for the floor."

"That does not make the grain dry again if something spills."

He glanced at me. I had begun to raise my hand, as though I could settle the matter by asking Amaya to move her goods. She saw it and turned back into the hall.

Lana called from the larger boat. The narrow vessel had reached the upper post and the small-boat owner was holding it there. The first jar was on the new step. His crewman stood with the second against his chest, waiting for the path to clear.

"Where do I put this?" he asked.

The buyer went down to him. He took the folded mat from his assistant and spread it across the middle of the narrow boat, away from the jar being carried ashore.

"Can I inspect them aboard?"

The small-boat owner watched the mat settle.

"One at a time. Keep your feet where I tell you. If you break a jar, you answer to her, not to me."

The buyer agreed. His assistant stayed on the path. The crewman carried the first jar back down from the step and set it on the mat. He had lifted it once without selling it, and now had to lift it again. I helped him steady the base as it came over the gunwale.

Lana leaned from her boat.

"Do not open both until you have agreed on the first."

The buyer lifted the lid. He loosened the binding beneath it, touched a little oil to his finger, and smelled it. He asked Lana where she had filled the jar. She told him, and he asked whether the other was from the same pressing. She said it was. He did not accept that as a reason to buy without looking.

They agreed on the first jar. The assistant carried it up the repaired step while Raya watched his footing. He set it on the path only long enough for the buyer to lift it onto his shoulder.

The second jar had a dark patch beneath the binding. The buyer turned it and found a place where oil had worked into the outer cord.

"That seal has been wet," he said.

"The jar is sound," Lana answered.

"Then why is the binding dark?"

"Because the boat is wet."

"Not under the lid."

Lana asked him to taste the oil. He refused. He had paid for the first jar and would not take the second at the same price. She offered a smaller amount. He shook his head again.

The small-boat owner held the narrow vessel against the post while they spoke. The current was beginning to pull harder around the bow.

"You can bargain on your own boat," he told Lana. "I have held this one long enough."

She looked at the buyer.

"Will you take it for less?"

"Not today."

She told the crewman to bring the jar back. He lifted it from the mat and nearly struck the gunwale with its base. The small-boat owner caught his elbow, then let go as soon as the weight was steady.

"One trip," he said to Lana. "That was our bargain."

She paid him the agreed portion from a small vessel she had kept beneath the covering. She measured it herself and made him inspect it before he took it. He did not thank me for helping with the jar. He had never hired me.

The buyer and his assistant went uphill with their purchase. I had watched one jar enter Maynila and one return to the broad boat. No place had been taken from Dala. No oil had been put in Amaya's hall. The keeper marked one sale witnessed at the upper step and one jar returned unsold. She left the second-trip space empty.

"Will you bring the others?" I asked Lana.

"Not here."

"We could find another buyer."

"Where would he look at them?"

I looked at the path. The mat was already being folded. Dala had returned to her frame. Raya was feeling the step where the crewman had turned with the jar. The small-boat owner was gathering his line.

Lana called to her steersman.

"Take us across."

A larger vessel was moving from the far bank toward her. Its owner was the man who had asked Raya to build a lower berth for his salted-fish cargo. He had kept his own goods across the river after she refused. He brought his boat close enough to speak without tying to Lana's.

"I can put your jars under a roof there," he said, pointing across. "I know two buyers who will look at them. I take a share if they sell."

"How much?"

He named it. She answered with less. They argued while their vessels drifted slowly downstream, the crewmen keeping the hulls apart with poles. Neither asked me to decide.

I could have called out that she should wait for our landing to improve. The buyer who had come for her oil was already gone. The step had held the one jar that was sold, but it could not give her a floor or another buyer before the tide changed.

Lana reached an agreement with the larger owner that I could not hear. She gave her steersman a signal, and the two boats turned toward the far bank. Six jars remained aboard her vessel. I could count their dark lids beneath the covering.

The keeper rolled the strip she had made for the crossing.

"Did we receive a share?" I asked.

"From whom?"

"I thought the buyer might give something for using the step."

"He paid Lana. Lana paid the small boat. Neither offered us a share."

"Could we ask the next one?"

"We can ask what we are giving in return."

I watched the boats reach the other landing. Men there were already clearing a place beneath a roof. One jar was lifted onto dry ground, and a second followed. They did not wait for a man from Maynila to tell them where to stand.

Raya called me down to the step. A little mud had been pressed from beneath the outer edge where the crewman turned with the unsold jar. She showed me the mark it left on the timber.

"It held the load we agreed," she said. "Do not call that proof it can take another."

I helped her put a stone beside the edge so she could see whether the next water moved it. I could still smell the oil on my fingers from the jar I had steadied. I had no oil of my own to show for the morning, and no payment for Dala, Raya or Amaya.

Across the river Lana's crew had begun carrying the remaining jars beneath the roof. She had found a place for them. It had not been ours.
