# Book III, Chapter 5 — Scene 2: The Name on the Boat

**Packet:** RA-B3C5-SCENE-02. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Sera took her covered fish baskets to the far bank at the end of Scene 1. The keeper did not grant priority, and no landing share was received. Book I Chapter 7: Mulaw gave only his name and advice to ask a buyer about boats, at an unnamed landing; Nima was guarding his absent sister's cloth. **Historical status:** This market, inquiry and named people are fictional. No precise trade route, standard payment or actual Mulaw location is established.

---

From our bank I could see Sera's baskets in a row beneath a roof made of palm leaves. I had watched the first one come ashore yesterday. By morning the row had shortened, and I could not tell whether she had sold the others or moved them out of the sun.

The keeper was hanging a new strip beside the old ones when I passed the hall.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"Across."

"For the baskets?"

"For the woman who owns them."

The keeper turned the strip so that its unmarked side faced her.

"Do you have a claim on her?"

"No."

"Then go without taking one of ours."

I had no tally in my hand. I had not thought I was taking anything. She looked at the cloth I wore and the empty cord around my waist.

"Yesterday you offered her first use of the step," she said. "She did not accept it. If she speaks to you today, she speaks to you, not to this landing."

"I know."

I had been saying that often. It was becoming an answer I gave before I had worked out what I meant.

There was no boat waiting to carry me across. The small vessels that used the upper post were unloading for the morning, and their crews had places to go afterward. I walked downriver until I found a man pushing a narrow canoe away from the reeds. He had a bundle of split bamboo behind his seat and was making for the opposite bank.

"Will you take another person?" I asked.

"Where?"

"To the market under the palm roof."

"That's farther than I meant to go."

"I can help with the bundle."

He looked at my hands.

"Will you carry it when we land?"

"If it belongs where you're going."

"It belongs at my sister's house. Not at the market."

I looked toward the far bank. His sister's house was upstream of the roof. I would have to walk back along the shore.

"I'll carry it there."

"And who brings it out of the canoe?"

"I do."

He moved his foot to make room. I climbed in, keeping my weight low while he pushed us clear. The canoe rocked beneath me. I had crossed the river many times and still disliked the moment when neither bank could be reached with a foot.

The bundle was tied with two loops of cord. The man told me to leave it where it lay until he had brought the canoe against the far shore. Halfway across, the current caught our bow and turned us. I reached for the bundle to keep it from rolling.

"Leave it," he said.

"It's moving."

"It can move. You cannot. Sit."

I sat. The bundle shifted a hand's width and stopped against the seat. He worked the paddle until the canoe pointed upstream again. I had offered him my hands and made them another thing he had to watch.

His sister's house stood above a narrow strip of stones. The canoe could come close, but not far enough to put the bundle directly on dry ground. He tied it to a root and stepped out first. I waited until he told me to lift.

The bamboo was longer than I had judged. I took the weight across one shoulder, and the ends struck the branches as I turned. The man walked ahead, calling which way the path bent. His sister was outside with a shallow basket on her lap. She looked up when she saw me.

"You brought someone," she said.

"He brought himself."

She showed me where to lay the bundle. It was not beside the door. It belonged under the eaves where the cut ends could dry. I put it there and stepped back.

"Do you want food?" she asked.

I was hungry. I could smell something cooking inside.

"I came to carry the bundle," I said.

"That was for him," she answered, pointing to her brother. "I asked whether you want food."

I took the small piece she offered. She watched me eat it before returning to her basket. Her brother untied the loops and counted the pieces of bamboo. I had carried the bundle, but he was still responsible for what arrived.

I went toward the market with the taste of warm rice on my tongue.

The far-bank path was crowded with people coming away from the river. Some carried empty baskets, others had jars against their hips. A child dragged a mat by one corner, leaving a dark line in the dust. Two women were arguing over a measure of grain. Neither looked at me as I passed. Here I was another person trying to find a place to stand.

Sera was kneeling beside her remaining baskets. She had opened one and spread the fish on a mat, turning the damp pieces toward the air. The smell was stronger than it had been on the boat. A buyer stood over her with a small knife, testing the flesh near a bone.

"You came," she said when she saw me.

"Your boat did."

"My boat came here. You walked."

The buyer cut a piece from the fish and tasted it. He shook his head, but did not leave. Sera waited until he named a price. She answered with another. They were still talking when I moved to the side of the roof.

One of her polemen recognized me. He was sitting on an overturned basket, rubbing a blister beneath his thumb.

"Is your step bigger today?" he asked.

"No."

"Then don't tell her to go back."

"I haven't."

He bent over his hand again. I watched him pull a thin strip of skin away and wince.

When the buyer left with two fish, Sera covered the rest.

"Did you come to buy?" she asked.

"I came to ask about a boat."

"You saw mine yesterday."

"Not yours. Someone I met years ago."

She folded her mat without looking at me.

"Where?"

"At a landing on my first journey away from home."

"Which landing?"

I tried to picture the shore. A post near the shallows. A fisherman with his net. A house with a repaired roof. A buyer who counted baskets. The people had been so particular about their own places that I had thought I would never forget them. Now I could not tell her which bend of the coast had held the post.

"I don't know its name," I said.

"Who was the buyer?"

"I never asked."

She sat back on her heels.

"And you came across the river to ask me to find a boat from a place you cannot name?"

"A young man led it. His name was Mulaw. He was taking cloth that belonged to his crewman's sister. The crewman was called Nima."

Sera glanced at the poleman. He did not look up.

"Did the young man say he would look for you?"

"No."

"Did he tell you where he was going?"

"Not to a place I could find."

"Then why do you want him?"

I had prepared an answer while walking from the canoe. I wanted to ask Mulaw which buyers still dealt with the people he knew. I wanted to know whether someone who had once seen me carry baskets might listen now that I spoke for a landing in Maynila. I wanted him to remember that I had kept my word about a fisherman's post.

"He told me to ask buyers about boats," I said. "I didn't ask enough then."

"That sounds like advice."

"It was."

"Not a debt."

"No."

She drew the covering over the fish again. A little of the salt had collected at the bottom of the basket. She shook it out onto the mat.

"There are men who hear names on every river," she said. "If they remember yours, they may ask. But a name alone won't tell them where to go."

"Do you know one?"

"Not one who works for nothing."

I looked at the remaining baskets. She followed my eyes.

"I have sold four. I have to move the rest before the sun comes over this roof. If you carry them to the woman who bought them, I can ask the people who come for my jars whether they have heard of a Mulaw. I will ask once. If they say no, that is the end of it."

"Will they be going toward his landing?"

"I have just told you I don't know his landing."

I almost asked whether she could find another person who did. The question was already reaching for work she had not offered.

"Where are the baskets going?" I asked instead.

She pointed past the market to a narrow house with a red clay jar outside. It was farther than I expected. There were four baskets left. I had carried a bundle of bamboo and eaten a stranger's rice, and my shoulders were already sore.

"All four?"

"Two now. Two after I have counted them with the woman."

"What will she pay you?"

"That is between us."

I nodded. She lifted one basket, then set it down again.

"Don't take it until she has seen the lid. I don't want you saying I sent her an opened basket."

We waited for the woman to come. She wore a dark cloth at her waist and had a little boy beside her who carried a shallow bowl. She examined the knots on the first two lids, pressed the wicker where it was damp, and made Sera show her the fish beneath. They disagreed over one basket. The woman wanted the damp pieces set apart. Sera said they had been damp when she bought them, and that she had turned them in the morning. They settled on leaving that basket for another buyer.

Only three went to the house with the red jar.

I carried the first one against my chest. The wicker rubbed my arms where the scratches from the old landing had long since healed. The boy walked ahead of me and called to his mother whenever I reached a turn. He was proud of being able to show a stranger the way. I followed him because the basket was too wide for me to see the stones underfoot.

At the house the woman made me set it beside the jar, not under the eaves.

"The shade moves," she said. "I will carry it in when I have room."

I went back for the second. By then Sera had sold the damp basket to a man who wanted it for his household, at a price I did not hear. She was counting the pieces of fish in the last two baskets with her poleman. He held his sore hand above the wicker and used the other to turn the pieces.

"Are you still carrying?" he asked.

"Two more."

"Then carry. The tide won't wait for either of us."

The third basket was heavier than the first. I had to stop halfway and put it on a stone to change my grip. A girl coming from the river stepped around me without slowing. She carried a jar on her head with one hand resting lightly against its side. I watched her go and wondered how long I would have to work before a basket could be carried without thinking about it.

When I returned, Sera had closed the last lid.

"That is the third," she said. "The woman has them?"

"She saw each one."

"Good."

She gave me a small fish wrapped in a leaf. I did not reach for it.

"I thought you were going to ask about Mulaw."

"I said I would ask. I didn't say I would pay you with an answer."

"Then why the fish?"

"Because you carried my baskets."

I took it. She looked past me toward the water.

"The man collecting the jars may come after the next tide. If he comes, I will ask whether he knows a boat leader by that name. I will tell him you asked. I will not send him to find a man I cannot place, and I will not promise he will come to Maynila."

"Can you tell him Ache asked?"

"I can tell him what you said. Whether he remembers is his own affair."

"Will you come to our landing again?"

"If there is room for what I bring."

There was no anger in it. She had sold her fish here. I had carried three baskets and earned a fish of my own. I had crossed the river hoping for a name that would open a route, and the only route I had found was a path between a market and a house with a red jar.

The older poleman rose and stretched his sore hand. Sera called him to help with the empty baskets. He took one, tested its weight, and passed another to the younger man. She did not ask me to carry them. My work was finished.

I went back by the house with the split bamboo. The man who had brought me across was no longer there. His sister was hanging the pieces beneath the eaves, turning each cut end toward the moving air. She saw the fish in my hand.

"Did you find what you came for?" she asked.

"I found someone who may ask."

She set one piece of bamboo beside another and studied the gap between them.

"Then you have found someone with work of her own."

I did not know whether she meant it kindly. I thanked her for the rice and walked toward the river.

On our bank the new step was visible between the people carrying jars. No boat had tied to the sounding stake. Dala's rack stood where it had been returned. The keeper's strips hung beneath the hall roof, dark against the light. I had no cloth for Amaya and no cord for Dala. I had a fish that I could eat, and the possibility that Sera would remember a question when another buyer came.

I put the fish inside my cloth and went to find out whether Dala had sent for me.
