# Book III, Chapter 6 — Scene 2: The Man Beyond the Bend

**Packet:** RA-B3C6-SCENE-02. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Scene 1 leaves the second boat outside the bend, a seller's buyer moving toward the far bank, the crewman and steersman unpaid for waiting, and the original disputed strip unaltered. This scene hears the absent owner without assuming deceit or a second-trip authorization. **Historical status:** Fictional hearing and marks, not a documented Maynila procedure.

---

By the time I found the man who had wanted the jars, he had already paid to cross.

He stood on the far side of the landing with one foot in a boat and the other on a stone. I called from our bank. He turned his head but did not come back. I could see the pole-handler waiting behind him, his pole planted in the mud.

"The jars are still here," I said.

"So am I," he answered.

"Only for a moment."

He looked toward the woman who had followed me down the path. She raised a hand.

"Come back after the water falls," she called. "I will send them by a smaller boat."

"I have paid for this one."

"I will pay the difference."

He glanced at the pole-handler, who made no movement. The man took his foot off the stone.

"You said they would be here before the water rose."

"I did."

"Then I cannot wait until it falls."

He stepped into the boat. The pole-handler pushed away. I watched them cross while the woman beside me counted the jars aloud, as though saying the number might keep them from being lost.

"Three," she said. "Three jars. Two baskets. None delivered."

"I saw them."

"I know you saw them. You have been watching all morning."

She turned away before I could answer. At the hall she took her cover from the table, shook dust from it, and folded it under her arm. The keeper did not ask her to stay. The woman's bargain with the boat owner had been made without the keeper, and she had no reason to wait for our quarrel to end.

The small-boat owner came after the midday heat had begun to break. He arrived on foot from the upper path, carrying a cord bag in one hand and a wet sandal in the other. His name was Tawan. I had heard it when he paid for the repaired step's first trial, but I had not used it since. He had been the one to give Raya the cloth for Madan's timber.

He saw the bamboo strip on the table before he saw me.

"Has he brought the second load?"

"He brought it."

"Where is the boat?"

"Beyond the bend."

"Why?"

"The step has not been cleared after the high water. Raya must look at it again."

He set the sandal on the floor.

"Then why did you tell him to come?"

"I did not."

"I told him to come. You told me there were two journeys."

The keeper was sitting on a low stool near the doorway. She rose when he said that. She did not touch the strip.

"I said there were two baskets," she answered.

Tawan looked at her with the patience of a man who had already decided that patience would be required.

"You said two. I put my hand on the two cuts. You nodded."

"Those cuts were for the baskets."

"You made them while we spoke of the boat."

"We spoke of a journey carrying two baskets. The woman paid you for that journey."

"And why would I be paid for a journey I had already made?"

"You had not yet made it," the keeper said.

He turned to me.

"Did I touch the two cuts?"

"Yes."

"Did she nod?"

"Yes."

"Did anyone tell me there would be no second journey?"

I remembered the cargo owner speaking beside the table. I remembered her saying she wanted both baskets carried together. I had stood close enough to hear her, and I had assumed the owner had heard her too. The door had been open. Boats were passing outside. The keeper had turned the strip so the marks faced the light.

"I heard one journey," I said.

"That is not what I asked."

"No one said to me that you had been promised a second."

"That is still not what I asked."

He took the sandal from the floor and worked a finger through its broken thong. It had been mended once with dark fiber. The new break was beside the old knot.

"I did not come here to take another woman's baskets," he said. "I came to see why my crew was told to wait without a place to tie. He has been holding the boat since morning. He says you sent him away."

"I did."

"On whose word?"

"On the keeper's account and the cargo owner's payment."

"Did you ask mine?"

"You were gone."

"That was why I left the crewman. He was to take the boat back."

The keeper held the strip toward him by its uncut end.

"Look at it again. One long cut for one journey. Two short cuts for the goods."

He did not take it.

"Those are the marks you say you made. I know what I heard."

The keeper lowered the strip. For the first time I saw her look at me rather than at him.

"You stood with us," she said.

"I did."

"You saw him touch the marks."

"I did."

"And you did not ask him to say what he understood."

"No."

Tawan looked from one of us to the other. The room had become very quiet. Outside, a child struck a pot with a stick and was told to stop.

"Fetch the woman who paid," I said.

Tawan sat down on the threshold.

"I will wait."

The keeper sent a boy to the house where the cargo owner had gone after yesterday's delivery. I went toward the bend to find the crewman. Tawan followed me, carrying his sandal. He walked faster than I expected, and I had to step aside when the path narrowed.

The boat was in a pocket of quiet water below the roots of a large tree. The older steersman had set the oar across the gunwales. He was sitting with his knees drawn up, his eyes closed. The crewman stood on the bank, trying to work a splinter from his palm with the edge of a shell.

"Did you bring him?" the crewman asked when he saw us.

"I came myself," Tawan said.

The young man held out his hand. The splinter was still there.

"I brought the jars. They sent me away."

"I know."

"The buyer has gone."

"I know that too."

Tawan took the shell from him, held his wrist against the light, and lifted the splinter free. The crewman winced but did not pull back. The owner gave him the shell again.

"You did what I told you," he said.

"Then will you pay me for today?"

Tawan's hand stopped in the air.

"I paid you for the first journey."

"And I have worked the second."

"The second was not carried."

"I was here."

The older steersman opened his eyes.

"I have held the boat in the current since morning," he said. "That work does not disappear because we never touched the step."

Tawan rubbed the broken sandal thong between his fingers. He had come ready to accuse us of changing a bargain. His own crew had now brought him a claim he had not prepared to answer.

"I will pay you for the waiting," he said to the older man. "I must first collect what is owed for the jars."

The older man looked at the empty water beyond the bend.

"The jars have not been carried."

"Then I will find another way."

The crewman held up his palm. The small cut where the splinter had been was beginning to bleed.

"Will you pay me too?"

Tawan looked at him.

"Yes. I have said so."

"You said you would pay me for two journeys."

"I said I would pay you for the work."

"Then tell me how much waiting is worth."

The owner did not answer. The older man lowered his feet into the bottom of the boat and leaned forward to examine the line. It had been rubbed raw where it passed over the bow.

I had thought bringing the owner would end the dispute. It had made three disputes visible.

"Come to the hall," I said. "The woman who paid for yesterday's baskets is being fetched."

Tawan picked up his bag.

"Will Raya be there?"

"I have not found her yet."

"Then we will still have no step."

"Not until she says it can be used."

The crewman looked at me. "And if she says it cannot?"

"Then it cannot."

He turned to Tawan.

"You told me they had agreed to let us come twice."

"I believed they had."

"Will you tell the woman that?"

"I will tell her what I heard."

The older man lifted the line and began coiling it. He did not follow us. He had been promised payment for waiting and had no reason to spend the rest of the afternoon hearing the same words.

The woman whose two baskets had gone upriver was at the hall when we returned. She stood beside the keeper, holding the same mat that had covered her goods. A small piece had torn from one corner. She smoothed it while Tawan came through the door.

"I paid you for one journey," she said.

"I carried your baskets."

"You did."

"Then we agree on that."

"We agree that you carried them once. I paid for that."

"You spoke of two."

"Two baskets. I held them both up. One had wax. The other had cord. You put them beside each other in your boat."

Tawan set his cord bag down.

"I was at the doorway when you said two. The boy was pulling the line. I came to the table when the keeper called me. I saw two cuts. I asked whether those were mine."

The keeper looked at him.

"You asked whether they were the two. I said yes. I meant the baskets."

"Then you did not hear what I asked."

"Nor you what I answered."

The cargo owner stopped smoothing the mat.

"Whatever either of you heard, I did not buy a second journey for another woman's jars."

Tawan looked at the strip. This time he took it by the uncut end and turned it toward the light. He touched the long cut with one finger and the two short cuts with another.

"I see how you meant it," he said to the keeper. "I did not see it yesterday."

The keeper took back the bamboo.

"Then we should have asked you to tell us."

Tawan nodded, but his face had not softened.

"That would have cost a moment. Today has cost my men a day."

"It has cost me a buyer," the other woman said from the doorway.

She had returned without her cover. I had not heard her approach. Tawan turned toward her.

"I was told you had another load."

"I did. You told me you could carry it. You did not say the journey belonged to someone else's payment."

"I thought the step was ours for two turns."

"It is not yours," the keeper said.

Tawan looked at her sharply.

"I know the wood is not mine. I helped pay for it. I did not buy the bank."

He said it as if the distinction were obvious. I had been ready to accuse him of treating the step as his property. He had just said the opposite.

"Then we have to speak of the new load separately," I said.

The woman folded her arms.

"After I have lost my buyer?"

"I cannot bring him back."

"No. You cannot."

She left again. This time no one called after her.

Tawan stayed beside the table. The cargo owner took her mat and went home, satisfied that no one had asked her to pay twice. The keeper laid the bamboo flat where all three of us could see it.

"I will not cut another mark over these," she said.

"I am not asking you to," Tawan answered. "I want something I can understand before I send a man with my boat."

I had wanted to rule for the keeper when he first arrived. I still thought she had recorded the bargain she and the cargo owner intended. But I had watched Tawan touch the marks. I had seen him leave with a different understanding, and I had been pleased to let him go.

"The crewman heard you say two," I told him. "He did not hear what was carried by that number."

"He was at the bow."

"Yes."

"He was doing his work."

"Yes."

Tawan looked down at the bamboo.

"Then he cannot answer for what we failed to say to each other."

I nodded. It was the first thing that day on which we had all agreed.

The keeper reached for a fresh strip. I put my hand on the table before she could cut it.

"Wait," I said. "We should ask who else has to hear the words."

Outside, the river was falling away from the repaired step. Raya had still not examined it. The crewman's pay had been promised but not counted. The woman who had lost her buyer was already finding another bank.

The keeper set the uncut bamboo beside the first strip and left them both where we could see them.
