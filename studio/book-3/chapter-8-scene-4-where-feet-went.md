# Scene 4 — Where the Feet Went

Bina came back with fish the next morning.

I had not expected her. I had gone to the landing to see whether Raya had opened the step. Her staff still lay across the upper path. The timber beneath it had been lifted clear at one end and propped above the wet hollow. A small patch of fresh earth showed where Raya had begun to clear the loose mud. The step could not bear a foot. I turned away before she could find me standing over work I did not understand.

At the market Bina was lowering a basket from her canoe. Kito stood in the stern with his pole upright. His scraped heel was wrapped in a dry strip of cloth.

"Did Nali speak?" I asked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Good morning."

"Carry this first. I must sell the fish before I can spend time with your keeper."

I took the basket she indicated. She had already set a second one on the bank, where the ground was firm. I carried the first to the shade she used yesterday and came back for the other. Kito kept the canoe clear of the stones while Bina counted the fish.

"Does that pay for your account?" I asked.

"It pays for carrying two baskets. Yesterday you carried one to the drying shed. Today you have carried two to the market. We have made no bargain about a third."

I wanted to ask whether she had remembered every word. I had not been there to hear them, and she had already warned me what my asking would sound like.

"Will you come when you have sold?" I asked.

"If the keeper is willing to hear me. If a buyer comes, I will serve the buyer first."

I left her to the sale. The keeper was opening the hall. I told her Bina had returned.

"Then let her come when she is ready," she said.

"Should I put the strip on the table?"

"Which strip?"

"The one for Ranu's crossing."

"I will take it down if she asks to see it. We are not going to make her answer a piece of wood."

I sat on the low bench. A man came with a broken jar and asked whether the keeper knew who had carried it from the landing. She did not. He left the jar beside the door while he went to ask the carrier. It was still there when Bina arrived.

Bina brought no strip and no cord. She carried a small fish basket under her arm, empty now except for the smell of the morning's catch. Kito did not follow. He was taking the canoe to a place upstream where the current would not drive it against the bank while she spoke.

"How long can you stay?" the keeper asked.

"Until Kito calls or a buyer finds me. I am not here for a hearing that lasts all day."

The keeper took the marked strip from the shelf and placed it beside the blank one. Bina looked at them without sitting.

"I took Iya to her sister's bank," she began. "Nali was lifting the rack's broken end onto a dry stone. Iya asked whether she would tell me what she wanted said here. Nali asked first who had hired me."

"What did you answer?" I asked.

"I told her Iya had paid for the journey home. I told her you had carried my fish to the drying shed in exchange for the time I spent hearing the keeper. I said no one had hired me to bring back a particular answer."

"Did she believe you?"

"She asked Iya. Iya told her the same. Then she asked whether I would take a payment from her to say the words differently. I told her I would not."

The keeper laid her palm flat on the table.

"What did Nali want corrected?"

Bina looked at me.

"She said the path she allowed Ranu to use was the higher ground behind the roots. She did not let Ranu pass through the rack path."

I had pictured Ranu stepping along the open strip beside the damaged rack. From our bank I had seen her return to the canoe. The roots hid the part between her yard and the water.

"Iya said Nali allowed her path," I said.

"Iya did. Nali says there are two paths there. One runs above the roots to the place where the canoe can be held at low water. The other passes the drying rack and the place where the children carry their fish. She allowed Ranu to return by the first. She did not open the second."

"Did you see Ranu use the first?" the keeper asked.

"No. I did not carry Ranu. I saw Nali point to the higher ground yesterday and say that was the way she had shown Ranu. I saw the rack path from where I stood. The broken end was still tied up. I cannot tell you where Ranu's foot went the day before."

The keeper picked up the strip.

"I cut one mark for the return," she said. "I did not cut one for which path."

"Then the mark is missing something," I said.

"The mark was never meant to show a path. I wrote no words on the wood. I told you what I had heard."

Bina waited until the keeper set it down.

"Nali said another thing. The man with the two bundles came on foot from above the bend. He did not come from a boat at her landing, as far as she saw. She sent him away because he claimed permission for the rack path. She did not accuse your boatmen of bringing him."

"Iya told us that," I said.

"She did. Nali wanted you to hear it from someone who had heard her say it, because people have begun speaking as though a boat from here must have carried the man."

I remembered my own question to Iya. How could she say the permission came from our landing? She had answered that she had not said it did. I had still been imagining a boat.

"Did Nali name the person who broke the rack?" I asked.

"No. She said the end was broken before Ranu came. She does not know who broke it. She has not asked Ranu to pay for it."

"And did she agree to another crossing?"

"No."

Bina said it without waiting for me to explain why another crossing might be useful.

"She said she will hear a person who asks before coming with a load. She will decide whether to let that person walk. She has not agreed to keep the root path open whenever a boat arrives. She has not agreed to let anyone use the rack path."

The keeper took the blank strip. She turned it in her hands but did not cut it.

"May I mark that you heard Nali say those things?" she asked.

"Mark that I told you I heard them. If someone asks me, I will say what I heard. If I have remembered badly, I want Nali free to correct me."

The keeper made one short cut on the blank strip and placed it apart from the older one. She said aloud what the cut would remind her of: Bina's return with Nali's correction, not Nali's agreement to anything.

"Do you want me to take your first strip back?" Bina asked.

"No. The people who saw Ranu leave may still correct my memory of that day. I will keep both. I will tell anyone who asks why they are different."

I had been waiting for the keeper to cut a mark that would end the argument. Now there were two pieces of wood and more to say when a stranger asked what they meant.

"Could you point out the two paths to someone from here?" I asked Bina.

"I could point where Nali pointed. That would not give the person leave to use either one."

"I know."

"Then you can tell him I saw them. Do not say I have shown him a landing."

She moved the empty basket from one arm to the other. She had given us the time she agreed to give, and I could see her thinking of the fish she had left in the shade.

A voice came from the doorway.

It was the owner with the coil of cord. The same younger man who had held his boat yesterday stood behind him. The owner looked at the two strips on the table.

"Which one will you show the pots coming tomorrow?" he asked.

"Neither will give them a landing," the keeper said. "The first reminds us of Ranu's one crossing. The second reminds us of a correction from Nali."

"What correction?"

Bina told him. She did not leave out the higher root path, the rack path, or the man who had come on foot. She did not tell him that every account had been settled.

He listened until she finished.

"I have told two owners they can bring their pots to the marked water," he said. "One has already paid men to carry them down to his boat."

"Did you tell them they had a right to put ashore?" I asked.

"I told them I had seen a crossing and that someone here was keeping the account."

"Then tell them what that crossing allowed."

"Will they hear the rest of your account before their boat is in the current?"

He looked at the keeper. She did not answer for me.

"I will pay men to watch the pots," he said. "If a boat arrives and the step is closed, they can keep the cargo dry while I find another place. That is my work to arrange. But I will not bring a boat here if you will say only what it cannot do."

Raya's staff was still across the upper step. The old stones would be covered by the time a heavy boat came. I could not offer him a place to land.

"I can tell you who must be asked," I said. "Dala for her path. Raya for the landing. The boat owner for the journey. If the pots must go across, Nali for her ground."

"Four answers," he said. "And the boat is paid for before I hear the first."

"Then ask before the boat leaves."

He shook his head.

"I came to hear what I could tell the owners today. I will tell them the step is closed and no other landing has been promised. They may keep their pots or choose another channel."

He did not leave at once. He looked at Bina.

"Would you carry that answer to them?"

"No. I have fish to sell."

"Even for pay?"

"Not today. You have heard it yourself."

The younger man touched the cord at the owner's shoulder. They had a boat to move and men waiting for an answer. The owner took the cord down, wound it once around his hand, and went out.

Bina watched him go.

"Was he here when the keeper told you the first account?" she asked.

"He was here yesterday," I said. "He wanted something he could show the owners."

"Then he will tell them what he heard today. You cannot make him repeat all of it."

I wanted to follow him and make certain. Instead I asked Bina whether I had kept my part of our bargain.

"You carried the fish," she said. "I came back when I chose and told the keeper what I heard. That is finished."

"And if Nali has another answer tomorrow?"

"Then you must ask who is willing to hear it."

Kito called from the path. Bina lifted her basket.

I had been ready to pay someone to carry my words across the river. A woman who owed me nothing had carried back a correction. The owner with the cord had heard it, and two boats of pots might never come. I had no way to stop him leaving with a shorter version than the one Bina gave us.

The keeper put both strips on the shelf with space between them. She left the blank wood she had not used beside her knife.
