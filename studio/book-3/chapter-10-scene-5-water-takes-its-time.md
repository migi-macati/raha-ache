# Scene 5 — The Water Takes Its Time

Marun reached Sela's awning before the fish were packed.

He was breathing hard from the walk and had mud on one foot. Sela set down the binding she was twisting and asked what Raya had said.

"She has not opened the step. She wants the water lower before she puts a basket on it."

Sela looked toward the sun. "Will she look before the buyer leaves?"

"She did not tell me when the buyer leaves. She told me to say the step is closed until she tests it."

Sela gave him the grain she had promised. He counted it in his palm and went back to the market. He had brought an answer worth paying for, though it was not the one she wanted.

I had come to help bind the fish. Sela had asked me the evening before, and I had told her I would come for the first hour. Amaya knew where I was. She had asked whether the work would earn anything toward the cloth. I told her Sela had offered food and a little grain for carrying after the fish were packed, if the landing opened. If it stayed shut, I would still help with the binding I had promised.

Sela had one basket ready and another covered beside it. The first was for the buyer above the bend. The second was for a different buyer who could be reached from the farther landing. She had not asked Dala for ground to hold both.

"Shall I take the first down?" I asked.

"Not until Raya calls it usable. I have paid Marun to tell me the truth. I will not make him walk twice because I do not like it."

She pulled the binding tight. I held the basket steady. The fish had been dried enough to travel, but the air was damp, and Sela did not want them uncovered while we waited.

A boy came from the path to say the buyer had sent him. Sela told him the fish were packed and the step had not opened. She asked whether the buyer would wait until midday. The boy said he would ask and ran back. Sela did not make him carry a promise he had not heard accepted.

***

At the bank, Dala had lifted the net and hung part of it above the high root. The rest lay folded beside her feet. She showed Sela the dry patch and pointed to the mark on the tree.

"The water has not reached it," she said. "You may put your one basket here while the path is clear. If I need the net again, I will tell you."

Raya was below them with a smaller basket filled with stones. She had chosen the stones herself and had not asked Sela to risk her fish in the first test. She put the weight on the new timber and stood beside it, listening as much as looking. The wood made a low sound when she shifted the load.

"Take it back," she said.

I lifted the basket and carried it to the dry bank. She crouched at the lower bearing. Water was moving through the gravel she had packed there. The stone itself had not turned, but a thin stream had begun to carry the smallest pieces away.

"Is it failing?" Laman asked from his boat.

"It is changing," she said. "If I say it is holding now, that does not tell you what it will do after another load."

Hiran had sounded the approach and was keeping Laman's bow away from the stone. She had agreed to a pole journey and a waiting measure. The boat was still empty. Laman asked whether she wanted to move farther out.

"Yes," she said. "The water is running harder beside that root. I will not hold the hull there."

They moved the boat a little downstream. The longer reach would mean an extra step for whoever carried the fish. Raya watched them move and did not call them back.

Sela arrived with the packed basket on her shoulder. She put it on Dala's dry patch, as agreed, and asked whether Raya would open the step.

"Not yet. I need to pack the gravel with larger pieces. I can do it while the water is low enough to reach."

"How long?"

"I do not know."

Sela turned to Laman. "If we wait, will you keep the boat?"

"I will if the owner pays for waiting. I have agreed to carry your basket if the step is safe. I have not agreed to stand here until dark."

Sela said she would pay him the smaller waiting measure they had discussed. Hiran heard the amount and asked whether her waiting was included. Laman said it was not; he would pay her separately, as promised. She agreed to stay until the water reached the dark mark on her pole. After that she would choose again.

The boy returned with the buyer's answer. He would wait until the sun was above the high roof, but not beyond it. He had another seller to visit. Sela sent the boy back with thanks. He went without a fish basket.

We waited while Raya found two stones large enough to hold the gravel. Madan had left an offcut from the timber near the bank. She used it as a lever, not as another step. I helped her lift the larger stone into place. Mud squeezed around my fingers. When the stone settled, the small stream slowed.

Raya washed her hands, then put the stone basket on the timber again. She carried it across herself. The lower bearing stayed in place. She came back and repeated the crossing with the weight held to one side, where a tired carrier might let it pull. The timber moved a little but did not roll.

"One carried load at a time," she said. "No stopping on the step. No boat tied to it. If the water reaches that pale mark, we stop."

Sela looked at the mark. It was lower than the one Dala had named for her basket. The two limits belonged to different work. Both could close the morning.

***

I took the fish basket from Dala's patch. It was heavier than Raya's test load. Sela saw me lift it and shook her head.

"Half first," she said.

She untied the cover and moved part of the fish into a smaller basket she had brought folded beneath the large one. Dala watched the transfer.

"You asked for one basket to wait," she said.

"Only the large one will wait. The small one goes now and comes back empty."

Dala considered that. "Then take it. Do not leave it on my path."

I carried the smaller load down the step. The wood was firm beneath my first foot. At the second step the basket pulled toward the river, and I shifted it closer to my body. I did not stop on the timber. Hiran held the boat where she had sounded safe water, and Laman took the basket from me without making the hull touch the step. I went back with the empty one.

The water had risen while I crossed. Raya looked at the pale mark, then at the gravel below the timber.

"Wait," she said.

I put the empty basket on dry ground. Sela had already moved the remaining fish into it. She did not lift it.

A thin thread of water was running again between the stones. Raya pressed the lower bearing with her pole. The wood held, but she could see the gravel shift beneath it.

"We close now," she said.

"The rest of the fish is here," Sela answered.

"I can see it."

"The buyer will take half a basket for half the price."

"Then that is what you may have sold today."

Sela stood over the fish. I had expected her to argue. She turned to Laman and asked whether he could deliver the smaller load to the buyer while she took the rest by the upper path.

Laman looked at Hiran. "Will you go with what we have?"

Hiran sounded once more. "Yes, if we leave now. I will not wait for another basket."

Laman agreed to carry the smaller load for a reduced price, but said he had still paid Hiran for her full morning. Sela accepted the charge. She had bought waiting and a journey, even though the load was smaller than she had planned.

I carried the remaining fish back up the path with Sela. Dala helped us clear the dry patch because she wanted to set her net again. She did not take responsibility for our loss. She had given the ground she said she would give, for the time she could spare.

At the market Sela found a carrier willing to take the remaining fish overland to the buyer. He named a price higher than she liked. She asked whether he could arrive before the sun reached the roof. He said he could try but would not promise the buyer would still be there. She paid him to try.

By the time I returned to the landing, Laman's boat had gone. Raya had lifted the testing basket away. She was putting larger stones beside the bearing, but she would not set them until the water fell again.

"Did the step fail?" I asked.

"It held the load we put on it. Then the water told me to stop. If I had called it open for the whole morning, I would have been wrong before the second basket."

"Will Sela come back?"

"Ask Sela."

The buyer took the smaller load Laman brought. He did not take the overland basket; the carrier reached him after he had left for another market. Sela paid the carrier for the walk. She sold the remaining fish later at a lower price. When she told me, she did not ask Raya to pay the difference.

We had carried a real load over the new step. We had also lost a sale while the water rose. I could point to both things without changing either of them.
