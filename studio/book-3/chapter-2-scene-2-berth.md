# Book III, Chapter 2 — Scene 2: The Berth They Would Not Give

**Packet:** RA-B3C2-SCENE-02. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 1's informal hearing and Amaya's invitation to inspect the hall the following day. No recognition of Ache as ruler, no armed fleet or wedding. Harun remains the owner of his trading vessel; Sulad earns wages at the nets; Isan's work is voluntary. **Historical status:** Fictional port negotiation; no specific 1520s tariff, office or named Tondo ruler attested.

---

The roof was better than the one I remembered.

Amaya stood beneath the new beams and watched me look up. Rain had darkened the old timber near the eaves. The newer pieces were pale and straight. A cord ran from one post to another, holding strips of fish where the wind could reach them without letting the dogs take them.

"Your mother said the roof should be mended," Amaya told me. "She did not say which trees would give us the wood."

A woman at the far end was beating dust from a mat. Two men carried a basket between them, one walking backward to clear the cooking fire. Nobody stopped when I came in.

"Who paid for the beams?" I asked.

"People who needed the roof. The timber came from a man who had no place to dry his fish. He gave us two lengths and took space under the eaves for a season."

"Does he still use it?"

"His daughter does. She will tell you herself if you ask."

I had imagined a hall where every object might prove that something had been stolen from me. Amaya was showing me things that had been made after I left. The work had owners even when the floor did not.

She took me beneath the raised room. A man was shaving a paddle with a curved blade. His daughter held the wood steady. He did not rise.

"Can you repair a boat?" Amaya asked me.

"I can help."

"That is what I asked."

"I can mend a seam. I know how to set a steering line. I have helped move a hull out of mud."

The man looked up.

"Can you pay for a seam you tear while learning?"

"I would have to."

"Then you can help after I have finished the part that matters."

Amaya smiled. She did not tell him who I was.

By the time we reached the landing, the woman who had given us the tool room was standing beside two owners whose boats lay on the mud. One owned a broad ferry with a raised platform. The other was a woman with two narrow boats that carried jars and dried fish between the upper landing and the bay. The broad ferry's owner had brought a measuring pole. The woman had brought her younger brother, who knew the depth of the channel after every rain.

Banu stood apart from them. He had not promised to bring anyone. He had come because he needed to take a load across the river before noon.

"I asked them to hear what you want," the landing woman said. "I did not ask them to give it to you."

"Thank you."

"Save that until you know what they say."

I told them I wanted the landing kept open while the households decided what place I might have in Maynila. I said I would help repair the lower steps and clear the mud where the boats turned.

The ferry owner lifted his measuring pole.

"Whose men?"

"Those who agree to work."

"Whose food?"

"I can pay for some."

"With what?"

I had kept the beads and cloth from my gifts. I could sell them, but once they were gone I would have little left. I had been counting on my name to open a way to the next day. The owner waited while I thought.

"With what I earn," I said. "And what people choose to contribute."

He made a sound through his nose.

"Then you have a day's work and a day's food. You do not have a landing."

The woman with the narrow boats crouched beside the water. She put her hand into the mud and brought up a broken stake. Its point had rotted.

"This is why I lose time here," she said. "My men cannot see where the bank falls away. Last week one of my boats grounded and we had to lift half the jars out before it would float."

"Would you pay to replace the stakes?"

"I would pay for one. The people who use the landing would have to pay for the rest. I will not mend the river for a man who says he has returned to own it."

"I have not said that."

"You may say it after I have paid."

She broke the stake across her knee. The wood fell apart where it had been under water.

The ferry owner pointed his pole toward the far bank.

"Your mother's people once asked us to keep the channel open when the water fell. We did. Then other men came and said we had no right to carry passengers without paying them. If you take back the landing, will you demand the same?"

"I do not know who demanded it."

"That is why I am asking you."

The landing woman stepped between us to let a porter pass with a basket. She waited until he was clear.

"Tell him what you will do," she said to me. "Do not tell him what you hope he will believe."

I asked the ferry owner what it cost to bring his boat across. He told me how many men he fed, what he paid for replacement paddles, and how much time he lost when the landing was crowded. I listened. I had crossed the river as a passenger and called the fare reasonable or unfair depending on the coin in my hand. I had never owned a boat that had to come back whether anyone paid or not.

"I will not charge you for landing while the steps are being repaired," I said. "After that, no new fee without the owners who use them being heard."

"And if the men across the river refuse?"

"Then there is no new fee."

The ferry owner laughed.

"You have made yourself a poor ruler."

"I have not been made a ruler."

"That may be why you can still say it."

He drove the measuring pole into the mud, took it out, and showed us the mark. He would lend it for one morning if someone promised to bring it back. He would not lend his boat.

The woman with the narrow boats offered a different bargain. She would pay for two new channel stakes if the keeper and the landing workers chose where to place them. She would carry a load of timber on one boat when it was not needed for trade. She would not give Ache the use of the second boat, and she would not take passengers into a quarrel with the far bank.

"Who will make the stakes?" I asked.

"The man you saw shaving the paddle," Amaya said. "If he agrees."

"Who will put them in?"

The woman's brother looked at the water.

"Someone who knows where the bottom is."

Sulad came up the path carrying a net over his shoulder. The net was wet and heavy. A boy followed with the floats. Sulad had been working since before sunrise. He stopped when he saw us.

"You have found people to give you boats," he said.

"They have not given me any."

"Then you are learning."

The woman pointed to the broken stake.

"Can you set a new one without putting it in the wrong water?"

Sulad looked at the mud, then at the bank beyond it.

"Not without taking a boat out at low tide and feeling the bottom."

"Would you do it?"

"Who pays?"

She named a price for a half day's work. Sulad looked at the boy with the net.

"I have this to finish first."

"We can wait until tomorrow," she said.

He agreed to sound the channel for one morning. He would bring his own pole. He would be paid whether the owners decided to use his measurements or not.

I wanted to tell him I was glad he had joined me. He had joined a job. The difference was plain enough that I kept quiet.

Isan had been listening from beside the shelter. He came forward when the woman with the narrow boats asked whether the people from Burné would be bringing more goods.

"Harun will sell what he has," he said. "He has not promised another voyage."

"Can you speak for him?"

"I can tell you what he told me. I cannot sell his goods or make his promises."

The woman nodded. She asked whether Isan would help her brother speak with a trader whose words he understood poorly. Isan asked what she would pay. They agreed on a basket of rice and a small piece of cloth for two days' work, provided the trader came before Harun left.

I looked at him.

"Will you still come with me to the hall?"

"When I am not working for her," he said. "If you need me at the same time, ask me first."

He had come from Burné to help me find my shore. Now he had found work on it. I could not object without proving that I had brought him here as a servant.

The ferry owner lifted his pole.

"I will take passengers tomorrow," he said. "I will not take your quarrel. If the far-bank house sends men with weapons, I will turn my boat away."

"Then tell them I have asked for a hearing," I said.

"They already know. Their man came yesterday."

"Did he tell you what he thought?"

"He told me you had no soldiers."

"He was right."

"He also said you listened while a woman spoke of the roof. That was harder for him to believe."

The owner left with his pole. I had not won his boat. I had won nothing from him except the promise that he would keep doing his own work, which he had meant to do anyway.

Amaya watched the narrow-boat owner walk toward the carpenter.

"She will have the stakes made," Amaya said. "But if you want men to help put them in, you must ask the people who use the steps."

"I will."

"And if they say no?"

"Then I will carry the stakes with those who say yes."

"Do not carry them into deep water to prove you are willing. Ask Sulad where they belong."

I looked toward the nets. He was already walking away with the boy, the heavy mesh slung between them.

In the afternoon I went to Harun's anchorage. He had sold two bundles of cloth and was repairing the wrapping on a third. A trader from the upper river was inspecting his resin. The buyer did not look up when I climbed aboard.

"How long will you stay?" I asked Harun.

"Long enough to finish this trade."

"I have found work on the landing."

"Good."

"Some of the owners will repair the channel."

"Better. It will make their boats easier to bring in."

"I may need to send word upriver."

Harun looked at me then.

"With whose boat?"

"That is what I came to ask."

He shook his head before I finished.

"My boat goes where I can sell my cargo. I will not carry messages into a dispute over your mother's house."

"I could pay."

"With what?"

I had been asked the same question twice that day. I did not answer quickly enough.

"When you have something to pay with," he said, "we can speak about a voyage. Until then, ask the people who work that river."

I stood beside the wet cloth, feeling the deck move under my feet. It had carried me from Burné. I had been grateful for it until I began to need it for something its owner had never promised.

"Will you leave soon?"

"When the wind is right and the goods are sold."

"Then I should thank you while you are here."

"You paid for your passage," he said. "Keep the rest for when you have a passage of your own to arrange."

He went back to his buyer.

At dusk I returned to the tool room. The keeper had set the mallet back beneath the bench. Isan was sitting on his mat, drying the cloth he had earned. I told him about the two stakes, Sulad's half-day work, the boat that would carry timber and the ferry that would not carry me.

"How many boats do you have?" he asked.

"None."

"How many will work with you?"

"One for a load of timber, when its owner has no other trade."

"That is not the same thing."

"I know."

He folded his cloth carefully and put it away.

"Then tomorrow you should ask what the timber is for."

I lay down where the roof did not leak. The landing was still someone else's. The hall was still full of people who had kept it standing. But two stakes might soon mark a safer channel, and I had begun to learn whose hands could put them there.
