# Scene 1 — The Full Basket

The first jar knocked against the second before we had lifted the basket.

Amaya heard the sound and put her hand on the rim. I had set the jars too close together. Their shoulders touched where the reed packing thinned, and a dark line of old glaze showed through the gap.

"Take them out," she said.

"We have not moved."

"That is why we can still take them out."

The house above the bend belonged to a woman who had kept the jars under her eaves while Amaya repaired a cover for her. The woman had already brought the cover home. Amaya had come to collect the empty vessels she had lent her for the work. They were not heavy, but the neck of one was narrower than the other, and the basket had been woven for a different load.

I lifted them out. Amaya laid a handful of dry reeds across the bottom, bent them into two rings, and made me set each jar inside one. She pressed another bundle between their shoulders.

"Now move the basket without lifting it."

I drew it a hand's breadth across the mat. The jars stayed apart.

"Again."

I moved it farther. One jar leaned. I stopped before it struck the other and pushed the reeds back into place.

"That is what you will do when the path turns," she said. "Stop. Do not wait for the sound."

The woman of the house watched from the doorway. She had a little boy beside her who kept trying to touch the basket. She took his hand away each time he reached for it.

"Will you send the jars back when you need them?" she asked Amaya.

"If I can spare them. I have been using smaller ones since the cloth went missing."

She did not look at me. The woman knew whose hands had lost the cloth. I bent over the basket and tightened the binding around its handles.

On the way home Amaya walked behind me. The path was dry at first. Where it dipped beneath the trees, water had gathered around a root. I stepped over it and felt the basket pull against my wrist. I stopped, put it down on firm ground, and checked the reeds. Amaya waited without offering to carry the weight.

"You could have crossed there in one stride," she said.

"I could have broken the jars."

"Then you have learned something from carrying cloth."

I wanted to ask whether that was praise. She was already looking at the next turn. I picked up the basket again.

At her house we set the jars beside the wall. She tested both rims and ran a finger along their sides. There was no new crack. She took the reeds out and kept the pieces that had not been crushed.

"Those will serve again," she said.

I stood with the empty basket in my hands. The rice she had taken from my carrying work was in her store jar. It was enough for a meal if she chose to cook it, and nowhere near the price of the cloth I owed.

"Will you ask Tesa for more work tomorrow?" she asked.

"If she has any."

"Ask before you promise Raya your hands."

"Raya may need me to lift the new timber."

"Has Madan given her timber?"

"Not yet."

"Then do not miss work for a piece of wood no one has sold you."

I set the basket down. She took the old cloth measure from a shelf and unfolded it. The square patch was still darker than the rest.

"When I have enough to buy another," I said, "will you come and choose it?"

"Yes. If you tell me what you have earned before you call it enough."

She folded the measure again. I had asked her to trust a future price. She had answered with the work I could do tomorrow.

***

At the next market the river was crowded before the sun cleared the trees.

Two boats had passed our turning without slowing. A third waited at the bend, its owner standing in the stern while a carrier on shore called that the farther landing was full. The owner held his boat against the current and shouted back. I could not hear all of it. I heard the word for waiting more than once.

I went down to the market path, where Marun was carrying an empty pole. He had been paid to bring baskets to the farther landing and had come back without them.

"Did they turn you away?" I asked.

"No. The owner who hired me knew there was room when we left. Another boat came before us and took the dry patch. The man there said he would clear it when the first load was gone."

"How long did you wait?"

"Long enough for the buyer to send a boy asking where his fish had gone."

"Then their promise was no better than ours."

Marun shifted the pole to his other shoulder.

"The man told the owner the place was full. He did not tell him it was safe when it was not. The owner could choose to wait or go farther. He chose to wait. He paid me for the time."

"Would you come here next time?"

"If the boat owner hires me and there is a place to put the load. Yesterday I carried for a woman whose buyer lives above the other path. This bank would have made me walk twice as far."

He went on before I could ask another question. I had heard him say the same thing when the first two boats passed our landing. This time I knew what a loaded basket could do to a wrist. I had carried one uphill for Sela and another home for Amaya. The shorter river still did not make every journey shorter.

The cord owner was standing beneath a palm with a group of buyers. His line hung over his shoulder, three knots pulled close together. He had brought them to look at the other channel, just as he said he would. One man carried a jar covered with cloth. Another held a sample of woven mat. A third had come without goods and was asking about a boat he expected after midday.

The cord owner saw me and did not call me over. He was explaining where the farther landing's dry patch began and how its keeper would warn a boat when the space was taken. I waited until he finished.

"Marun says it filled before one boat arrived," I said.

"It did."

"Then what do you tell these owners?"

"That it fills. They may send a man ahead if the load cannot wait. They may hire the other path. They may stay here and hope a place opens. I am telling them what I saw this morning."

The man with the covered jar looked toward our landing.

"Is the step there open?"

"No," I said. "Raya has not repaired it."

"Then there is no choice between the two."

"There is a choice between waiting here and going elsewhere," the cord owner said. "But I will not bring your jars to a rotten step. Ache has told you the same."

The man with the mat sample asked whether a boat could unload at the old stones when the water fell.

"A person crossed there once with a small sample," I said. "That was under Raya's eye and with Dala's permission for the path. It did not open the stones to a loaded boat."

He turned back to the cord owner.

"Then I will ask the man at the farther landing. I need a dry place, not a story about one journey."

The cord owner led them away. He had not hidden our answer. He had used it to show why his own offer was worth hearing.

I could have followed and told them about the timber Madan might show Raya. I had no price for it, no crew and no day when it would be set. The buyers would have heard another promise without a place to put their goods.

***

When I returned to the hall, the keeper was hearing a complaint from a woman whose boat had waited at the farther landing. The woman had paid a carrier to hold a basket beneath a tree while the first boat unloaded. The basket's binding had loosened, and a small jar had slipped against a root.

"Did it break?" the keeper asked.

"No. But I paid the carrier for the waiting, and I had to buy a new binding. The man who said there was room told us the first boat would leave soon. He did not know its owner had gone to find the buyer."

"Who said there was room?"

"The man at the farther landing."

"Did he promise a time?"

"He said the first boat was unloading."

"Then he told you what he saw. You wanted him to tell you how long the owner would be away."

The woman frowned.

"Do you keep marks for the other landing too?"

"I keep what people bring here. If you want that man to answer, you must ask him. I did not hear what he said."

I stood beside the doorway. The keeper had no reason to defend the farther landing. She was asking the same questions she asked about ours. The woman wanted someone to pay for her waiting, and there might be someone who should. The keeper would not invent that person because the basket had nearly broken.

After the woman left, I told the keeper about the boats passing our turning.

"Will they all go there now?" I asked.

"Who are all?"

"The men the cord owner has brought."

"He brought three that you saw. He did not bring the woman who came here with her complaint. She chose that landing before she spoke to us."

"Could we ask the owners to return when Raya finishes?"

"We could ask. They could refuse."

"Then what is left for me to do?"

She pointed to the strip for the disputed watcher charge.

"Salin came from Naru's household to ask about oil. He did not come to join a war between landings. If you speak to Naru, tell him what you know. Do not make him choose a side because you dislike where another man's boat has gone."

I looked at the strip. It was one of several, and the keeper had left room between them. She had not cut a mark for every trader who passed the bend. The river was full of boats whose owners had never asked her anything.

Outside, Amaya called to me from the path. She had brought an empty basket and was going toward Tesa's awning. I followed her.

"Tesa may have work today," she said. "I will ask with you."

"Raya may need me."

"Then ask Raya when she needs you. You cannot carry cloth and timber at the same time."

We passed the bend where the third boat was still waiting. The owner had decided not to follow the other two. His pole-handler stood in the stern, watching the water. No one had forced him to stay. No one had promised him the waiting would be short.

At the awning Tesa was winding a long length of thread. She looked at my hands, then at Amaya's empty basket.

"I have bundles to carry after the cloth is dry," she said. "If you want the work, come when the shade reaches that post. I will not call for you."

I looked at the post. The shade had a long way to travel. Raya might ask me to help lift a piece of wood before then. Madan might arrive with it. The boat at the bend might leave.

"I will come," I said.

Tesa looked at Amaya.

"Did he bring you the rice?"

"He did."

"Then he knows how to finish one job. We will see whether he can finish another."

Amaya set her empty basket beside the mat. She had not come to speak for me. She had come to see whether the work existed before I promised it. I watched the shade move a finger's breadth along the ground and began to count the time until I had to return.
