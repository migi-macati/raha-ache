# Scene 5 — The Bank at Low Water

The next morning the old stones showed their tops.

I could see three of them from the hall, a dark line below the place where Raya had barred the upper step. The water had gone down in the night. A strip of mud lay between the stones and Dala's path, narrow enough that a man with a basket would have put his foot in her drainage groove. I went down before anyone came to tell me that the landing was open.

Raya was already there. She had brought the short staff and the girl with her basket. The loose brace lay where she had left it, above the water, and the upper timber rested on its sound end. Raya had covered the hollow with leaves so the rain would not carry more earth into it.

"Those stones were used before you built the step," I said.

"They were used when people could reach them."

"Can someone reach them now?"

She put the staff against the lowest stone and pressed. The tip slipped on a skin of mud.

"Ask me after I have looked."

I stayed on the higher path. She went down on her hands, testing each place before moving a knee. The girl held her basket out of the way. Raya scraped the mud from the middle stone with a shell, then put one bare foot on it and shifted her weight slowly. The stone did not move. She stepped back and tested the next.

Dala came with her net folded over one arm.

"I was told a woman would bring cane again," she said.

"She has not agreed to come."

"Then why are you standing in my path?"

I moved onto the dry ground above it.

"I wanted to see whether the stones were sound."

"You have seen Raya seeing it."

Dala put her net down where she could keep it out of the run of water. The channel I had mended yesterday still held, though the outer edge had sunk a little. She pressed a pinch of earth into the gap and went back to her knots.

A canoe came from the bend. Iya was in the bow. Beside her sat an older woman holding a bundle of broken rack slats, tied with a cord that had once been white. The man at the stern brought the canoe against the higher bank without touching our stones. He stepped out and held it there while the women climbed onto dry ground.

"This is my sister Nali," Iya said. "She owns the ground where the rack stood."

Nali looked at Dala's net and at the wood across the upper approach.

"Which is the path you have been telling people to use?"

"I have not told anyone to use yours," I said.

"Then perhaps the people who came through my yard did not hear you."

"I do not know who they heard."

She laid the slats on the ground. One had a deep scrape across its end, as though something heavy had been dragged over it. The split peg Iya had carried yesterday fitted the broken corner, but it did not tell us who had struck it.

"I did not come for you to name a man you have not seen," Nali said. "I came because my sister says you have begun asking people where they may put their feet."

"That is what we have been trying to do."

"Then ask me."

I waited.

"There is a narrow path from my yard to the water," she said. "It passes behind the drying rack. The rack is broken, and the fish must be laid farther uphill. A load carried down that path now would knock against the children working there. I will allow one woman to walk from the yard to the water with a small thing in her hands. No carrying poles. No bundles laid on my ground. The men who broke the rack have not been found, and this permission does not settle what they owe."

I looked at Iya. She had not brought Nali to surrender the path on her behalf.

"Does Ranu know?" I asked.

"She has heard what I am willing to say," Nali answered. "She has not agreed to it."

The man who had brought them pulled the canoe farther up. Iya paid him from a small wrapped parcel. He counted the pieces of dried fish before putting them away. He had not come to wait for our discussion; he turned the canoe and left.

We walked to the lower stones. Raya was sitting on her heels beside the lowest one. She did not rise when Nali came.

"Are you the maker?" Nali asked.

"I am the one who says whether this step is safe. The step is closed. The stones are older than my work."

"Can a woman stand on them?"

"One at a time, when the water is low enough, if someone keeps her from stepping into the soft edge. I have not tested the mud above the last stone."

Raya stood, went to the top of the old line, and pushed her staff into the ground. The point sank almost to her fist. She drew it out and walked two paces upstream. There the staff stopped sooner. She made me try it myself. My weight on the staff left a round hole but did not make the bank sag.

"That is the way," she said. "No one jumps from the boat. The boat must hold still, and someone on land must take a hand. Do not put a load down there. If the water covers the second stone, the way closes again. I am not leaving the upper step open because this is possible."

Dala came to see the line Raya had marked with three small shells. It passed above her net, though close enough that a careless foot could catch a drying cord.

"One person," Dala said. "With a small thing in her hands. Not a line of people following her because she was allowed."

"Will you stand here?" I asked.

"I have work. If she comes while I am at my net, I will tell her where to walk. If I am not here, she must wait. You cannot borrow my answer for another day."

A voice from the water called my name. It was Ranu, seated in Ilan's boat, with no cane bundles this time. She held a short roll of fine strips in her lap. Ilan himself stood at the stern, and the pole-handler was beside him, his wrist bare now. A red mark remained above the joint.

They did not come against the bank. Ilan kept the bow in the slower water.

"I was told you had a way," Ranu said.

"We are looking at one."

"I did not hire a boat to watch you look again."

Ilan lifted his hand before I could answer.

"I have brought her to hear what is possible. I have not hired my boat for another journey yet."

Ranu turned toward him.

"You said we would settle the price when we saw the bank."

"That is what I am doing."

The pole-handler touched his wrist.

"And I will not hold it for half a morning while you settle."

"Then we should settle before you come closer," Nali said.

Iya stepped to the edge of the higher path and called her sister's terms across the water. She named the yard, the rack and the small thing carried in one woman's hands. Nali repeated the terms herself, louder. Ranu listened without interrupting.

"I can come down with this," Ranu said, lifting the cane. "I will not carry the rest of the load. I will not set it on her ground."

"Nor will you bring a man to carry it for you," Nali said.

"I heard."

Raya told them what she had found at the stones. She named the second stone, the soft edge, and the point at which she would close the path. Ilan looked at the water rather than at me.

"I will not put my hull against the upper step or tie to the sounding stake," he said. "If the water rises or the boat will not hold, I turn back. No one calls that a debt."

Ranu nodded.

"And the journey?" she asked.

"From Nali's water path to these stones, and back to the same place after you have spoken to the basket maker. No cargo beyond the sample. No waiting after the light reaches that leaning tree."

The pole-handler looked at the tree.

"That is still too long if the current begins to run hard. I want my food before we leave Nali's bank, and I will decide whether the boat can hold when we get here. Yesterday I held it for nothing beyond my first journey."

Ranu pressed her lips together. She had paid for yesterday's wasted morning. I saw her looking at the cane as if its weight had grown.

"What food?" she asked.

"A bowl of cooked rice and fish for this journey. Not a promise to feed me when the maker has finished speaking."

"That is more than I expected."

"Then find someone else to hold the pole."

Ilan said nothing. He owned the boat. He had not offered the man's arms with it.

Ranu asked whether a smaller share of fish would do. The man shook his head. At last she agreed to the bowl and fish, to be given at Nali's bank before the boat set out. Ilan named his own hire, a measured portion of rice from Ranu's household, payable on return. She agreed to that separately. I did not offer her rice from Amaya's stores.

The keeper arrived from the hall with two empty strips and no knife. She had heard the voices from above. She stood beside Nali, looking first at the boat and then at the stones.

"Do you want me to hear this?" she asked.

"I do," Ranu said. "But I want to know whose words you will keep."

"Only the words spoken here. I cannot mark an answer for someone who has not heard them."

She asked each person to repeat what they had accepted. Nali gave the one-person path on her bank. Dala gave the one-person path above her net, while she was present and the stones were exposed. Raya gave a conditional judgment about the stones, and kept the upper step closed. Ilan gave one return journey under his own command. The pole-handler gave his work for the food he had named, retaining the right to turn away from unsafe water. Ranu gave her payment and limited her cargo to the sample.

When the keeper asked me what I had promised, I found that I had promised nothing that made the crossing possible.

"I will stand above Dala's path," I said. "If the boat comes, I will help one person from the stones when Raya says the way is safe. I will not ask another worker to leave her work for it."

Dala looked at me.

"And if someone arrives with a bundle?"

"I will tell him this was not leave for a bundle."

"And if he says you promised both banks?"

"I will send him to the people who own the paths."

Nali bent to pick up her rack slats.

"Do not send him through my yard to find me," she said. "He can ask from the water."

The keeper made one mark for the proposed journey, with the witnesses' names spoken aloud. She kept the strip apart from the older ones. Nothing had crossed. No payment had been made. Ranu and Ilan still had to return to Nali's bank, and the pole-handler still had to decide whether the water would let him work.

I had wanted the keeper to carry the terms back to the hall, where people could see them and speak of the crossing as ours. Nali was already turning toward her yard. The agreement had begun on both shores, but it could fail on either one.

The basket maker's boy came down the higher path with an empty jar on his shoulder.

"My mother is at the potter's yard," he said to me. "She will look at a sample before she goes home. She says you can carry the two full jars you left yesterday if you still mean to help."

I looked at the boat. Ranu had heard him.

"Go," she said. "I have paid enough for men to wait for me. I will not pay for you as well."

I told the boy I would come for the jars. When I reached the potter's yard, I would miss the chance to watch the first boat approach. I could still carry what I had promised. The keeper could stand at the stones without pretending that she owned either bank.

Ranu told Ilan to turn the boat. The pole-handler planted his pole and brought the bow around. They went toward Nali's path to make the payment before beginning the journey we had only spoken about.
