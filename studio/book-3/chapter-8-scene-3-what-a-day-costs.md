# Scene 3 — What a Day Costs

The woman who had brought Iya across the river was packing the last of her fish into a shallow basket.

Iya stood beyond the shade of her awning. She had paid for the return and was waiting without calling the woman away from her customers. A boy sat on the canoe's overturned bailer, picking scales from the wet edge with a piece of reed. He looked up whenever someone approached the boat.

I went to the woman, keeping out of the path of a man carrying a basket of shells.

"Are you taking Iya home?"

"When I finish here." She tied the basket's lid with a strip of fiber. "If you have something to say to her, say it before I push off."

"I want someone from this bank to hear what her sister says."

"Then go."

"I have work I promised."

"So have I."

She turned the basket to test the knot. One fish had slipped beneath the lid. She pushed it back with the flat of her hand.

"I am Ache," I said.

"I know. I am Bina. I heard your name when a man wanted to land a basket and the keeper asked him whose boat it was."

"He left."

"He had no agreement to wait. His brother had a buyer upriver." Bina lifted the basket onto a plank. "They went where the buyer was."

I had thought of the man as someone who had refused our landing. He had been a brother trying to make a sale.

"Would you hear the keeper's account before you go?" I asked.

"Of what?"

"Ranu's crossing yesterday. And the man who says it opened Nali's path."

"I brought Iya here this morning. I have not been hired to settle anyone's quarrel."

"You would only need to listen."

"Only." She pointed to the boy at the canoe. "That is Kito. He poles when the water is too strong for me to manage the basket and the steering together. I pay him whether I sell the fish or not. If I stop to listen, he stops too."

Kito heard his name and stood. He had a red scrape across the back of one heel.

"Would you wait?" I asked him.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Then ask Bina. She tells me when I am working. I tell her if I can do it."

Bina put her hands on her hips.

"You have found two people to ask. Have you found anything to pay them?"

I looked at the baskets. One held fish that had not sold. A third was empty except for a small weight stone. I could carry them, but carrying them now would not replace her morning.

"Could I work for you tomorrow?"

"What work?"

"Carry your baskets from the canoe to the market."

"Who carries them today?"

"Kito?"

"He poles the boat and helps me when it is tied. If you take his carrying, I do not get to keep his wage."

"I did not mean to take it."

"Then tell me what you offer."

I looked toward the hall. The keeper was there, holding the blank strip. I could have asked her for rice from the hall stores. Those stores belonged to people who had left them against claims, and she would have asked which of them had agreed to pay my witness.

"I can carry your baskets to the upper sellers tomorrow," I said. "After Kito is paid for the pole, if there is work left."

Bina looked at my hands.

"Those are hands that carried jars."

"Tesa asked the basket maker whether I had kept a bargain. She learned I had missed a day."

"Then I know what kind of promise you make."

"I will be here."

"That is what a man says before another man comes with a boat."

She did not turn away. I waited.

"I come to this market when the catch and the water allow," she said. "I cannot promise a morning's fish merely to give you a chance to work. If I come tomorrow, I may have baskets for you. If I do not, your promise buys nothing."

"Then what would you accept today?"

"Tell me what you want me to do, from beginning to end. Do not say 'listen' as though the river will do the rest."

I asked Iya to come closer. She approached but did not speak for Bina.

"The keeper will tell you what she heard here," I said. "You can take Iya back, as you have already agreed. If Nali is willing, you can hear her own words. When you next bring fish to this bank, you could tell the keeper what you heard."

"And if Nali will not speak?"

"Tell us that."

"And if I hear that you have marked something falsely?"

"Tell us that too."

Bina untied the basket she had just secured. She counted the remaining fish, touching each with a wet finger.

"If I listen here, I miss the last buyers. If I wait for Nali, Kito waits in the boat. If I return to tell you, I spend time away from the market. You have named no price for any of it."

"I have no rice enough to hire a day."

"Then do not hire a day. Ask me whether I wish to carry a thing I can tell truthfully. The price of that is mine to name."

Iya watched us from the edge of the awning. She had made two crossings for her sister and paid Bina for both. She was not offering the fare a third time.

Bina lifted one unsold fish by the tail.

"This one will not be better tomorrow. I can sell it for less at the drying shed. If you carry this basket there now, the shed woman may take it before the heat spoils it. I will hear the keeper while Kito washes the canoe. That is what I can give today. No promise to return before my next market day."

I looked at Iya. She had already waited through our bargain.

"Will that delay your return?" I asked.

"Some," she said. "I paid Bina to take me home. If she agrees to hear you, I can wait while she hears. I will not ask her to wait half a day for Nali after we arrive."

"Nor will I," Bina said.

I took the basket. Its bottom was damp enough to soak my cloth. The drying shed stood above the market path, farther than I had thought. By the time the woman there had examined the fish and named her price, the sun was on the open stones. She gave me a small piece of smoked fish as a carrier's portion. I had expected nothing, but I took it. Bina's fish had sold for less than it might have earlier.

When I returned, Bina was tying the canoe's painter. Kito had washed the scales from the bailer and set it upright to dry.

"You are late," Bina said.

"The woman argued over the price."

"She always does. Did she take the basket?"

"All of it."

"Then the carrying was worth something. It has paid for the time I lost here, not for a second journey."

"I understand."

"You may eat that fish. It is your wage, not mine."

I put it in my cloth. I had come to buy a witness and had earned a mouthful of food.

The keeper was at the doorway when we reached the hall. She brought out the strip from Ranu's crossing, the blank one, and the older strip from the two-basket dispute. She laid them apart.

Bina stayed standing.

"I can hear until Kito calls," she said. "He knows when the water will turn."

The keeper described the journey from our bank: the failed first approach, Raya's inspection, Dala's limited path, the piece of cane carried by Ranu, the three pieces bought by the basket maker, and the return boat. She said what she had seen. When she came to Nali's permission, she stopped.

"I heard Iya call that her sister would allow this one return," she said. "I did not stand beside Nali when she spoke."

Bina looked at the strip.

"Does that mark tell me where Ranu put her feet?"

"No. It tells me what I wanted to remember of the journey."

"Then why did you put it where a man could point to it as permission for his bundles?"

The keeper's fingers tightened around the strip.

"Because the people who were there should be able to correct it."

"That is an answer for people who were there," Bina said. "The man with the bundles was not."

I wanted to tell Bina the man had misused the mark, not the keeper. I had seen the man who wanted watchers point to the same shelf. He had wanted something he could show another owner. I had refused to give it to him, but I had left the strip where he could describe it as he pleased.

"Would you carry the strip?" I asked.

"No."

"You have not heard the rest."

"I have heard enough to know the cuts will not speak when I am absent. I can carry my account. The keeper may keep hers."

The keeper put the marked strip back on the shelf. She did not reach for the blank one.

"Tell us what you heard, then," she said.

"I heard that Ranu made one crossing and returned. I heard you say the upper step was closed, the old stones were used only with Raya's leave, and Dala allowed one woman above her net. I heard Iya say Nali had allowed that woman on her path for that journey. I did not hear Nali."

"That is accurate," the keeper said.

Bina looked at me.

"And I heard Ache ask me to carry his account. I refused to promise it would be his when I brought it back."

"I asked you to hear both banks," I said.

"You asked for a witness before you knew what I would charge."

I had no answer that improved what I had done.

Kito called from the canoe. The water had begun to draw away from the higher stones. Bina picked up her empty baskets.

"At the other bank," she said to Iya, "I will take you where we agreed. If Nali is working, I will not take her from it for a hearing she has not accepted. You may ask whether she wishes to speak. I will listen if she does. If she does not, I will go home."

"And the report?" I asked.

"When I next come to sell fish, if I have heard anything worth bringing, I will tell the keeper. If you want me to come sooner, you must make a different bargain and pay Kito for his time as well."

Kito was already aboard. He had not heard the keeper's words. Bina called down to him to hold the bow while Iya climbed in. He said he could wait until she sat, and no longer.

Iya turned once on the bank.

"I will tell Nali that Bina has listened here," she said. "I will not tell her she must listen there."

I held the canoe while Iya put her foot against the plank. Bina stepped in after her and took the steering oar. Kito set the pole against the riverbed. The bow moved away before I could ask another question.

The keeper stood beside me with the uncut strip. I had found a woman willing to hear me, a boat already going to the other bank, and no promise of a return message. I had also promised Tesa my labor two mornings from now. If Bina came to market on that same morning, I could not carry both women's bundles.

The fish in my cloth had begun to smell of smoke. I took it out and ate it while the canoe moved toward the far bend.
