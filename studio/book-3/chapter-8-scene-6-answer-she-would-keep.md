# Scene 6 — The Answer She Would Keep

I found Bina two mornings later with her canoe drawn above the wet line. Kito was sitting on a flat stone, picking a thorn from his foot. She had sold the last of her fish and was washing the scales from a basket. When she saw me, she set the basket down.

"I have no answer from Nali today," she said.

"I have not asked for one."

"You are looking at the canoe."

"I wanted to thank you for bringing back her correction."

"You carried my fish. I brought back what I heard because I chose to. We finished that bargain."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

A man stood a little farther up the path with a covered bundle on his shoulder. He had been watching us. He stepped forward before I could answer.

"I am the one who asked Ache to find you," he said. "My brother has two jars to send across. Nali sent away the last man who asked to use her rack path. We want to know whether the upper path can be used."

Bina looked at the bundle.

"Have you already loaded the jars?"

"No. We have learned to ask first."

"Good. Ask Nali."

"We cannot leave the jars and the buyers for a whole day. We were told you carried her words."

"I carried them once. I was already taking Iya home. I have no reason to go across for you today."

"We can pay."

"You can pay for a boat, if its owner agrees. You cannot pay me to say Nali will answer."

"I only want you to ask."

"And if she does not wish to answer me? I will have taken a day from my fish for a question she never asked me to carry."

The man shifted the bundle. Its cover slipped, showing two small woven mats rather than jars. He had brought samples for a buyer, and the jars were still somewhere safe. He had done the thing the pot owner wished he had done. I wanted him to succeed.

"What would make the journey worth your time?" I asked Bina.

She lifted the empty basket and turned it to let the water run out.

"Nothing today. The river is turning. Kito's foot is hurt. I have sold my fish and I am going home by this bank. I will not cross because you have found a man with money."

Kito looked up.

"I could pole," he said, "but I would rather get the thorn out first."

"Then get it out," Bina told him. "You do not owe the man a journey either."

The stranger looked from one of us to the other.

"Who carries the answer, then?"

"No one has agreed to," I said.

He frowned.

"Your keeper has two marks for the crossing. I was told one came from a witness."

"One reminds her what she saw. The other reminds her that Bina reported Nali's correction. Neither is a promise that Bina will travel again."

"Then the marks do not help me."

"They can tell you what not to assume."

"I already know what not to assume. I need to move jars."

Bina returned to her basket. She had given us an answer, and I had nearly made her repeat it until she agreed to something else.

"Will you tell the keeper you refused?" I asked.

"Tell her yourself. You were here when I said it. If you tell her I would not carry anyone's words, you will be wrong. I have carried them. I will not carry these today."

The stranger went up the path with his samples. He had not hired a boat or lost a wage, but he had lost the time he had spent looking for Bina. I watched him go, thinking that our narrow truth had still cost him a morning.

***

At the hall, I told the keeper what had happened. She had the two strips on the shelf, apart from the one for the disputed baskets. She asked whether Bina had refused all future journeys.

"No. She refused this one."

"And the man?"

"He had not loaded the jars. He went to look for another way to ask."

"Then there is nothing to cut for his crossing. There was none."

"I thought we should make it easier for him to know whom to ask."

"Whom can we promise him?"

"Someone willing to hear Nali."

"Name that person."

I had no name. I could tell a stranger to seek Iya, but Iya had paid to cross for her sister and had refused to be my mouth. I could tell him to seek Bina, but Bina had just declined. The keeper watched me look toward the river.

"Can we tell him what was said before?" I asked.

"We can. He must still ask whether the people concerned will say it again. If I mark a journey that never happened, the next man will have one more thing to mistake."

Amaya came to the doorway carrying a narrow bundle. I knew the dark square patch on her old cloth where it showed beneath the cord. She had been to Tesa's house and had brought the old measure with her.

"Tesa sold the length we looked at," she said.

I stood.

"Before the next market?"

"A woman brought wax yesterday. Tesa told us she would sell it if someone paid first. She did."

"I meant to carry her bundles."

"You meant to. You went to hear about pots instead."

"I was going to come when the bundles arrived."

"She did not promise to hold work for you. She promised to tell you what it might count toward if it was there."

The keeper did not move the strips. She had heard me say I would work for cloth, and now she was hearing what that promise had been worth.

"Is there another length?" I asked.

"Not one I have chosen. Tesa has work for a carrier tomorrow if you go before she hires another. She will tell you what she can pay. I will decide whether what you bring is fit for my household."

I had wanted to give Amaya a cloth and close the claim. She had brought me a smaller thing to do.

"Will you go with me?" I asked.

"No. I have shown you the measure. You can carry it to Tesa. I am not going to stand beside you each time you ask for work."

She untied the bundle and laid the old cloth in my hands. Its patched corner was stiff. I could see where the threads had been drawn together by many washings.

"Do not sell this one," she said. "It is mine."

"I would not."

"You have been offering things that are not yours to offer. I want you to hear yourself before you do it again."

She left me with the cloth. I held it until the keeper asked whether I wanted a place to put it. I shook my head and tied it under my arm.

A young woman came into the hall with a reed basket of dried leaves. She wanted to know whether the crossing strip meant that someone had accepted responsibility for Nali's rack. The keeper told her it did not. The young woman said her cousin had heard that Ache would pay for repairs because the crossing had damaged the rack.

"The rack was broken before Ranu crossed," I said. "Nali does not know who broke it."

"Then will you repair it anyway?"

"I have not asked Nali whether she wants my work."

The young woman looked at the cloth beneath my arm.

"People say you have work enough for every shore."

"I have work I have not paid for," I said.

She nodded as though this was the answer she had expected. She took her basket and went out. I wondered which part she would repeat.

***

The keeper called Iya when she saw her at the market that afternoon. Iya had come on her own business, bringing two baskets of dried fish to exchange for rice. She had not crossed for our hearing. She sat only after she had set the baskets where she could watch them.

"I heard Bina refused a journey," Iya said.

"She refused a man who wanted her to ask Nali about jars," I answered.

"That is her choice."

"The man had not hired a boat. He wanted to know whom he could ask."

"Then tell him he may come to Nali without a load and ask her himself, if he can arrange a journey. She may hear him. She may not. I cannot promise for her."

"Could you take his question next time you go home?"

"I could, if I choose. I do not know the man or his jars. I will not promise to make my sister listen."

"Then what can we agree to remember?"

Iya looked at the two strips on the shelf.

"Remember that you heard me say Nali would hear a person who came to ask. Remember also that she has not promised an answer. When someone asks about the crossing, say who saw the journey and who only heard about it."

The keeper took the first strip down.

"I saw Ranu leave this bank and return. I heard you call across that Nali allowed that journey. I did not see which path Ranu took beyond the roots."

She set the strip down and took the second.

"Bina told me she heard Nali name the higher path and refuse the rack path. I did not hear Nali say it. Bina did. I will not tell a stranger I heard it myself."

Iya touched neither strip.

"And if I come with a different account?" she asked.

"I will hear it," the keeper said. "I will ask who was there. I will not cut away Bina's words because you are Nali's sister, nor keep them because Ache likes them."

"Do you like them?" Iya asked me.

"They cost a man a boat's morning. They cost another man his way to sell pots. I wanted the first account to be enough."

"And now?"

"Now I know what Bina heard. I will tell it when I am asked, including the part that sends people away."

Iya picked up her baskets.

"Then I have heard you say that. I can tell Nali if she asks what happened here. I am not agreeing to come again."

"Nor am I asking you to."

She lifted one basket onto her hip and left the other at her feet while she adjusted the strap. I went to help, but she shook her head. A boy she had paid was waiting at the doorway. He took the second basket when she called him, and they went toward the market together.

The keeper put the strips back with space between them. She had not made a new mark. I had not appointed a witness. We had heard one another, and none of us had agreed to more than that.

***

At the landing, the cord owner was speaking to two men I had not seen before. One wore a short cloth stained with the red earth of the upper market. The other carried a measuring cord and a small clay weight. They had come to ask about moving jars and pots when the water rose.

I stopped near enough to hear. The owner saw me and did not lower his voice.

"This bank has people who will tell you why a boat cannot land," he said. "If you want a place ready when you arrive, find the people who will promise the work before you hire your carriers."

The man with the clay weight looked at Raya's closed step.

"Can she mend it?"

"Ask her. She knows the wood. She will not take a promise from Ache for timber that belongs to another house."

"And the watchers?"

"I can hire men for my own boat. Ache has no charge for every boat, and neither do I. If you want someone to wait for your cargo, you must pay that person yourself."

He was telling them what he had learned. He was also telling them why he would no longer risk his name on our landing. I had wanted a witness whose words would travel. Here was a man carrying words I could not stop.

"That is accurate," I said.

The owner turned.

"Which part?"

"All of it. And the woman who carried the correction from Nali did so once. She is not bound to carry the next one."

The man with the red-stained cloth looked between us.

"Then who stands for this landing?"

"Raya judges the step," I said. "Dala answers for her path. The keeper remembers what people said. I can speak for what I have heard and for work I agree to do. No one here can promise the others."

He weighed that answer, then turned to the cord owner.

"You were right. We should speak to the other landing before we bring the jars down."

They went together toward the market. The owner did not ask me to follow. He had lost one pot owner's trust, and he meant to keep the next owner's by taking him somewhere else. I could hear him naming the other channel and the men who worked there.

Raya was still beside her timber. She had turned it over and was marking the rotten edge with a point of charcoal. The tide was coming in. Soon the old stones would disappear.

I went to her.

"Is there work I can do before the water reaches you?" I asked.

"Hold this end while I mark it," she said.

I put my hand where she showed me. The wood was heavier than it looked. She moved the charcoal along the dark line and stopped when she reached sound grain.

"Do not lift it yet," she said.

I kept my hand still. Behind us the cord owner was leading the two men away, and I could not tell whether they would ever return. I had no mark to keep them. Raya finished her line, and only then did she tell me to let go.
