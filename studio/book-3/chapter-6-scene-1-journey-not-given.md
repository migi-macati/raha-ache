# Book III, Chapter 6 — Scene 1: The Journey That Wasn't Given

**Packet:** RA-B3C6-SCENE-01. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Directly follows the final disputed strip in published Book III Chapter 5 *Two Short Cuts*. One long cut = one paid journey; two short cuts = two baskets. Boat owner absent, crewman heard a different instruction. Upper step needs Raya's inspection after high water, old upper post is the only permitted tie, sounding stake is not for mooring; Dala's path remains hers. No new charge, court, fleet, or settled debt. **Historical status:** Specific people, tally and procedures are fiction.

---

The first thing the river brought back was a broken reed mat.

It came sideways along the bank, caught on the sounding stake, and spread open under the pull of the water. Two boys went after it from the higher path. One took off his shirt and wound it around his hand before reaching down. The other told him to leave it. Neither listened to me when I called. The mat tore at its middle, and the loose half passed between their feet and went downstream.

The tide had not yet begun to fall.

Behind them the small boat was coming toward the upper step. I knew its crewman by the way he stood at the bow with one knee against the gunwale. He had brought his paddle back. The owner was not with him.

There was another man at the stern, older, his back bent over the steering oar. Between them stood three covered jars and two baskets with their mouths sewn shut. The jars had been set on wet leaves so they would not knock together. A woman on the bank called out when she saw the baskets.

"Keep those dry. The buyer will turn away if the covers split."

The crewman lifted his hand to show he had heard. Then he pointed at the step.

I stepped down to the old landing and stood where he could see me.

"Stay off the new wood."

He put his paddle across the bow.

"We have come back."

"I see that."

"He said to bring the other load before the water turned."

"Where is he?"

"Upstream. He took the first payment to the man who owns the jars."

The older steersman eased the oar. The boat slid sideways until its bow faced the current again. A line had been coiled on the forward plank. The crewman picked it up.

"Not the sounding stake," I said.

"I know what it is."

He had spoken sharply, but he lowered the line.

The woman came down from the path. She was carrying a folded cover under her arm and had a smear of dark oil along her wrist. I had seen her once at the hall buying cord. She looked from me to the boat without greeting either of us.

"Why have you stopped them?"

"The step has not been inspected."

"It held yesterday."

"It held a smaller load yesterday, before the water came over it again."

"These are the same jars."

"They are not the same jars," the crewman said. "The ones yesterday went on the first trip."

The woman made a small impatient sound. "I mean the same kind. Three jars, not a house."

The boat rose against a wave left by a passing canoe. The jars shifted together. The older man put his bare foot against one and held it there until the hull settled.

I could see the new step through the brown water. Its pale upper edge was gone. The old post still stood above it, dark and swollen at the foot. The soft bank beneath the repair had been cut away in a narrow curl. I could not tell whether the piece Raya had braced was moving or whether the water only made it seem so.

"Raya said she would look after the next high water," I told the woman.

"Then call her."

"She is at her house."

"Do you know where it is?"

"Yes."

"Then why are you standing here?"

The crewman laughed once, without pleasure.

"Because he will tell us not to tie until the owner comes," he said. "Yesterday they told me to fetch him. Today he is on the river, and we have brought what he sent us to bring."

"Did your owner tell you that these jars had been paid for?" I asked.

"He told me we had two journeys."

"That is what you told us yesterday."

"Then you heard me."

He leaned forward. The basket nearest his knee moved with him. I saw that its cover was sewn with two different colors of fiber, one old and one new. Someone had spent the morning closing it. The woman followed my glance.

"Those are wax cakes. If the sun reaches them before they are in the shade, they will soften against the weave. I have a man waiting at the market path. He has to leave before the noon meal."

"Who agreed to carry them?" I asked.

She looked at the crewman.

"The boat's owner."

"At what price?"

"That is between us."

"Was it paid?"

"No. He was to take his share after delivery."

The crewman shook his head.

"He said this was the second journey of the payment already made."

The woman stared at him.

"Made by whom?"

"By the woman yesterday. Two baskets."

"Those were her baskets. These are mine."

He opened his mouth, then closed it. The older man at the stern looked over his shoulder toward the river bend, where their owner had gone.

I remembered the bamboo on the keeper's table. One long cut. Two short ones. I had watched the owner put his thumb on each of the shorter marks. I had thought that was enough.

"Bring the boat outside the old post," I said. "Do not put the line on it yet."

The older man turned the oar and held the boat in the slower water. It cost him effort. His shoulders moved beneath the thin cloth he wore, and his toes searched for purchase on the wet floor.

"How long?" he asked.

"I will fetch Raya."

"How long will that take?"

"I don't know."

He looked at the crewman, who looked back at me.

"If I take this boat home with the goods," the young man said, "he will say I refused a journey. If I stay here until the buyer goes, he will say I lost his payment. Which one will you tell him happened?"

"I will tell him what I saw."

"You did that yesterday."

I had no answer that would make the work easier for him.

The woman pushed past me to the end of the old landing. Her foot struck a loose stone. It went into the water with a soft, heavy sound.

"Can they unload at the upper path?" she asked.

"That path belongs to the households beside it. Dala uses the ground below for her nets. We have no leave to make it a landing."

"Then ask her."

"She is working."

"Everyone is working. So am I."

A man carrying a basket on his shoulder stopped behind us. He had been heading toward the hall. The woman called to him and asked if he would carry the jars from the boat if they could be brought ashore. He named a price. She named another. They stood apart for a moment, measuring each other. The man shifted his basket and said he could not wait while we argued over where his feet would go.

He left.

The woman watched him climb the path. She had not called him back before he disappeared.

The older steersman moved the boat out of the current again. His heel slid. The crewman caught the oar handle before it struck the jars.

"You cannot keep us holding here," the older man said.

"I know."

"Then let us use the post."

"The post is for a boat that has permission to land. If you tie there, the next one cannot come in."

"No one else is here."

"Not yet."

He looked at the empty water, then at the bank. He was old enough to know how little an empty river promised.

The crewman pulled the line through his hands, paying out one arm's length and gathering it back. He was not trying to defy me. He was making sure it had not caught beneath the baskets.

"I was told to come twice," he said more quietly. "I came the first time. I have come the second. Whatever the marks say, that is the work I was given."

"Who pays you for waiting?"

"The owner."

"Did he say so?"

"He pays me for taking the boat where he sends it."

"That is not the same thing."

"Then perhaps you can explain the difference to him when he comes."

The woman lowered her cover onto the dry part of the path. She opened it, folded it again, and made a shade with it over the nearest basket. Her fingers were quick, but she had to reach from the old landing, and the bow kept drawing away from her. At last she took the cover back.

"Where is the keeper?" she asked.

"In the hall."

"Bring the strip."

"The owner has not come to say what he heard."

"I want to see what was cut."

"The keeper will show it. She will not let it be changed."

"I didn't ask her to change it."

She walked up the path. The crewman watched her go.

"She thinks we made the marks," he said.

"I know who made them."

"Then tell her."

"I will."

He turned his face toward the stern, where the older man had begun to breathe through his mouth. The current was running harder now. The older man's feet were braced against opposite sides of the boat. His left hand trembled as he held the oar.

I went down to the water's edge and took hold of the line the crewman threw me. I could not tie it to the new step or the sounding stake. I stood with it around my forearm and let the boat come closer until the older man could rest his shoulders.

The pull was stronger than I expected. The rope burned where it moved over my skin. Mud slipped beneath my heel. I set my other foot against the old landing and felt the boat's weight pass through my arms.

"Do not let it pull you in," the crewman said.

"Then keep the bow into the water."

"We are trying."

For a little while no one spoke. The old post was close enough that I could have thrown a turn around it. I did not. The boat was waiting, and I had told them to wait, so I held the line myself.

A girl from the woman's household came running with a message. She stopped at the top of the path when she saw me standing in the mud.

"The buyer has left the shade," she said. "He is going to the far bank."

"Tell her," I said.

"She knows. She sent me to find out if the jars have come."

"They haven't."

The girl looked at the boat. It was almost touching the old landing. One basket was no farther from her than a grown person's reach. She could have stepped down and touched it, but she did not.

"Will they come before he crosses?" she asked.

"I cannot promise that."

She turned and ran.

The crewman saw her go. He had not heard every word, but he knew what it meant.

"Now she will say we were late."

"She may."

"We were here."

"I saw you."

"Then say that when he comes."

"I will."

He rubbed his thumb against the rope where it passed through his hands. His skin was already raw. The older man looked at me and nodded toward the bank.

"You have held us long enough to learn the weight," he said. "Let us go to the bend where the water is quiet. We can wait there until you have someone who may answer."

The crewman did not want to move. I could see it in the way he kept his knees fixed against the planks. But the older man had already turned the oar.

I let the line run out slowly and gave the last of it back. The boat swung away. The jars knocked once and were still.

"I will fetch Raya," I called.

"Fetch our owner too," the crewman said.

"I cannot find him if I don't know where he has gone."

"That is what I have been telling you."

The boat moved beyond the stake. The torn reed mat was gone. Nothing in the water marked where it had caught.

When I reached the hall, the keeper had put the disputed strip on a dry board. The woman was standing across from her with both hands pressed against the table. She had already explained the jars and the buyer.

"Did you let the boat in?" the keeper asked.

"No."

"Good."

"I held its line while they waited."

She looked at the wet mark around my forearm.

"Then you know what the crewman was doing."

"Yes."

The woman lifted her hands from the table.

"My buyer is crossing to the other bank. Your marks have cost me a sale."

The keeper touched the long cut with one finger.

"These marks were for yesterday's journey."

"I know that now," the woman said. "The man on the boat does not."

She turned to me.

"Will you tell the owner that his crew came when he ordered them? Will you tell him I brought the goods and did not refuse to pay?"

"Yes."

"And will you tell my buyer to wait?"

I looked at the strip, then at the light coming through the doorway. The buyer was already on his way. I had spent the morning holding a rope while the people who needed an answer were somewhere else.

"I can go after him," I said. "I cannot make him stay."

"Then go," she said.

The keeper kept the strip on the table. I went out with the wet rope mark still rising on my arm.
