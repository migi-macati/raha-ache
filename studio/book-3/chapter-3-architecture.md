# Book III, Chapter 3: A marker, a claim, and the other shore

**Packet:** RA-B3C3-ARCH-01. **Status:** Studio architecture; not published prose or historical testimony. **Working reader title:** *The Two Crowns of the Bay* (review after full manuscript). **POV:** Ache, first person. **Read against:** published Book III Chapters 1–2 and merged Chapter 3 continuity outline PR #75. **Architecture:** Book III Chapters 1–10 plus historical bonus Chapters 11–12; Chapter 3 settles a bounded Tondo–Maynila passage dispute, Chapter 4 owns building an order that must survive ordinary work.

## Causal opening and boundaries

Chapter 2 ends with Ache authorized to hear complaints for Maynila's principal landing and working hall, under the keeper's collection and Amaya's continuing stewardship. The two passages were measured and tried; the far-bank boat-owning house accepted them only provisionally and retained independent rights. Dala's repaired rack and Pawi's nets still carry losses. A blocked household path has not been certified repaired. Harun departed on his own ship. Ache has no army, inherited control of Tondo, or proved relationship with Lakandula.

**Dramatic question:** When the channel marker shifts, who may move it back, and who is entitled to judge the resulting loss? Ache must make a ruling that his own supporters dislike and learn that the far-bank representative cannot bind all of Tondo. Lakandula's involvement must be earned through a real representative and a credible grievance, without asserting an exact undocumented office, age, genealogical tie or historic encounter.

## Six-scene architecture

1. **The Marker That Walked.** A temporary channel stake has been shifted between tides. A boat misses its intended landing; a Maynila household loses a delivery and a far-bank crew is blamed. Ache is asked to decide before the keeper has the previous soundings. Sulad can identify the old measured line but refuses to certify the cause. Ache preserves the disputed stake rather than declaring theft. Irreversible consequence: both sides demand separate witnesses.
2. **The Man Who Was Not Sent.** The far-bank representative returns and rejects Ache's assumption that one household's earlier consent binds every Tondo crossing. A second delegation claims authority to negotiate and disputes the first. Banu and Isan distinguish what each person actually said from what Ache wanted to hear. Irreversible consequence: Ache agrees to delay a levy while each delegation produces accountable principals.
3. **A Crossing With No Passenger.** Ache visits a neutral meeting place without Harun's vessel or armed escort. Lakandula is introduced through specific claims of the people he answers to, not a ready-made ruler-versus-ruler tableau. Amaya refuses to pledge the hall's stores as security for Ache's bargain. Irreversible consequence: the parties agree to a limited test of who can order a marker moved.
4. **The Cost of the Wrong Shore.** The trial reveals that Maynila's own workers moved the marker to protect a loaded hull, but failed to notify the far bank. Ache's trusted supporter urges him to call it an emergency exception. Dala and the affected crew identify the real losses. Irreversible consequence: Ache publicly accepts a finding against his own landing.
5. **The Right to Refuse.** Negotiations nearly fail over who may appeal a toll, who pays for damaged cargo, and which households can refuse an unapproved levy. Lakandula insists that Tondo's boat owners are not his property to give away; Ache must say the same of Maynila's workers. Irreversible consequence: named witnesses agree to a procedure that can overrule either side.
6. **The Line That Holds.** A limited passage-and-appeal settlement is tested on a real crossing. Neither shore surrenders jurisdiction. The original stake is preserved as evidence, and a new marker is set only with witnesses from both banks. A dissatisfied Maynila supporter remains free to oppose Ache. Chapter 4 inherits fragile, workable authority rather than an accomplished conquest.

## Editorial gates

The opening must occur after the Chapter 2 hearing without silently undoing the agreed two passages. Do not reopen the entire restoration dispute or repeat Chapter 2's full hearing structure. Each scene must change a material constraint, relationship or decision. Maintain physical tides, draft, pole placement and working time without claiming an exact historical engineering practice. Distinguish a witness's knowledge from inference and accusation. Ache may be wrong, and must not resolve every dispute with a speech. Avoid presenting Dery's genealogical hypothesis or the uncollated Aganduru CODOIN 78 pp. 59–60 as established events. Preserve the absence of an army, royal marriage or guaranteed captain. Check title after prose: *Two Crowns* risks implying literal crowns or coequal formal kingships; a title rooted in the disputed marker may better fit the finished chapter.
