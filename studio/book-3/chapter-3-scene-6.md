# Book III, Chapter 3 — Scene 6: The Line That Holds

**Packet:** RA-B3C3-SCENE-06. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 5's limited witnessed sounding, complaint procedure and explicit refusal of a new general toll. The original displaced stake remains available as evidence. The Maynila owner who supplied Dala's cord has not withdrawn his objection. **Historical status:** Fictional passage trial and local settlement, not an attested Tondo–Maynila treaty or personal meeting with Lakandula.

---

The old stake came out of the mud with a sound like a foot pulled from a deep shoe.

The landing worker had waited until the witnesses arrived. He had stood beside it with both hands empty while the keeper called the names of the people who had seen it leaning. The steerswoman from the far bank was there. So were the first representative, the man who had brought his aunt's old marker, and two workers from Maynila.

The man who had moved it before did not touch it.

He stood on the bank and watched the younger worker loosen the mud around the wood. When the stake came free, the keeper asked him to show the place where he had tied the loaded vessel's line. He pointed to a worn band below the top. The fibers had left a pale mark in the dark wood.

"Is that where the line was?" the steerswoman asked.

"Yes."

"Did you see the line take the wood out?"

"I saw it lean. We pulled it free to keep the boat from dragging it under."

The keeper repeated his words so the people on both banks could hear. She asked the younger worker what he had seen. He said he had helped put the wood back and had not checked the old measure.

No one said the wood could tell us who first made the mistake. The workers had told us that themselves. The keeper wrapped the stake in an old cloth and laid it above the tide, beside the strips holding the soundings and the basket count.

The new stake lay on two blocks of timber. Its point had been shaped the day before. The older worker had made it, then left it where the keeper could see it until the witnesses came.

The far-bank steerswoman examined the point. She ran her hand along the grain and asked whether the wood would split when driven into the hard bottom. The older worker said it might. He had brought a second piece in case it did.

"Who pays for the second?" she asked.

"I cut both from the wood the landing gave me."

"Then the landing has paid already."

The keeper made him say which pile it had come from. It was timber set aside for repairing the lower steps, not from Amaya's hall. The owner who had supplied the cord had agreed to let one length be used. He had not agreed to pay for another after that.

We went into the shallows while the water was low enough to show the edge of the turn. The two steersmen lowered their poles in the same places. The keeper held the cord. The far-bank woman watched the cuts and asked them to repeat one depth where the bottom fell away sharply.

The first representative wanted the marker farther toward his bank, to leave room for a loaded boat to turn without scraping the Maynila posts. The steerswoman objected that the place he chose would send a narrow canoe across the deeper line used by ferries.

"Then put it between," he said.

"Between what?"

He showed her with his pole. She tried the bottom and found a hollow he had not noticed.

"That is where a boat will strike when the water drops," she said.

He tested it himself. He did not argue again.

I had come into the water ready to say where the stake should stand. I had seen the sounding once before. I could point to the old mark and the place where the fish boat grounded. When the steerswoman moved her pole a hand's breadth, the depth changed enough to make my answer useless.

The keeper asked whether the two witnesses could agree on a place. They chose a point beyond the hollow, where the bottom rose slowly enough for a loaded boat to turn. The keeper called it aloud. Each repeated it. The older worker brought the new stake and waited.

"Drive it," the first representative said.

The steerswoman lifted her hand.

"Who will tell the next man he may not pull it out to hold his line?"

The older worker looked at his palms.

"I will," he said.

"You may not be here."

The keeper said a mark was not a mooring post, and that the landing would keep a separate post for vessels needing to hold against the current. The far-bank witnesses could not order Maynila to build it. The Maynila owners had to decide where it would stand and how to pay for it.

"Then leave room for the post," the steerswoman said. "Do not make a rule today that gives you no place to tie a boat tomorrow."

The keeper measured the space from the steps to the proposed stake. She asked the older worker whether a mooring post could be driven closer to the bank without narrowing the passage. He said it could, but the bank there was soft. It would need bracing before a loaded vessel trusted it.

"That is work for another tide," she said.

We had no bracing ready. The first representative agreed that boats could use the upper post until the landing repaired the lower one. He would warn his house not to tie to the sounding stake. The steerswoman said she would carry the same warning to hers, but could not speak for every vessel that came downriver.

The older worker drove the new stake.

It went in straight at first, then twisted as the point met something hard beneath the mud. He stopped, pulled it a little, and tried again. The keeper watched the cord. The steerswoman tested the depth on both sides. When they were satisfied, the worker pressed the wood down until it stood firm.

No one cheered. The first representative wiped his hands on his cloth. The steerswoman went back to her boat to fetch the load they had agreed to use for the trial.

The load belonged to her household: four jars of oil and a basket of cloth. She had brought it from the far bank and meant to take it to a buyer above Maynila. The boat was neither empty nor as heavy as the cargo vessel that had pulled the first stake loose. She said that was enough for a first trial and no more.

The keeper asked who would steer. The steerswoman said she would. The first representative asked to stand at the bow with a pole so he could call the depth. She agreed on the condition that he did not touch the new marker.

The canoe left the upper bank and entered the turn.

At first it moved easily. The current took its stern toward the Maynila steps, and the steerswoman turned the paddle to bring the bow back toward the deeper line. The man at the bow called a depth. She answered without looking at him.

Then a boat came out of the inner passage.

It carried two women and a child, with a basket of leaves beneath the forward seat. The women had not been told there would be a trial. They were crossing before the sun reached the roof of the hall.

The man at the bow of the loaded canoe raised his pole.

"Hold there!"

The woman steering the small boat shook her head.

"Where?"

He pointed to the outer shallows. She looked at the stake and saw the mud beyond it.

"That is not deep water."

He lowered his pole again and found the bottom.

The steerswoman in the loaded canoe had already turned her bow toward the upper bank. She called to the small boat to keep the inner line. The two vessels passed close enough for the child to reach toward the oil jars. His mother pulled his hand back.

The loaded canoe swung once in the wake. The steerswoman corrected it with a long stroke. The bow passed the new stake with room to spare. The stern came close enough that the first representative reached for his pole, then stopped when she told him to leave it.

They cleared the turn.

The keeper made no mark until the canoe had reached the upper landing. Then she asked both steersmen whether the passage had been deep enough. The first representative said yes at that tide, but he wanted the turn sounded again after a heavy rain. The steerswoman said a wider boat would need more room.

"Then say that," the keeper told them.

They did. She recorded what the trial had shown and what it had not.

The woman from the small boat came ashore with her child. She asked whether she would have to wait for the next trial before using the passage again.

"No," the keeper said. "The inner way is open."

"And who will stand here to tell us when it is not?"

The keeper looked at the owners who had agreed to send warning men. One had already gone to unload cargo. The other was helping the steerswoman carry her jars.

"For now, the people working the steps will call the safe line," she said. "We still have to agree who keeps watch when they are away."

The woman nodded and went on with her child. She had asked a question the witnesses could not answer with a stake.

The owner who had brought Dala's cord arrived after the trial. He had not come to see it. He had come to ask when his worker would return.

"The passage is marked," I told him.

"Then he can work."

"The keeper needs him to help set a mooring post."

"I have already given wood for the stake."

"Yes."

"And now you want more."

"I want the landing to decide how boats will be tied without moving it again."

He looked at the new marker, then at the old one wrapped above the steps.

"How long will you keep that broken piece of wood?"

"Until the people who lost work have heard what is offered."

"They have heard it."

"Dala has taken the cord. The carrier has agreed to one day of replacement work. The buyer is waiting for fish. Those are not the same thing as every loss being paid."

The owner shook his head.

"You will keep men coming to this hall until they learn that every broken thing has a claim."

"Then bring yours when something of yours is broken."

He did not smile.

"I will bring it to the owners who pay for the landing," he said. "I do not know why I should bring it to you."

He walked away before I could answer. He was free to take his boats elsewhere. He was also free to tell other owners that I had made the work harder. The passage we had opened could lose him without closing at once. I did not know how long it could lose men like him.

Dala came down with Pawi carrying the repaired net frame. The new cord was darker than the old, and the knots sat close together where he had joined them. She set the frame beside the steps, but did not put it in the water.

"Will you use it today?" I asked.

"When the tide is right."

"Is the repair sound?"

"We will find out."

Pawi looked at the new stake.

"That one is farther out."

"It is where the witnesses measured it."

"Can I fish beside it?"

"Ask your mother."

Dala lifted the frame.

"He can fish where the water and the other boats leave room," she said. "No one here has promised him a place forever."

She went down to the bank and set the frame where she could hold it. Pawi followed, watching the cord as the current took it.

The keeper brought the marked strips to the hall. She had recorded the new stake, the witnessed sounding, the trial at that tide, the absence of a new passing fee, and the people who would hear a complaint if the marker moved again. The first representative had marked the account for his house. The far-bank steerswoman had marked what she saw. The man who carried words from the household associated with Lakandula had not returned. His people had made no promise.

Amaya read the names aloud to the workers gathered near the door. She kept the old stake in the corner beside the broken red post from my father's hall. They were different pieces of wood. One belonged to a dispute about a passage. The other belonged to a house that had changed while I was gone.

I sat on the low bench. A porter came in to ask whether the new mooring post would be ready before the next cargo boat. I told him the owners had not yet agreed on the bracing. He said he would tell the steersman to use the upper post until they had.

Then he asked who would pay him for carrying the message.

I looked toward the keeper. She was putting away her cord. The work of keeping a passage open had begun again before the mud dried on our feet.

"Ask the owner who sent you," I said.

"He told me to ask you."

I had no coin ready. I told him I would go with him and speak to the owner myself. He waited while I put on my sandals.

Outside, the tide was rising around the new stake. The old one lay dry above the steps, where anyone could ask why it had been taken out. Boats passed on either side of the turn. Some used the marked water. Others chose their own way.

I went down to meet the man who had sent the porter. Across the river, the houses kept their boats and their claims. They had given me no crown. I had given them no shore. The wood stood between us until the next tide, and the people who had put it there knew where to find one another.
