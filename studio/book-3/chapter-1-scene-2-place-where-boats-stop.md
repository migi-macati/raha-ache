# Book III, Chapter 1 — Scene 2: The Place Where the Boats Stop

**Packet:** RA-B3C1-SCENE-02. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 1 at Harun's outer anchorage. No trading right, landing permission, local allegiance or historical Tondo-ruler identity has yet been established. Harun retains ownership and command; Isan interprets within his knowledge; Sulad is not Ache's sworn follower. **Historical boundary:** The harbor procedure and interlocutors are fictionalized plausible negotiations, not documented law for a named 1520s port.

---

The larger boat did not come close enough to touch ours. It held against the current while a man in the bow measured the distance between the hulls with his eyes. The pole in his hands had a flat end worn smooth from pushing against mud. He lowered it into the water once, felt the bottom, and said something to the people beneath the shade.

A woman came forward. She wore a dark cloth folded at the shoulder and carried a small wooden box beneath one arm. The man with the pole shifted aside without being told.

"Who has come to sell?" she asked.

Harun stood where she could see him. "I have."

"From where?"

"Burné. We have cloth and resin. We are willing to trade at a place where the goods can be weighed and kept dry."

She looked at the sail, the lashings, the jars and the men who had come to the rail. Her eyes rested on me longer than on the others.

"How many will go ashore?"

"We have not agreed to go ashore," Harun said.

The woman opened her box. Inside lay several narrow sticks with cuts along their sides, a cord, and a small lump of dark wax. She lifted one of the sticks and turned it so that the cuts caught the light.

"Then you can remain here," she said. "But you cannot send your goods into the river without someone answerable for them."

"Answerable to whom?"

"To the people whose landing you use. And to the men who will have to pull your boat free if you ground it."

Harun smiled at that. He had spent the morning sounding the bottom. He knew what it cost to free a loaded hull.

"How much do they take?" he asked.

"That depends on what you bring, where you put it, and how long it stays."

"Then tell me what they take for a man who wishes only to ask questions."

She glanced at me. "Does the man have anything to sell?"

I had the letter. I had a little coin, less than I had intended after buying the replacement water jar in Burné. I had a knife, two changes of cloth and a small bundle of gifts that had once seemed suitable for a homecoming. I could have named them all. None would have answered her.

"He has a name," Harun said.

"Names do not keep goods from being stolen."

She spoke without raising her voice. The men behind her had not moved. One of them was looking at the rope between our anchor and the bow, judging whether the tide might bring us closer.

I stepped forward.

"My name is Ache. My mother had a house upriver. I left this shore as a boy."

The woman looked toward the trees. "Many boys leave."

"I have returned to speak with my kin."

"Then your kin can receive you. Have they sent a boat?"

"No."

"Have they been told you are coming?"

"No."

She shut the box. "Then you are a stranger asking us to guess who will answer for him."

I felt Sulad shift beside me. He had listened without offering a word. I wanted him to speak, to name the house and the river and the people who had once known us. Instead he asked the woman where the men who worked the lower nets sold their catch.

She looked at him with new attention.

"At the lower landing when the tide is high. Why?"

"I worked there before I went south."

"With whom?"

He named a man I remembered as a youth. I had not thought of him in years. The woman shook her head.

"He has not worked those nets for a long time."

"Is he dead?"

"He lives beyond the second bend. His sons work the lower landing."

Sulad's mouth tightened. He asked how many sons. She named two. He knew the older one's name. The woman looked at him again, this time as if she were trying to place a sound heard in another room.

"You can ask them," she said. "If they wish to answer."

I had spoken of my mother and received nothing. Sulad had asked about a net worker and learned where to look. I told myself this was because he had chosen an easier question.

Harun called for the bundle of cloth nearest the hatch. The steersman brought up a folded length and set it on a clean mat. Harun did not unfold it.

"This is a sample," he told the woman. "You may look at it here. If there is a lawful place to sell, I will take the rest there under terms we agree upon."

She asked him whether the cloth had already been promised to a buyer. He said no. She asked whether anyone aboard had a claim against it. He said that the crew's wages and the owner's obligations had been settled for the passage, but the sale would still have to meet his terms. She listened as if she had heard merchants say these things before and sometimes found them untrue.

"Who will keep it while you go upriver?" she asked.

"I will," Harun said. "I have not agreed to go upriver."

She smiled for the first time. "Then we understand each other."

The man with the pole brought their boat a little closer. The woman leaned across the gap, lifted a corner of the sample cloth, and felt its weave between finger and thumb. She looked for damp along the fold. Harun let her inspect it but did not pass the bundle over.

She named a place on the bank where visiting merchants could wait for a buyer. The shelter had a roof and a raised floor. Goods stored there would be recorded against a custodian's marks. A charge was due for each night. Another share would be owed if a sale took place through the landing.

I knew where she meant. There had been a shelter there when I was young, but its roof had been thatched with palm leaves and its floor was low enough for boys to crawl beneath. The new one rose above a line of posts. Two boats were already moored beside it.

"Who holds that landing?" I asked.

"The people who work it."

"Who speaks for them?"

"I do, for goods brought to this water. Someone else will hear a claim to a house."

I was about to ask whose order gave her that right. I could hear how the question would sound: a man who had not come home for years demanding the name of the person who allowed other people to work.

Harun asked about the charge. She gave a number. He countered. They settled on a fee for one night, payable only if his cargo was actually put ashore, and a smaller sum for the use of a landing boat. She would send someone to show him the channel after the tide turned. He would decide then whether to move the goods.

I had been standing beside him, thinking of the first words I would say to the people of Maynila. He had secured the possibility of a roof.

"May I go ashore before the cargo?" I asked.

The woman turned to me. "With what purpose?"

"To find the people who knew my mother."

"Will you carry goods?"

"No."

"Arms?"

I had a knife. I touched its handle before answering. "Only this."

"Leave it aboard."

I looked at Harun. He gave no sign that he would speak for me.

"How many people?" she asked.

"Three. Myself, Sulad and Isan."

Sulad turned his head. "You have not asked me."

The woman waited. Her two companions looked at the river. Neither seemed surprised.

I felt heat rise in my face.

"Will you come?" I asked him.

"To the lower landing, perhaps. I have someone to find. I will not tell strangers I speak for your family."

"I did not ask you to."

"You said three."

I had said it because three sounded safer than one. I had counted Sulad among my people as easily as I counted my own hands.

"Then I ask you to come as yourself," I said.

He looked at the shore. "I will come to the lower landing. After that I may go another way."

Isan was leaning against the mast. I turned to him.

"And you?"

"To help you ask for a place to sleep," he said. "If I can understand the people who answer. If they speak among themselves and do not wish me to hear, I cannot make them."

The woman watched this exchange with an expression I could not read.

"Three people," she said, "with separate reasons for coming. That is easier to understand."

She told us that a small boat would take us to the lower landing at the next safe turn of the tide. We would pay its paddlers. We would not enter any storehouse or dwelling without an invitation. If we wanted a hearing about a household, we would find the person responsible for that household and ask.

"Who is responsible for my mother's house?" I asked.

She looked at me a long moment. "You have not told me where it is."

I described the bend, the old red post, the path behind the house and the trees that had once shaded the yard. I gave the names of two neighboring households. She knew one name. The other had moved, or its people had changed what they called themselves. I could not tell from her answer.

"There is a house there," she said. "It is not the house you describe."

"Who lives in it?"

"I will not give you a household's names from the deck of a foreign boat."

I wanted to say I was not foreign. I had been born on this river. I knew where the fish gathered when the tide fell and how the mud smelled before rain. I could still picture the shadows of the roof poles on my mother's floor.

But the boat beneath my feet had come from Burné, and I had no one on shore who had agreed to speak for me.

The woman put the sample cloth down. Harun took it back without folding it.

"At the next tide," she said. "If you still wish to come."

Her boat turned away. The man with the pole pushed hard, and the hull slid clear of ours. She remained standing until the gap widened. Then she sat beneath the shade and opened her box again, checking the sticks one by one as they went toward the landing.

Harun ordered the cloth returned to the hold. He told the steersman to keep the anchor where it was until the promised guide arrived.

"You can go," he said to me. "But you will not use my cargo as a pledge for your family."

"I know."

"Then leave the letter here unless they ask for it. A letter from a ruler across the sea will not tell them whose floor you may sleep on."

I wanted to argue that Siripada's name might make someone listen. It might. It could also make the people on shore wonder what kind of man needed a distant ruler's seal before he could ask for his mother's house.

I took the knife from my belt and laid it beside my sleeping mat. The letter stayed wrapped. I counted the coin I had left, then put away half for the passage back to the boat. The other half would pay the paddlers and perhaps a meal. I had imagined arriving with gifts. I would begin by paying a fare.

Sulad sat near the bow, looking toward the lower nets. He had not spoken since the woman's boat departed.

"Did you know the man she named?" I asked.

"I knew his father. I thought he would still be working."

"She said he was alive."

"She said he lived beyond the bend."

"That is good news."

"Maybe. I owe him something."

"What?"

Sulad rubbed his thumb along a scar on the side of his hand.

"A net weight. I took it when I left. I meant to give it back."

"After all these years?"

"After all these years."

He looked at me then. "You have come to ask for a house. I have come to return a stone. We will see which one of us is welcome."

I tried to laugh. I could not.

The tide had begun to turn. The water along the bank drew a new line of foam around the posts. A boat pushed off from the landing shelter and headed toward us, its paddlers working slowly against the current.

I watched it come without reaching for the letter.
