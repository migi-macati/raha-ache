# Scene 5 — The Pots That Went Elsewhere

The pot boat did not come the next morning.

I knew this because I had gone down before the light reached the far bank. The water was still low enough to show the old stones. Raya's staff lay across the upper step, and beneath it the timber she had lifted from the mud was drying on two smaller pieces of wood. She was crouched beside it with a wedge in her hand.

"Are you opening it?" I asked.

"Does it look open?"

The timber's lower face had gone dark where the water had entered. Raya pushed the wedge against the end. A thin piece came away in her fingers.

"That part will not hold," she said. "The rest might. I will know when it is dry enough to see."

"I could bring you another length."

"Whose?"

I had not asked. I had been looking at the space where a step ought to be.

"Then do not bring me one yet," she said. "Someone has to cut it, and someone has to give it up."

I left her with the wood. A boat came around the bend carrying a woman with a basket of charcoal and a man with his feet in the bilge. They passed our stones without turning. I watched until the bow went out of sight.

By the time the market was open, the owner with the cord had returned. He came on foot. His younger companion was not with him, and there was no coil on his shoulder. He stopped at the hall door and looked at the keeper's shelf.

"Did the two pot boats turn back?" I asked.

"One did not leave. The other went by the eastern channel."

"Did they hear the correction?"

"They heard what I told them. They asked whether they could land here. I told them no one had given them that leave."

"You told them about Nali?"

"They had no wish to go to Nali. They wanted this bank, and the step was closed. Do you think they paid me to learn the name of a path across the river?"

The keeper put down the small basket she was sorting. It held two pieces of cord and a broken fastening from a load brought the day before.

"Who paid the carriers?" she asked.

"One owner paid his men to bring the pots to the boat. The boat was hired for a morning. He had to pay the men to carry the pots back again."

"Did he pay the boatman?"

"He paid for the time he had taken. The boatman lost a journey he might have made for someone else."

"And the second?"

"The second owner found a landing he had used before. He paid for the longer passage. He did not ask you to make it shorter."

I was relieved to hear that the pots had gone somewhere. The owner saw my face.

"Do not look pleased," he said. "A seller has to name a price. The extra passage will come out of something. Perhaps his buyer pays more. Perhaps the men who shaped the pots are asked to take less."

"I did not close the step."

"No. Raya did. You have told me that often enough. But I told the owners you were making a place where they could ask for a landing. Now I have to explain why asking did not bring one."

"Because there was no safe place for their boats."

"That is an answer about the wood. I have heard it. What do I tell a man who paid for a boat because I said your keeper had marked a crossing?"

The keeper did not reach for either strip.

"Tell him the crossing was Ranu's," she said. "I did not mark one for pots."

"I know what you marked now."

"You heard it when I made the mark."

"I heard one woman crossed and returned. I heard that people had agreed to let her pass. I thought it meant the landing was being made useful."

"It is useful to some people," I said.

"Not to the man who carried his pots down and back."

He spoke without raising his voice. I wanted him to be angry. Then I could have said he had made a promise no one authorized and left the matter there. Instead he had brought me a loss I could count.

"Will you tell the owners you misunderstood?" I asked.

"I have. One asked whether I had been careless or whether you had changed the terms after I left. I told him I had not asked enough questions."

"That is fair."

"He did not ask whether I was fair. He asked who would pay for the wasted morning."

The keeper turned the broken fastening over in her hand.

"What did you answer?"

"I said I had no money to pay it. He said he would remember that when I came asking for cord work again."

I had seen the owner bring cord to the landing when we needed it. He had argued for paid watchers, but he had also given us something we used. I had been content to let him carry away a refusal. It had cost him more than his temper.

"Could I speak to him?" I asked.

"You could. He will be at the lower market until the pots are loaded for another day."

"I will tell him there was no permission for the landing."

"He knows that now."

"I will tell him it was not your fault."

"It was partly mine. I spoke before I had asked. Do not go there to take that from me."

The keeper looked up at him.

"Would you like your own account kept here?" she asked.

"Would a mark pay the carriers?"

"No."

"Then I will keep the words myself."

He went out. I followed at a distance because I had said I would speak to the pot owner, and I did not want to arrive with the man whose work I had helped spoil.

***

The lower market was crowded with baskets being lifted from the mud. I found the pot owner under a roof of woven leaves. His vessels stood in two rows on a plank, each separated by twists of dry grass. A boy was testing a handle with one finger. The owner took the pot away before the boy could lift it.

"Are you Ache?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then do not touch them."

I stopped beside the plank.

"I came about the boat that did not leave."

"Which part? The boat, the pots, or the men who carried them?"

"All three."

"Then you have come to the wrong person for two of them. The boat belongs to a man who waited at the water. The carriers have gone to other work. I own the pots."

He picked up a vessel with a narrow mouth. He turned it slowly until a pale line near the base caught the light.

"This one was sound yesterday," he said. "A carrier set the basket down hard when I told him to bring it back. It may still hold oil. It may not. I will have to test it, and if it leaks I cannot sell it for the same use."

"How much did you lose?"

"I have not sold them yet. I know what I paid the carriers and the boatman. I do not know what the cracked pot will cost me. Do you have that much?"

"No."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I should know."

He set the pot down carefully.

"I asked the cord owner whether the new landing could take a load. He said a crossing had been marked and people were watching the water. I took that for an answer. I should have asked who had given leave. He should have asked before he spoke. And you should have told people plainly that your step was still shut."

"Raya told us it was shut."

"Raya was right. I am not asking her to put my pots on rotten wood."

I had prepared to defend her. The owner had taken the words out of my mouth.

"Would you bring them when she repairs it?" I asked.

"Perhaps. If I can find a boat, if the buyer still wants them, if Dala allows the path, and if I am told the truth before I hire men."

"That is what I can offer."

"You can offer to ask those people. You cannot offer their answers."

A customer approached and examined the small pots. The owner turned to him at once. I waited while they bargained over two vessels. The customer left without buying either. When the owner faced me again, his expression had not changed.

"I heard there was another path across the water," he said. "The man with the cord says your witness brought back word that it was closed."

"One path was allowed for one woman. The rack path was not."

"Then why is that my concern? I wanted to sell pots here."

"It became your concern when the crossing was told as leave for other boats."

"Someone used a woman's journey to sell me a morning's work. That is what I understand."

I looked toward the river. From where we stood I could not see Raya's step or the keeper's strips. The pot owner could not see them either. He had hired a boat on the strength of a sentence spoken in the market.

"I will go back and say this before the men who come to ask," I said. "No strip gives a landing. A person must hear from those who own the boat, keep the path, and judge the stones. The answer may be no."

"Then some men will go elsewhere."

"Yes."

"Are you willing to have them say it was your answer?"

I thought of the cord owner, who had already taken blame for speaking too soon. I thought of Bina refusing to carry my preferred words. I had wanted someone else to explain the closed path so that the refusal would not be attached to me.

"I will say it myself," I answered.

"Then say also that I paid men for a morning I could not use. You cannot make that loss vanish by telling the next man earlier."

He turned to the next customer. I had nothing more to offer him. I left while he was still weighing the price of his pots.

***

The keeper was hearing a quarrel about a broken basket when I returned. I waited outside. The woman with the basket wanted the man who had carried it to replace the missing bottom. He said the basket had been weak before he touched it. Neither had brought the maker. The keeper told them she could remember what each said, but she could not see the basket before it broke.

I had heard her say such things before. This time I stayed until both had finished.

When they left, I told her about the pot owner. I told her the cracked vessel might be worth less, and that I had no payment for it. I told her he had not asked Raya to open the step. He wanted a warning before he hired a boat.

"Did he ask us to mark his loss?" she said.

"No. He said he would remember it."

"Then let him. We cannot cut a mark for a bargain he made elsewhere."

The owner with the cord was still at the landing. He had come back with his younger companion and was checking the length of a rope against a boat's side. I went to him where others could hear.

"I spoke to the pot owner," I said.

He looked up. The younger man kept his hand on the rope.

"Did he ask you to pay?"

"He asked whether I could. I cannot. He said you spoke too soon, and so did he. He said I should have made the closed step plain before either of you hired a boat."

The owner nodded once.

"Then he told you what he told me."

"He also said he will not hire another boat until he has heard from the people whose agreement he needs."

"That will take time."

"Yes."

A woman standing with a basket of salt asked whether the old stones could take her boat that afternoon. I could have pointed to the dry edge and told her that Ranu had crossed there. The stones were visible, and the water was quieter than it had been the day before. The boatman beside her was listening.

"Raya must judge the stones," I said. "Dala must agree to the path. Your boatman must agree to wait while you ask. No one has agreed for you yet."

The woman looked toward Raya's barrier.

"Then I will go to the upper landing," she said. "My brother has carried salt there before."

The boatman took up his pole. They left before I could ask whether they would return another day.

The cord owner watched the boat move away.

"You have sent another one," he said.

"I told her what I knew."

"You did. I heard it."

"Will you tell the next owner?"

"I will tell him what I heard, and what happened to my pot boats. I will not promise him that your step will open."

The younger man tightened the rope around the cleat. He had been waiting while we spoke.

"Will you pay him for this time?" I asked the owner.

"He works with me. We settled his morning before we came."

The younger man glanced up.

"He paid for the rope work," he said. "If we stay to watch another boat, that is another bargain."

The owner looked at him, then at me.

"There. Another person who knows how to ask."

I had expected the cord owner to accuse me before the people on the bank. Instead he began to coil his rope. The salt boat was already beyond the bend. I could not call it back with a better explanation, and I had no right to send it where its owner did not wish to go.

Behind me the keeper had come to the hall door. She had heard enough to tell anyone who asked what I had said. I did not ask her to cut a mark for it. The cord owner could repeat the same words, or remember the lost pot morning more sharply. Both accounts would follow me into the next market.
