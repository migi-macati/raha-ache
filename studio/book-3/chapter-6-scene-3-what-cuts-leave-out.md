# Book III, Chapter 6 — Scene 3: What the Cuts Leave Out

**Packet:** RA-B3C6-SCENE-03. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Scenes 1–2 leave the original one-journey/two-basket strip intact, a new strip uncut, Tawan's crew owed payment for waiting, Raya's step uninspected and the new seller's buyer lost. The boat owner may explain his account but cannot grant Dala's path. **Historical status:** Invented marks and hearing, no attested early-sixteenth-century administrative form.

---

The keeper put three stones on the table. One was white, one dark, and one had a thin red vein through it. She had taken them from the path because they were flat enough to stand without rolling.

"Which one is a journey?" she asked.

Tawan looked at the stones and then at me.

"All of them, if I am carrying them."

The keeper moved the dark one aside.

"Suppose this is the boat. These two are baskets."

"Then I carry two baskets," he said.

"How many times?"

"Once, if I have the sense to put them both in."

The crewman had followed us from the bend. He stood near the doorway with the split skin of his palm wrapped in a strip of cloth. When Tawan said that, he gave a short laugh.

"You did not put them both in the first time," he said. "The woman made us take one out to keep it from pressing on the other."

"We carried them in one journey," Tawan said.

"That is what I am saying. One journey. Two loads in the boat, if you count what we lifted."

The keeper pushed the stones together.

"That is what the marks failed to tell you."

"They told me two," Tawan said.

"Because you asked a question we did not hear."

He reached for the uncut bamboo strip. She moved it out of his reach.

"Let him hold it," I said.

"He may hold it when we agree what it is for. The first strip was passed from hand to hand before anyone knew what the other meant."

Tawan looked at me, waiting for me to tell her that he had a right to touch it. I had been ready to do so. I kept my hands on my knees.

The crewman picked up the white stone.

"Here is the journey," he said. He put it at one end of the table. "And these are the baskets." He set the other two beside it.

The keeper nodded.

"Then make one mark for that journey and two for those baskets."

Tawan took a piece of charcoal from the brazier. He drew one line across the table's edge and two shorter lines beneath it.

The crewman leaned close.

"That is the same as yesterday."

Tawan looked at his own marks. The charcoal had broken under his thumb. He rubbed it away with his palm, leaving a black smear in the grain.

"What if we make the journey longer?" I asked.

"We did," the keeper said. "You saw it."

"Then put the marks on different pieces."

The keeper brought two short strips of bamboo. On one she cut a notch. On the other she cut two. She laid them beside the stones.

Tawan took the first strip.

"One journey."

He took the second.

"Two baskets."

"Now tell me what you will carry," the keeper said.

"Two baskets, one journey."

"And when?"

"Before the next high water."

The crewman looked toward the doorway.

"What if Raya has not cleared the step?"

Tawan's fingers tightened around the strips.

"Then we wait."

"Who pays us for waiting?" the crewman asked.

Tawan set the bamboo down.

"We have spoken of that."

"You said you would pay us. You have not said how much."

The keeper reached for a third strip.

Tawan shook his head.

"If you cut a mark for every word we say, we will need a whole tree."

"Then perhaps we should say fewer things."

The crewman did not smile. He had taken the cloth off his hand and was looking at the small red cut in the palm.

"I need to know whether I am paid for holding the boat, or only for arriving where you told me."

Tawan looked away. The keeper left the third strip uncut.

I had wanted the two strips to make the matter simple. The crewman had found another question before I could enjoy the result.

"Could we tie a knot for the waiting?" I asked.

"What does a knot mean?" Tawan said. "One hour? One tide? A meal?"

"A tide," I said.

"And if the water does not fall enough to work?"

"Then another tide."

"Another knot?"

I put the cord down. It had been a poor answer.

The keeper pushed the stones aside. She placed the two strips on the dry board, away from the original tally.

"Let us try it with something that has not been carried," she said.

The crewman went outside and brought back a small basket of empty shells from the path. He set it beside the table. The basket belonged to the woman who collected shell grit for the potters. He had asked her before taking it, and she followed him to the doorway to see what we were doing.

"That is mine," she said.

"We will put it back," he answered.

"Who will?"

"I will."

She nodded once and remained by the door.

The keeper took the basket and moved it to the other end of the table.

"One basket, one journey," she said.

Tawan looked at the two strips. He held up the one with the single cut.

"One journey."

"And the goods?"

He reached for the other strip and hesitated. It had two cuts.

"That one says two baskets."

"So it cannot be used for this basket," the crewman said.

The keeper brought another blank strip.

"Then we make one for this load. But first, whose basket is it?"

The woman at the door raised her hand.

"Mine."

"Who may carry it?"

"The young man asked. I said he could carry it here and bring it back. I did not say he could take it on a boat."

The crewman put down the strip.

"Then I cannot carry it on a boat."

"Correct," the keeper said.

The woman took the basket back. She counted the shells before leaving, not because she thought we had stolen any, but because she had brought them for a purpose and did not want to lose one.

Tawan watched her go.

"Now we must ask the owner of every basket?"

"Before we carry it away," the keeper said.

"That much I understand."

He picked up the original strip and looked at it for a long time. Then he put it down without touching the cuts.

"Yesterday I thought the marks were about my boat. They were about her baskets. I see it now."

The crewman pointed to the door.

"And the path?"

"What path?"

"The path from the boat to the hall. If I carry a basket, where do I put my feet?"

I looked toward the river. The upper step was still wet. Beyond it the narrow strip of ground where Dala set her frame lay in shadow. Her drying rack had been moved back to its old place. There was room for one person to pass if the nets were lifted. I had been thinking of that as a small inconvenience.

"Above the step," I said. "Then along the path."

"Past Dala's frame?"

"Only if she is not using it."

The crewman looked at me.

"Did she agree?"

I had not asked her. I had thought the new strips were enough to make the next journey safe.

"She has used the path every day," I said.

"That was not his question," the keeper answered.

Tawan rose.

"I will ask her."

"She may be fishing," I said.

"Then I will wait until she comes back."

He walked out before I could stop him. The crewman stayed, rubbing the edge of the bamboo with his thumbnail. The keeper took the trial strips off the table and placed them beside the uncut one. She left the original strip alone.

Dala came to the hall while the light was still strong. Tawan walked beside her, carrying her broken frame. Pawi followed with a coil of cord. The frame had been bound at one corner with an old piece of fiber, but the split still showed through.

Tawan put it on the floor.

"She says the path is hers," he said.

"It is the way to her fishing ground," I answered.

Dala looked at me.

"That is not what I said."

"What did you say?"

"I said I use the path to bring my frame to the water. I do not want men carrying jars over it while my net is spread. The last time a boat came close, its line pulled my frame sideways and broke the joint. I have not been paid for the fish I lost."

"I remember."

"Then why did you tell them they could pass?"

"I said they could pass if you were not using it."

"Who will tell them when I am using it?"

I looked at the crewman. He had no answer. Tawan held the broken frame with both hands, as if it were heavier than it was.

"Could we make a separate path?" I asked.

Dala shook her head.

"Through whose ground?"

I looked past her toward the houses. The nearest yard belonged to Amaya's household. I owed Amaya a cloth and had no right to send strangers across her yard.

"I don't know," I said.

Dala took the frame from Tawan.

"Then do not promise one."

The keeper brought the trial strips to the doorway.

"We were trying to make the next agreement clearer," she said. "One strip for the journey, one for the goods. We had not asked about the ground."

Dala examined the cuts.

"You could make a hundred of these," she said. "None would mend my frame."

The keeper did not defend the strips. She laid them on the table where Dala could see them.

"The claim for your frame is on its own strip," she said. "It has not been marked paid."

Dala looked at the old claim hanging beside the doorway. Then she looked at the new strips.

"Keep them apart," she said. "People will think a new mark means the old one is finished."

I went to the wall and moved the claim strip farther from the trial pieces. I left it where anyone entering could still see it.

"Is that enough?" I asked.

"No. But it is clearer."

Tawan set his bag down and looked toward the river.

"Then we have two strips for a journey we cannot make, and a path we have not been allowed to use."

"Yes," I said.

He rubbed his forehead.

"I came here because I thought the first strip gave me two journeys. Now I see that even a new strip would not give me one."

Dala lifted her frame onto her shoulder. Pawi took the coil of cord and went ahead to clear the doorway. She followed him without waiting for us to settle anything.

The keeper left the trial strips on the dry board. She did not cut a mark for the path. There was no agreement to record.

Outside, the water had fallen far enough to show the pale edge of Raya's repair. It looked sound from where I stood. I knew better than to call that an inspection.

Tawan went to find his crew. I remained beside the table, looking at the two new strips and the old one. I had wanted a way to make a promise survive the journey between two people. I had forgotten the people whose ground lay between the boat and the hall.
