# Book III, Chapter 6 — Scene 6: The Question Left Open

**Packet:** RA-B3C6-SCENE-06. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** The first journey was paid and completed; no second was authorized. Tawan has given his crewman rice acknowledged as half of the waiting compensation, with the rest unpaid; the older steersman's waiting pay is separate. Dala's frame/lost catch, Raya's household covering and Ache's cloth owed to Amaya remain outstanding.

---

The potter was already loading his jars when I came to ask whether I could carry them for the basket maker. He had arranged them in two rows on the packed earth outside his yard. A boy had put a handful of dry grass between each pair so the rims would not strike.

"She sent you?" the potter asked.

"She asked whether I could help her boy."

"Then take the ones with the red marks. The others belong to a buyer who has not come."

I bent to lift the first jar. The potter stopped me.

"How many can you carry without knocking them together?"

"Two."

"Try one."

I put my arms around it. The clay was cool where it had been shaded and warm where the sun had touched it. When I lifted, a line of wet earth stuck to the base. I shifted the weight against my hip.

A woman came from the river path and called my name. It was Pawi's sister. She had been sent because Dala and Pawi were ready to lift the rack before the water rose again.

"I told Dala I would come after the water fell," I said.

"It has fallen. They are waiting."

The potter looked at the jar in my arms.

"Will you carry it or put it down?"

I set it back in the grass. I had promised the maker the morning and Dala the falling water. Both had understood me to mean the same hours.

"I cannot do both now," I said.

"Then tell the maker before her boy waits for you."

I went to her house. The boy had already tied a carrying pole to the first pair of jars. The maker was trimming the returned basket, her knife moving in short, sharp strokes.

"I have to help Dala lift her rack," I said. "I cannot carry the jars this morning."

She stopped cutting.

"You told me you could."

"I thought the water would fall later."

"You thought without asking."

"Could I come after the rack is lifted?"

"The potter has his own work. He will not keep the jars for me because you misread the tide."

"I can come tomorrow."

"I will find someone else today."

"And the trial binding for Dala?"

She set down the knife.

"I said I would make it after the jars were carried. If someone else carries them, I will pay that person or owe them work. Your promise does not buy my fiber."

"I know."

"Then go. Dala is waiting."

Her boy untied the pole and began looking toward the houses for another pair of hands. I left him to find them. I had made a smaller mess than the one at the landing, but it was mine, and the maker would remember it when I next asked for a favor.

Dala had spread a mat beside the rack so the wet net would not touch the mud. Pawi stood with his shoulder beneath the long side of the frame. They had loosened the old cords and were waiting for someone to take the other end.

"You came," Dala said.

"I told the maker I could not carry her jars."

"Then take this corner."

We lifted together. The frame had held when it was dry, but the wet net dragged it sideways. Pawi's shoulder dipped. I caught the far end and felt the split joint twist against my palm.

"Down," Dala said.

We lowered it onto the mat. She ran a finger along the crack. The old binding had opened at the point where the wood bent.

"That is what I wanted you to feel," she said.

"More cord will not make it straight."

"It may hold a short time. It will not give back the fish I could not catch."

Pawi brought a thin piece of wood from the edge of the rack. It had been cut for a handle, not a brace. He held it against the broken joint and showed how a longer piece might cross the split.

"I can shape one if we find wood fit for it," he said. "But I won't cut from the rack while it is still doing work."

Dala nodded. She had not asked me to choose a repair for her.

We moved the net to a higher line and set the broken frame in the shade. Dala could still use her smaller gear while she decided what to do. My arms ached from the weight. I had not paid for a lost fish or a length of wood. I had helped them see why a promise of fiber was not a repair.

When I reached the hall, Tawan was there with the young crewman and the older steersman. The woman who had paid for the first journey stood beside the keeper, holding the mat that had covered her two baskets. She had come because the keeper asked whether she would repeat the first bargain once more, with the owner present.

Tawan had brought his own small strip of bamboo. He placed it on the table beside the original tally but did not touch the old one.

"I have thought about what I heard," he said. "I saw two short cuts and believed I had leave for two journeys. I was wrong about what the cuts meant."

The keeper held up the original strip by its uncut end.

"I said one boat, two baskets, after Raya and Dala gave leave. I said nothing here promised another journey."

"I did not hear the last words. That is what I have said."

"I believe you did not hear them," she answered. "I still said them."

The woman with the mat put her hand on the table.

"I paid for the two baskets to go once. They went. I owe nothing for the jars that were not mine."

"You do not," Tawan said.

The crewman looked at him.

"Then you did not send me for a journey she had bought?"

"No. I sent you because I thought I had leave for a second one. I did not."

"Will you tell the man who was waiting with the jars?"

"I will tell him myself."

The keeper set the strip down where all could see it. The long cut was still there, and the two short ones. No one proposed scraping them away.

"Then the first bargain is finished," she said. "One paid journey. Two baskets carried. No second journey bought or allowed."

Tawan repeated it. The woman repeated it. The crewman repeated it, though he kept his eyes on the rice packet tied at his belt.

"What of the time I waited?" he asked.

Tawan put his hand on the new strip they had cut beside the bank.

"I paid you half yesterday. I owe the other half. I owe the steersman for holding the boat. Those are mine to answer."

The older man spoke for the first time.

"Then do not put a mark on my behalf until I have said what I accept."

The keeper moved the crewman's strip away from the steersman's empty place on the table.

"I will not."

"Could you pay him today?" I asked Tawan.

"I can give him the same amount of rice when I have it. I will not promise a tide for which I have no fare."

The steersman shook his head.

"I may take other work before then."

"You may," Tawan said.

The crewman folded his arms.

"And I will not take your next load until we speak of both the journey and the waiting."

"I heard you yesterday."

"Then say it again here."

Tawan looked at the keeper's clean strip. He did not ask her to cut it. There was no new cargo, no permission from Raya, and no leave from Dala.

"I will ask before I hire you," he said. "I will tell you what I can pay if the landing closes. You can refuse."

The crewman nodded. He had not forgiven the lost day, but he no longer had to defend a second journey that never belonged to him.

The keeper laid three separate pieces on the table. The old tally was for the completed basket journey. The newer piece recorded the crewman's half payment and what remained due. The third was blank.

"This is where we begin when there is another load," she said. "We ask whose goods they are, who carries them, where they may pass, what the owner is paying, and what happens if the way closes. We say it aloud. We ask each person to say it back."

"That is a great deal of speaking for two baskets," Tawan said.

"It was a great deal of waiting when we did not," the crewman answered.

No one laughed. The keeper left the third piece uncut.

Tawan picked up his bag and went toward the river. The older steersman followed at a distance, carrying his own rope. The young crewman stayed long enough to ask the keeper whether he could see the strip for his waiting pay whenever he returned. She said he could. He went after the others without agreeing to another journey.

The woman with the mat took her things home. She had not been made to pay for a stranger's jars. Tawan had accepted that his first bargain gave him no second trip. The crewman had a claim against his employer that could be shown without making the hall its debtor. None of this opened the step. Raya still had to judge it after the water. Dala still controlled the path.

Amaya came in as the keeper was setting the strips on their dry shelf. She carried a folded length of cloth across her arm. It was old, patched at one end, and clean. Behind her stood a woman from a neighboring household whom I knew by sight but not by name.

"I heard you have begun keeping promises where anyone can see them," Amaya said.

"We are keeping the ones people bring us," the keeper answered.

Amaya laid the cloth on the table.

"This is the kind he owes me."

I knew what she meant. I had promised her one cloth for the use of her household's stores when the landing work began. I had spoken of finding it after the first boats paid. The first boats had brought disputes instead.

"I still owe it," I said.

"Then why is there no strip for that?"

The keeper looked at me. I had spent the morning asking Tawan to own his unpaid men's work. I could not tell Amaya to wait outside while I decided how my own debt should be remembered.

"Ask her what she wants marked," I said.

Amaya unfolded the cloth. The neighboring woman held one corner while Amaya showed its width and the patch. It was not a new cloth, and she was not offering it as payment. She wanted the keeper to see what she meant by one piece fit for household use.

"One cloth of this measure," she said. "Not the torn end of a sail. Not something taken from my own store and handed back to me."

"Have you agreed when he will bring it?" the keeper asked.

"No."

I looked at Amaya.

"I can bring it after I earn it."

"That is what you said before."

"Then I have no day to promise."

The keeper did not cut a date. She took a blank strip and held it toward Amaya.

"Will you accept a mark that says he owes the cloth, with the time still open?"

Amaya looked at the neighboring woman, then back at me.

"I will accept that the debt is spoken here. I have not agreed to wait forever."

The keeper cut a single mark. She asked Amaya to say what it meant. Then she asked me. I repeated the measure, the purpose, and the fact that I had not paid. The neighboring woman had heard both of us. The keeper placed the strip on the dry shelf, separate from Tawan's wages and Dala's damaged frame.

Amaya took up her old cloth.

"When you bring the new one," she said, "you will bring it to me. You will not put it in the hall and call the matter finished."

"I understand."

She turned to leave. The neighboring woman remained beside the table, looking at the shelf.

"If he does not bring it," she asked the keeper, "who tells him he has waited long enough?"

The keeper looked toward Amaya, who had stopped at the door. Then she looked at me.

I had asked the boat owner to accept a hearing when the river could not give him what he wanted. Now the person I owed was standing in the same doorway, and the keeper's clean bamboo was running out. Outside, a boat passed the bend without coming to our step. I waited for Amaya to answer.
