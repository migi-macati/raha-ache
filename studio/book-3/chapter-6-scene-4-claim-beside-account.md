# Book III, Chapter 6 — Scene 4: A Claim Beside the Account

**Packet:** RA-B3C6-SCENE-04. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** After Scene 3, the original journey tally remains intact; Dala has refused use of her fishing path, her frame and lost-catch claim are unpaid, and the basket maker's proposed binding has not been made or accepted. The trial journey/goods strips confer no passage.

---

The next morning I found the keeper taking the bamboo down from the doorway. She had laid a dry mat on the floor so the strips would not touch the mud carried in on people's feet. The old claim for Dala's broken frame lay at one end. The trial marks for Tawan's boat lay at the other. Between them was the original strip, the long cut and the two short cuts dark with yesterday's handling.

"Are you putting them away?" I asked.

"Moving them out of the rain."

She pointed to a dark patch spreading across the wall above the doorway. The roof had leaked in the night. The mat beneath it smelled of damp reed.

"Where will anyone see them?"

"Where I can keep them dry."

A boy came from the houses with a jar balanced against his hip. He stopped at the threshold, watching her lift the strips one by one.

"My aunt says the fisherwoman has been paid," he said.

The keeper did not put down the strip in her hand.

"Who told her?"

"The man who buys her fish. He saw the old claim taken down."

"It was taken down because the wall is wet."

"He says there is new cord for her frame. He says the marks have been cleared."

I looked at the strips on the mat. None had been scraped smooth. Dala's claim was still there, its cut edge rough where Pawi had helped mark the loss. But I could see what the boy had seen from the doorway: the keeper carrying the old strip away, the new ones lying beside it, and a woman promised some cord.

"Where is the fish buyer?" I asked.

"At the bank. He says he will come back when she can fish."

"She has been fishing," the boy said after a moment. "Only not with the big frame."

The keeper set the original journey tally on the highest shelf, beyond the drip. She left the claim on the mat.

"Tell your aunt that I have marked nothing paid."

The boy shifted the jar.

"Will you tell the buyer?"

"I will," I said.

I went down to Dala's rack before he could ask what authority I had to speak for her. Pawi was there with his feet planted in the mud, holding the damaged frame upright. The joint had opened again. A loop of old binding dangled from it, dark and swollen. Dala sat beside a shallow basket of small fish, sorting the ones she could dry from those that had to be eaten before evening.

"Someone says your claim is finished," I told her.

She did not look up.

"Then someone has caught my missing fish."

"He saw the keeper move the strip. He heard about the binding."

"The binding I have not seen?"

"Yes."

She put a small fish aside and lifted a larger one by the tail. Its belly had torn where it was caught.

"Take that to him," she said. "Ask if he wants to buy it as a whole fish."

"I came to correct him."

"Do that, then. But don't tell him I have stopped asking for what I lost."

Pawi lowered the frame. The broken joint scraped his ankle. He caught it before it fell into the fish basket.

"Would a new binding hold it?" I asked.

"Perhaps," Dala said. "Perhaps it would pull the split wider. We need to know how the wood takes the strain when the net is wet."

"The basket maker offered a short trial."

"She offered to make one when she finished her other work. I have not hired her to mend this."

I knelt beside the frame. The wood was rough where it had cracked. The old fiber had sunk into the split and left a pale groove.

"Could I take it to her?"

Dala drew the frame back toward herself.

"You may carry it if I ask you. I want to see what she makes first."

Pawi held the loose end steady while Dala lifted the joint clear of the mud. She pressed it with her thumb, then released it. The two sides parted again.

"I need a piece that will sit across this," she said. "Cord alone may hold it for one pull. Then it will break where the cord ends."

"I know a maker who works wood."

"Raya has enough work on the step. Don't promise her hands."

I had been about to do exactly that. Dala wiped her thumb on her skirt and looked at the path.

"Go and find the buyer. He thinks I have stopped selling because the hall has settled my claim. He has not come for two mornings. If he won't buy what I can still catch, that is another loss."

I left the frame with her and went toward the bend. The buyer was sitting in the shade of an overturned canoe, picking scales from a reed tray. His own small basket was empty.

"Dala has fish today," I said.

"Not enough for my household and the drying shed."

"She says you have not come."

"She says her frame is broken. Yesterday I heard she had new cord and the hall had cleared her account. If she is paid, she can mend it and bring me a proper catch."

"She has not been paid."

He looked toward the hall.

"I saw them take down the strip."

"The wall leaked. They moved it to keep it dry."

"Then why are people saying it is settled?"

"Because the maker offered to try a binding."

"Has she made it?"

"No."

"Has Dala taken it?"

"No."

He pulled a scale from the tray and flicked it away.

"Then what did you come to tell me?"

"That she still has fish. You can buy what she has, or refuse it because there is too little. But the hall has not paid her claim."

He stood and brushed sand from his knees.

"Did she send you?"

"She told me to correct you."

"Then I will hear what she has caught."

He took his empty basket and walked past me. He did not thank me. I had expected relief when he agreed to look; instead I felt the cost of two mornings when he had not come.

At the hall the keeper had found a dry place for the strips on a low shelf beneath the window. She had tied the old claim with a loose loop of plain cord, leaving its cuts visible. The original boat tally lay separately on the high shelf. The new trial strips were on the table, where anyone could pick them up and mistake them for an agreement.

"Those should not be beside the claim," I said.

"They aren't."

"They were, when the boy came."

"They were on a mat because the roof was leaking."

"He saw you take the old claim down. The buyer heard it had been cleared."

The keeper put both palms flat on the table.

"Then I must tell people what I did."

"Yes."

She looked at the roof. A drop fell beside the mat, darkening it.

"And you must help me move this table before the next rain."

We carried it across the room. One leg caught on a raised board. The keeper lifted her end while I pulled mine, and the leg came free with a splintering sound. We set the table beneath the dry wall, away from the window. The strips were not damaged. The table leg was.

"That will need a wedge," she said.

I bent to inspect the split. The hall had no pile of spare timber waiting for me. I remembered Madan's wood and how many people had tried to spend it before he agreed to sell it.

"Do not take anything from Amaya's store," the keeper said.

"I was not going to."

"You looked toward the store."

I had. I straightened and went to find a small offcut among the scraps left from the repair. Raya's nephew was sitting beside them, scraping a handle. He told me which pieces had already been promised and which were fit only for fire. I asked for one thin wedge. He held it up, examined the grain, and said I could have it if I helped him turn a plank before the tide.

I helped. The plank was heavier than it looked. We carried it shoulder-high across the mud and laid it where Raya had told him to keep it, well above the waterline. By the time I returned with the wedge, the keeper had taken the strips down again and was holding them before two women from the houses.

"This is the boat's disputed mark," she said, showing the original without letting it out of her hand. "It has not been changed. This is Dala's claim. Nothing on it says she has been paid. These are trials we made yesterday. They give no one a journey or a path."

One woman nodded. The other asked whether a man who had waited all day could demand a boat's payment from the hall.

"No," the keeper said. "He can ask the man who hired him. If they bring the matter here, we can hear what each promised."

"Then what good are all these marks?" the woman asked.

The keeper looked at the strips in her hands.

"They keep us from pretending the questions have disappeared."

The woman turned away, unsatisfied. The keeper put the strips back on their separate shelves and let me fit the wedge into the table leg. It held when she leaned on the table, but I would not have trusted it under a full jar.

Near midday I went to the basket maker. She had the returned fourth basket open on her knee, pulling out a damaged row. Her boy was turning a coil of fiber so it would dry evenly.

"Have you made Dala's binding?" I asked.

"No. I told you what work came first."

"The fish buyer thinks it has been made and that her claim is settled."

"Did you tell him that?"

"No."

"Then find who did."

"I told him it was not made."

She tested a strand and laid it across the basket's rim.

"If I make a short length for Dala, I will want something for the work. The fiber was mine before anyone spoke of her frame. And if it fails in wet use, I will not have you say I sold her a mended frame."

"What would you take?"

She looked at the basket on her knee.

"Help my boy carry the clay jars from the potter's yard tomorrow. He cannot take them alone while I finish this. Then I will make a short trial length. Dala can test it before she decides whether to pay for more."

"I can carry them."

"Ask the potter first. The jars are his until I buy them. Do not lift them because I told you to."

I laughed once, without amusement. It seemed everyone had learned to speak that way after meeting me.

"I will ask him."

She went back to her basket. No fiber changed hands. I left knowing I had promised my own morning, and nothing of Dala's lost catch had been paid.

At the rack, the fish buyer had taken six of Dala's smaller fish. He was counting them into his basket. Pawi stood beside him, watching the count. Dala had put aside two for her own pot.

"Did he buy them?" I asked.

"He paid for six," she said. "He did not buy the frame."

The buyer tied his basket and went away. He had taken less than he usually did. Dala watched him go, then turned the broken joint over in her hands.

"The maker will try a short binding after I help carry jars," I said.

"Who asked you to promise her that?"

"I did."

"Then it is your work. Do not call it my payment."

"I won't."

She set the frame down beside her basket.

"Come tomorrow after the water falls. Pawi and I need to lift the rack clear so we can see whether the joint is splitting under its own weight. You can hold one end. That will tell us more than looking at it here."

"I will come."

"You have also promised the maker's jars."

"In the morning. The rack after the water falls."

Dala gave me a long look. She knew as well as I did that the tide might fall before the jars were carried.

"If you cannot be here, say so before we lift," she said. "I can ask someone else. I cannot hold the frame in the air while you are finding out where you ought to be."

I promised to send word. It was a smaller promise than the one I had made to the boat owner, but this time I asked her to repeat what she needed from me. She said: one pair of hands at the rack, after the water fell, and warning if I could not come. Nothing about a repaired frame or a paid claim.

When I returned to the hall, Tawan's young crewman was waiting beside the table with a strip of frayed rope in his hand.

"My owner has found someone who will take the jars from the far bank," he said. "He wants to know whether your new way of speaking can get us one safe journey before then."

"Raya has not cleared the step."

"I know."

"Dala has not given leave for the path."

"I know that too."

He put the rope on the table.

"Then send someone to ask them. He is tired of hearing what cannot be done."

The keeper reached for a clean strip and stopped. I could see the question in her hand: whether a new mark would record a promise we had no right to make.

"Come back when the water is lower," I said. "I will ask them what they are willing to inspect. I cannot promise you a journey."

The crewman took up the rope again.

"Tell Tawan that yourself," he said.

He left without waiting for the keeper to cut anything. On the dry wall Dala's old claim remained visible, separate from the boat's disputed strip. The roof still leaked. The table held on a wedge that had cost me half a tide of labor. I had corrected a false report and made two promises with my own hands. Neither would put Tawan's jars on a boat.
