# Book III, Chapter 3 — Scene 5: The Right to Refuse

**Packet:** RA-B3C3-SCENE-05. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 4's public finding that Maynila landing workers displaced the marker. Claims for the fish, carrier's injured shoulder and Dala's net frame remain unsettled; no general toll has been approved. Lakandula has not personally appeared. **Historical status:** Fictional negotiations, reported household message and participants; no claim of an attested treaty or Lakandula quotation.

---

The Maynila owner came to the hall with a coil of new cord.

He dropped it beside the keeper's bench. It struck the floor hard enough to make the child sleeping behind the partition wake and cry.

"For the woman's net," he said. "There. One loss settled."

Amaya came from the cooking place and lifted the coil out of the child's reach.

"Which woman?"

"Dala."

"Then give it to Dala."

"I brought it here so everyone could see."

"She does not sleep here."

He looked at me as though I had told Amaya to shame him. I had said nothing. She carried the cord outside and laid it on the rack where Dala had left the damaged frame.

The owner followed her.

"She will say it is the wrong length."

"Then she can say so to you."

He returned to the bench without the satisfaction of having his gift accepted. He had helped pay for the first sounding and had lent two men to the landing repairs. He believed that entitled him to be heard before the people whose work had been interrupted.

"We cannot keep counting every complaint," he said. "The passage is open. I have boats waiting."

"The carrier cannot lift a basket," I said.

"Then his employer must find another man."

"His employer says the journey is finished."

"Then let him come here and say it."

The keeper was already sending for him. She had also asked the buyer to bring the basket marks, and Dala to bring the frame. The owner complained that we were holding another meeting when everyone should be working.

I wanted to tell him that his boats could work while he sat. Then I remembered that I had asked the landing workers to clear the steps quickly, and they had tied their line to a sounding stake. I had made the same demand in gentler words.

The man who had moved the stake arrived carrying a short piece of timber. He said he could set a new marker in the deeper line as soon as the witnesses agreed.

"Will you be paid?" the owner asked.

"I have work here."

"That was not my question."

The man set the timber beside the bench.

"I will do the work," he said. "I do not know who will pay me for it."

The keeper told him to leave the timber where it lay. The new stake would not be driven until the people from the far bank had seen the sounding.

The owner sat down and folded his arms.

"Then I will take my men back. I did not pay them to stand beside a piece of wood while strangers decide what it means."

"You may take them," Amaya said.

He looked up.

"Will you finish the repairs without them?"

"We will find out."

His face tightened. He had expected us to beg him to stay. I had expected it too.

By the time Dala arrived, the cord was still on the rack. She tested it between her fingers. She pulled a short length against the frame and shook her head.

"It will mend the part that was cut," she said. "It will not give us the hours we lost."

"I have not asked you to forget them," I said.

"Good. I have not asked you to buy the fish I did not catch."

She called Pawi over and asked him to show the keeper which loops needed replacing. He did so, counting each knot and then showing how the new cord would be joined to the old. He knew more about the repair than I did. The owner watched him without speaking.

The carrier came with the buyer and the man who had sent the fish. His injured arm was still wrapped. The sender had brought no goods. He said he would pay the carrier for another journey when the shoulder healed, but would not pay him for days spent sitting.

"He was hurt bringing your fish," the buyer said.

"He was hurt because the passage was wrong."

"Both things can be true."

The sender looked at the Maynila owner.

"Your men moved the mark."

"My men did not own that boat."

The older landing worker stood by the door. He had come because the keeper asked him to hear the finding again. He had brought his younger companion, who would say what he had seen. Neither man could pay the loss from the coins in his house. The owner knew that.

The keeper asked the buyer to name the baskets she could not sell. She gave the count from Amaya's rack and the price she had agreed before the journey. The sender disputed the price of two baskets, saying they had been packed with smaller fish. The buyer asked him to look at the marks made before the cargo was opened.

He looked, then said he would accept the count if the keeper repeated the difference aloud. She did. He agreed to replace the spoiled fish from his next catch, provided the buyer accepted it at the same price and did not demand coin that day.

The buyer said she would accept the fish if it arrived before the next market and was sound. She would not take a promise that could be changed when the tide was wrong.

The keeper cut a mark for the replacement and named two witnesses. The sender said he would bring the catch himself.

The carrier asked who would feed his household until he could work.

No one answered.

I had been waiting for the accounts to become small enough to settle. The shoulder was not an account that could be closed with a basket.

The older landing worker offered to carry loads for the injured man on the next two market days. He would take no wage for that work. The carrier asked whether he could lift as much as the man who had been hired.

"I can carry," the worker said.

"Can you carry for my employer without losing the work you already owe the landing?"

The worker looked at the keeper.

She said he could leave the repairs for two days if the owners found someone else to finish them. The Maynila owner made a noise of protest, but did not say no. The carrier agreed to try the arrangement for one market day and return if the work was not enough.

The hall had grown hot. The cooking fire had been banked, but the air was thick with the smell of rice and wet cloth. A child crawled beneath the bench and touched the new stake. The older worker moved it away before the child could pull it down.

Banu arrived with the woman who had brought her paddle to the waiting bank. With her was a man I had seen crossing from the far side on a narrow ferry. He carried a short staff used for walking on mud, not a weapon.

The woman said the man had been asked to carry words from a household that brought disputes to Lakandula.

"Did Lakandula send you?" I asked him.

"I was asked to hear whether a new charge was being set on the water."

"By whom?"

He named the person who managed that household's boats. I did not know the name. The woman with the paddle did. She said the man had carried messages for them before, and that he had returned with the same words each time.

"Then tell us what you were asked to say," the keeper said.

"No house has given you all the boats on the far shore. The people who follow Lakandula may hear a claim about their own passage, but they will not let another house promise their boats in their name."

He paused.

"That is what I was asked to carry. I have no order to agree to a payment."

The Maynila owner laughed once.

"Then you have come to refuse something no one offered."

"I came because people are already saying the new marker will have a price."

The keeper showed him the strip on which she had recorded the first sounding. It named a share for goods unloaded at the Maynila steps and no payment for boats that passed through.

"Who may change it?" he asked.

"The people who use the landing must hear a proposed change," she said. "Those who only pass cannot be made to pay by an agreement they did not make."

"And if a boat owner refuses?"

"Then we cannot say he agreed."

The owner who had brought the cord stood up.

"If every man can refuse, no one will pay for the passage."

Dala looked at him.

"Then do not ask us to pay for a passage that belongs to your boats."

"My boats bring goods you sell."

"And my nets feed people who do not own boats."

They faced each other across the bench. I thought he would walk out. Instead he pointed to the new cord on the rack.

"Will you take it?"

"I said it would mend the frame."

"Then take it."

"I will. I will also come back if the frame fails where the new cord joins the old."

He sat down. The keeper cut a mark for the cord received, leaving the work still to be checked.

The far-bank messenger asked what would happen when the next boat struck a marker. Would the owner of the boat have to bring his claim to Maynila, where the men who moved the wood would decide it?

The Maynila owner said the keeper knew the passage. The messenger said knowledge was not the same as an answer to a complaint.

I asked the keeper whether she would let a far-bank witness hear a claim against her own landing. She said she already had, and pointed to the woman with the paddle.

"That is one claim," the messenger said. "What of the next?"

The keeper asked him what his people would accept.

He said a boat owner should first bring a complaint to the shore whose work caused the loss. If the shore refused to hear it, the owner should be able to ask witnesses from both banks to see the mark and the cargo before it was moved. Neither shore should collect a new payment while the complaint was being heard.

The Maynila owner objected that people would use a complaint to avoid paying the landing share. The keeper answered that a boat which unloaded goods still owed the share agreed for the steps. A boat which passed through did not.

The messenger asked who would choose the witnesses.

"Each household may send someone who knows the passage," the woman with the paddle said. "But the person who made the claim must be allowed to hear what they found."

Dala raised her hand.

"And the people whose work was blocked? A boat may be gone before anyone asks what happened on the bank."

The keeper said they too could bring a claim. She asked Pawi whether he would be willing to repeat what he saw at the grounded boat when the next witnesses came. He nodded, then looked at his mother.

"He will speak for himself," Dala said. "I will be beside him."

The Maynila owner wanted a time limit. He said no one could keep a boat waiting for days while people searched for witnesses. The far-bank messenger agreed that a passage must be warned or opened at once if it was dangerous. The damaged cargo could be counted separately. The stake should not be changed secretly while the claim remained.

We had almost reached terms when the owner said the landing should collect a small payment from every boat to keep two watchers at the turn.

The woman with the paddle picked up her staff.

"Then we are finished."

He stared at her.

"Someone must pay them."

"Ask the owners whose boats need the watch. Do not take from the people who did not agree."

She started toward the doorway. I moved to let her pass, then spoke.

"We have agreed on the sounding and on how to hear the loss. We have not agreed on a new payment. Let the trial happen without one."

She stopped. The messenger waited beside her.

"Will you put that beside the other terms?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then I will stay until the keeper has marked it."

The keeper made a fresh strip. She did not call it a treaty. She marked who had agreed to witness the next sounding, who would hear complaints, and which payments remained unsettled. The first far-bank representative added his mark for his own house. The woman with the paddle added hers for her household. The messenger said he would carry the terms back to the people who sent him, without pledging their consent.

The Maynila owner refused to add his mark until the keeper recorded that he had supplied cord for Dala's frame and would lend one worker to finish the new marker. She did. He marked the strip, then said he still opposed any rule that let a passing boat refuse a charge for keeping the water safe.

"You may oppose it," the keeper said. "You may not collect it today."

He looked at me. He wanted me to overrule her. I had been given a place in the hall to hear claims, not the right to empty the keeper's words when they troubled an owner.

"I will not collect it," I said.

He left before the others. The coil of cord remained on Dala's rack. Pawi had already begun to cut it into lengths he could use.

When the meeting ended, the far-bank messenger asked me what title I intended to use when I sent the terms upriver.

"Ache," I said.

"That will not tell them which boats you speak for."

"Then say I speak for the Maynila landing and the people who have asked me to answer there."

He nodded.

"And say which people have not."

He went down to his ferry. I watched him cross without asking whether Lakandula would approve. I did not know if the man who carried our words would ever stand before him.

Behind me, the keeper hung the marked strip above the floor where the children could not reach it. It held fewer promises than the men had wanted when they entered the hall. The promises it held belonged to people who had been present to make them.
