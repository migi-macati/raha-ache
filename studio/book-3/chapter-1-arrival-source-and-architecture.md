## Continuity fault: the arrival is a trading passage, not an armada

The current Book III Chapter 1 outline says Ache returns "with power" and "as an experienced commander"; Book III Chapter 2 promises to use the expedition from Book II to retake Maynila. **But the actual published ending of Book II is specific and smaller:** Ache leads a voluntary personal mission aboard **Harun's trading vessel**, whose owner retains command and the right to refuse a hostile river. Sulad joins as a paid hand, Isan as an interpreter, both on independent terms. **Tamin's vessel accompanies only the first leg** toward its own buyers. Siripada grants a limited letter and commercial goods, **not an armed state fleet**. Ache has not married, obtained a foreign army, secured a Maynila foothold or proved who holds the river.

**Mandatory forward correction:** Book III Chapter 1 must begin with Harun's real boat, its cargo and its limits. The early chapters may develop new local allies, voluntary crews, negotiations and defensive force on page, but cannot retroactively convert the Book II passage into a fleet. Book III Chapter 2 can still deliver restoration by its end if its force and political support are earned within Chapters 1–2. Reconcile the Book III book-level starting state and Chapter 2's "Bruneian expedition" wording through scoped editorial updates after a complete causal plan. Do not silently invent a second expedition, royal order, wedding or cargo. Preserve the Book III 10-chapter main conflict resolution and bonus 11–12 historical coda.

## Historical and chronological evidence gates

- **Ache's exact return date and terms:** The 1521 Brunei encounters described in Pigafetta, Albo and the Genoese-pilot tradition are not eyewitness records of a subsequent Ache-led return to Maynila. The novel's timetable remains fictional unless a primary passage explicitly supports a particular event. Do not use the date of the 1521 Brunei encounter as the date of Ache's later homecoming.
- **Aganduru Móriz:** The 1882 printed *Historia general de las Islas Occidentales á la Asia adyacentes, llamadas Philipinas*, vol. 1, is catalogued as CODOIN vol. 78; the specific Spanish pp. 59–60 remain uninspected. The narrator is retrospective, not an eyewitness sailor in 1521. Do not turn later narrative reconstruction into an exact personal itinerary or dialogue. Bibliographic locator: https://books.google.com/books/about/Historia_general_de_la_Islas_Occidentale.html?id=jiViHQAACAAJ .
- **Lakandula chronology is a serious risk:** Historical Lakandula is documented in the Spanish-contact period around 1570–71, decades after 1521. His birth year, age in Ache's youth and role in an early return conflict are not established by the present dossier. **Do not automatically cast the historically named Lakandula as the same mature political rival at an early-1520s homecoming.** Investigate dating and whether an earlier Tondo power-holder should remain unnamed or fictional until evidence supports identification. This affects the current Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 outlines and the trilogy's political chronology.
- **Sulayman chronology:** The historically documented Sulayman belongs to the later Spanish-contact period. His relationship to Ache, exact age and presence during an early return cannot be presumed. The Book III succession arc must track elapsed decades and generations rather than place him prematurely on stage.
- **Marriage:** The book-level outline anticipates a Bruneian marriage and dynastic link, but Book II established no marriage or reciprocal romance with Laila. Do not identify Laila with the later wife, produce a wedding as a reward, or assert a date/identity from uncollated secondary summaries. If a marriage becomes necessary for the novel, establish the adult woman's independent decisions and chronology.
- **Maritime trade and political institutions:** William Henry Scott's *Barangay* and Tomé Pires's *Suma Oriental* offer comparative or period-adjacent evidence about Philippine and regional trade, but neither automatically authenticates the invented owners' contracts or specific 1520s Manila Bay jurisdictions. Distinguish source-supported background, inference and invented plot.
- **Language and material culture:** Check period-appropriate shore approaches, boats, cargo, place-names and speech against sources; avoid modern maps, administrative labels, exact borders or generalized uniform "Filipino" identity.

## Six-scene architecture: provisional

1. **The Water Outside the River.** Begin directly aboard Harun's vessel after the northbound passage. Weather, water, cargo and crew obligations are still real. Isan helps question coastal traders; Sulad recognizes a landmark but cannot identify current authority from a remembered shore. Harun chooses a safe anchorage outside the river, not a triumphal entry. Ache is tempted to mistake recognition of land for permission to land.
2. **The Place Where the Boats Stop.** A trading intermediary negotiates landing conditions. The local gatekeeper wants an account of Ache's cargo and purpose, not a speech about ancestry. Harun refuses to risk his goods to force an entry. Ache chooses a small party and a bounded first visit. Establish exactly who owns the landing and which arrangements may have changed during his absence.
3. **The House That Kept Working.** Ache approaches a household he expects to remember him. Its adult representative has paid dues, formed ties and protected workers under the authorities who stayed. They have obligations that Ache cannot erase by returning. One person may remember him warmly but still refuse to call him ruler. The emotional reversal should emerge from a concrete dispute over rent, work, kinship or river access.
4. **A Message Across the Water.** A messenger or trader with Tondo connections brings a competing account of current power. Do not assume the named Lakandula of the 1570s is already the mature 1520s antagonist. Ache learns what his letter can and cannot do. Isan's translations have limits; let a local voice correct Ache's interpretation. Harun will not turn his trade into an ultimatum.
5. **What the Shore Will Lend.** Ache makes an agreement for a temporary foothold: perhaps warehouse space, safe lodging, a hearing or a single boat, with real payment and an expiration condition. He earns this through an informed concession that costs him something. Sulad may dissent; the local ally has reasons beyond restoring Ache's title. No instantaneous army or universal welcome.
6. **The Bay That Forgot Him.** A specific political fact destroys Ache's old map of loyalties. He must choose to learn the present order rather than punish those who survived without him. End with a defensible first foothold, a credible opposing interest and a material path to recruit local support for Chapter 2's promised restoration. The core conflict of Chapter 1 resolves as *arrival and recognition of changed reality*, not military victory.

## Literary and causal QA

Ache's first-person voice should remain immediate, observant and fallible. He may desire the prestige of a conqueror while demonstrating the competence he earned in Brunei. Harun remains an independent owner; Isan interprets but cannot guarantee every language; Sulad has his own family and work stakes. The people of Maynila and Tondo must have reasons to prefer the order they know, including women, traders, servants, boat crews and household leaders. Give a returning character an unwelcome discovery that is specific, material and not reversible by a speech.

**Historical suspense:** Readers know Ache eventually becomes Raja Matanda, but should not be able to predict which local relationship will fail, who pays for the first foothold, or whether a familiar household will cooperate. The documented later political configuration cannot be projected backward without age and source checks.

**Chapter 2 handoff gate:** Before writing its restoration, specify the sources of boats, people, food, local legitimacy and force, including how much time passes between Chapter 1 and Chapter 2. If the timeline requires a longer buildup, show it on page without postponing Book III's Chapter 2 main resolution.

**Open blockers:** primary-language source collation; Lakandula age/identity at proposed return date; wife identity/chronology; reader feedback and rendered-live/audio checks. These are research and verification gates, not reasons to fabricate a 1520s political chronology.

## Counterevidence to an automatic Lakandula age objection

A 1992 genealogical study published in the *National Academy of Science and Technology Transactions* proposes that Raha Matanda, Raha Lakandula and Sulayman's father were probably brothers, with a link to the "king of Luzon" mentioned in 1521. This is a **scholarly hypothesis**, not a verified birth year or a primary eyewitness identification. If correct, it could make Lakandula an adult contemporary of Ache during an early return, so **do not rule him out as anachronistic merely because his best-attested political activity is in 1571**. Conversely, do not treat the proposed genealogy as established fact or infer his office, location or actions in the early 1520s. Read and verify the paper and its underlying sources before deciding whether to use him as the named Chapter 1 antagonist.
