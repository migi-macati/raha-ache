# Book III, Chapter 5 — Scene 3: The Man at the Bow

**Packet:** RA-B3C5-SCENE-03. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** After Scene 2, Ache has earned one small fish by carrying Sera's baskets. Sera may ask a jar buyer about Mulaw if he comes; no message has been sent. Sulad was present at the unnamed landing in published Book I Chapter 7 and is returning from independently paid boat work. The old silver paid a man to steady Mulaw's boat, not to land Nima's sister's cloth or secure a future passage. **Historical status:** Fictional encounter and local work arrangements; no fixed historical price or customary entitlement asserted.

---

Dala had not sent for me.

Pawi was at the rack, holding a length of cord against the broken corner while she drew a knot tight with her teeth. When I came near, she took the cord from her mouth and looked at my hands.

"What have you brought?"

I showed her the fish wrapped in its leaf. I had carried it from the far bank with the salt drying against my skin.

"Food," she said.

"I thought you might want it."

"Did I ask you to buy fish?"

"I earned it carrying baskets."

"Then eat it. You will be hungry again when you come to help."

She turned the frame so that I could see the split. The sound cord had been saved, but there was not enough of it to bind the corner where the wood had given way. The loose ends made a little brush against her palm.

"Can I hold that?"

"When I have cut the bad part away."

She worked the joint loose. Pawi braced the other side with his knee. I waited for her to tell me where to put my hands. She did not.

"You came before I called," she said.

"I saw the rack from the path."

"You have seen it every day."

I put the fish back inside my cloth. The leaf had begun to soften. I was afraid it would leak onto the only dry part of my shirt.

A boat came in above the new step, close enough to show the scrape marks on its bow. Its steersman waited until the narrow vessel at the upper post had cleared its line. I recognized the man at the forward thwart only when he stood to take a bundle from beneath the seat.

Sulad.

He had tied his hair back with a strip I did not know. His feet were pale where the wet cord of his sandals had rubbed them. He passed the bundle to another crewman before stepping onto the old upper path. He did not use the repaired step until the owner told him the load was light enough.

I started toward him.

"Not with my frame," Dala said.

I had lifted one hand to wave. She had thought I meant to take the wood from her.

"I'll come back."

"When I send," she said.

Sulad saw me from the path. He did not smile until the crewman behind him had carried the bundle clear of the water. Then he came down with a short length of thick line over his shoulder. One end was wound around a wooden toggle. The other had been spliced back into itself, leaving a swollen place that would not pass through a narrow ring.

"You're here," I said.

"I was hired to come here."

"By whom?"

"The owner of that boat."

I looked at the line.

"Is that your pay?"

"My pay is not a rope."

He shifted it so that the wet part lay against his back rather than mine. A man called from the vessel. Sulad turned and answered that he had the line.

"Will you stay?" I asked.

"Until the owner takes his cargo onward. I have to return this first."

He had been gone while Raya repaired the step. I wanted to tell him how we had made the bank hold. I wanted him to see the new timber and the keeper's marks, and to know that I had done more than wait for someone to let me ashore.

"Did you hear about the step?"

"I saw it when we came in."

"Raya says it will hold a small load."

"Then let her say what it holds."

He started up the path. I walked beside him. The line brushed his heel with each step, leaving a wet mark in the dust.

"I went across yesterday," I told him.

"To work?"

"To ask after someone."

"Did you find him?"

"No. Sera may ask a buyer if he comes."

"Who?"

"Mulaw."

Sulad stopped beside a post where the shade from the hall crossed the path. He moved the line from one shoulder to the other.

"The one with the cloth?"

"The one who led the boat. Nima was guarding the cloth."

"I remember."

"He may remember us too."

"He may."

"I helped them at the landing."

Sulad looked toward the river. The crewman he had answered was carrying two small jars up the bank, one at a time. Sulad waited until the second jar was set down before speaking.

"You paid a man to hold their bow," he said.

"Yes."

"That is what he did."

"He kept the cloth from being soaked."

"Did he?"

"The boat stopped striking the wash."

"For a little while. The cloth stayed aboard."

"I was there."

"So was I."

I remembered the silver in the man's hand, shining wet after he came out of the water. I had wanted to take it back because the cloth had not come ashore. He had held the boat for the time I paid him to hold it. That had been the whole bargain.

"Mulaw saw what I did," I said.

"He saw you hire someone. He also saw you tell Nima to bring his sister's cloth onto a fisherman's ground."

"I was trying to find a dry place."

"You hadn't asked the fisherman."

"I asked after."

"After Nima refused."

Sulad put his hand beneath the line to keep it from sliding. He did not speak as though he had been saving the memory to use against me. He was telling me which part of it had happened.

"I thought I had done him a favor," I said.

"You may have. I don't know what he thought. But the man with the pole was paid. The fisherman kept his ground. Nima kept the cloth aboard. You cannot make the cloth dry by remembering it that way."

I had repeated the story to myself during the crossing to Sera's market. A silver piece, a steady bow, a young boat leader who would remember my help. I had left out the fisherman's foot planted beside his net. I had left out Nima's question about who would answer to his sister if the bundle spoiled.

"Did Mulaw thank me?" I asked.

"I don't remember."

"Neither do I."

The crewman came back down the path and pointed to the line.

"The owner wants it on the stern before the tide turns."

"I am bringing it," Sulad said.

I reached for the end that hung near his hand.

"Let me carry some."

"You can carry the loose end if you don't drag it."

I took it. The cord was rough and swollen with water. A broken fiber caught against the skin at the base of my thumb.

We went to the vessel together. Its owner stood with one foot on the old post's platform, watching the space between the hull and the bank. He had a broad scar across one knuckle. When he saw me holding the line, he looked first at Sulad.

"Who is that?"

"He is carrying the end," Sulad said. "I have not hired him."

The owner pointed to the stern.

"Lay it there. Keep the toggle out of the water."

I did as he asked. The toggle struck the board and made a hollow sound. The owner lifted it, checked the worn hole, and put it into a basket with two other pieces of wood.

"That has to be changed," he told Sulad.

"I saw the wear when we brought it in."

"Did you tell the man who tied it?"

"I told him to use the other."

"Good. We will not take a loaded boat out on that one."

He moved away to speak with the steersman. I stood with my hands empty.

Dala's frame was visible downriver, held upright by Pawi. I could see the pale line of the broken corner. The boat's line had looked sound enough to bind it, thicker than anything she had saved.

"Would the owner spare a length?" I asked Sulad.

"For what?"

"Dala needs cord."

"He needs this line."

"The end is worn."

"The toggle is worn. The line is still his."

"Could I ask him to cut the bad part off?"

"You can ask. He can say no."

I looked at the basket with the wooden toggles. The owner had already begun discussing a repair with the steersman. If I asked while they were busy, perhaps he would give me the loose end rather than have to explain why he wanted it.

Sulad saw where I was looking.

"Don't tell Dala I have cord for her," he said.

"I haven't."

"Then don't start."

I had been about to send Pawi to fetch her. I could feel the words still waiting in my mouth.

"The line is here," I said.

"It is here because I brought it back. I was paid to bring it back."

"Would you lend your share?"

"My share is the food and the cloth the owner agreed to give me. It is not the boat's gear."

I looked at his shoulder where the wet line had darkened his shirt. He had done the work. I had seen the line and counted it as something he could give away.

The owner returned and found us beside the stern.

"Is there a problem?"

"No," Sulad said.

I could have let him answer for both of us. The owner waited.

"Someone at our landing has a broken fishing frame," I said. "I wanted to ask whether you have any cord you would sell."

"Not that line."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

He pointed at the splice.

"That holds a boat when the water pulls. I cannot shorten it because another man sees a loose end. If you want small cord, ask the basket maker beyond the hall. He bought a coil from us last season. I don't know what he has left."

"Would he take fish?"

"Ask him."

The owner went back to his work. Sulad bent to check the knot securing the line in its basket. He did not need me to hold anything.

"You could have told me where to buy cord," I said.

"I could. You had not asked where."

"Do you think the basket maker has anything that will serve?"

"You can take Dala's old cord and find out."

I looked back at the boat. I had been asking other people to tell me what a man I had not seen in years might do. Sulad was standing beside me, and I had nearly done it again.

"You were with me at that first landing," I said. "I remembered him differently."

"You keep telling me he saw the silver. You leave out what happened to the cloth."

"Perhaps."

"Do not put my name beside his as a witness to a promise. I heard no promise."

He said it plainly. I had been considering whether to ask the keeper to mark Mulaw's name as a contact from my first voyage. If Sulad had agreed that the young man owed me a favor, I might have asked her to write more than she could prove.

"I won't," I said.

Sulad took a small packet from beneath the thwart. It was wrapped in dry leaves and tied with a strip of palm fiber.

"Mine," he said when he saw me look at it. "Food from the owner. I have already been paid for the first part of the journey."

"Will you eat with me?"

"After I return the other basket."

He stepped aboard to fetch it. I waited on the path. I could have told him to hurry; instead I watched him choose where to place his feet as the boat shifted beneath him.

When he came back, I showed him the fish I had earned from Sera.

"That is mine," I said.

"Then you have something to eat."

"I thought I might give it to Dala."

"Did she ask?"

"No."

"You have seen the broken corner."

"She needs cord."

"And a frame that will hold it."

He went up the path with his packet. I followed, carrying nothing for him this time.

The basket maker's place was beyond the hall, where the ground rose and the path narrowed between two walls. Strips of bamboo lay in a shallow trough, held beneath water by stones. A woman sat splitting one of them with a short blade. She stopped when she saw me looking at a coil hanging from the roof.

"That is not for nets," she said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were looking at it."

I told her about the broken frame and the cord Dala had saved. She asked how thick the old binding had been, whether it would stay wet, and how much of it had been cut away. I could answer only the last question, and even then I showed the length with my hands rather than naming it.

"Bring me a piece of the old cord," she said. "I will tell you what I can make or trade. Do not buy the wrong thing and tell the fisherwoman it is a gift."

"Would you take a fish?"

"For what? We haven't agreed on anything."

I showed her the leaf bundle. She shook her head.

"Eat it before it spoils. Come back when you know what you want."

On the way down I found Sulad sitting under the eaves with his food. He had left space beside him, but he had not called me. I sat and opened my leaf. The fish was smaller than I remembered when Sera gave it to me. I divided it carefully, putting the bones on the leaf where I could see them.

Sulad ate without asking for any. When he finished, he stood and wiped his fingers.

"I go with the owner again after the tide," he said.

"Will you come back?"

"If the work brings me back."

I recognized the words, though Mulaw had said them years earlier about his own boat. Sulad had been filling our jar when Mulaw spoke. I could not say whether he had heard. The words belonged to his own work now.

"Then I will ask when you return," I said.

"Ask me what?"

"Whether you have time to eat."

He nodded and went down to the boat.

Dala was still at the rack when I came back. I did not tell her that a cord had been offered, or that a buyer had been found, or that a man I once knew might come to Maynila.

"The woman above the hall asked to see a piece of the old binding," I said. "She will tell us whether she has anything that will serve."

Dala held out the frayed end she had cut away.

"Take this. Bring it back if she cannot use it."

"Will you come to choose?"

"When this side holds. Bring the sample back first."

I took the cord. It was damp and rough, with the knot still holding a splinter from the broken frame. I carried it between two fingers so the fibers would not catch on my cloth.

When I reached the basket maker's trough, she was still splitting bamboo. She saw the wet cord in my hand, set down her blade, and held out her palm.
