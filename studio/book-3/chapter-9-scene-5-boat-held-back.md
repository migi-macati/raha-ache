# Scene 5 — The Boat Held Back

I found Amaya beside her jars. She had spread the old cloth measure on a dry mat and was comparing it with the smaller covering she used when the weather changed. The patched square lay between her hands.

I held out the rice Tesa had paid me.

"She gave me this for carrying one bundle. I took it to the buyer, waited while she measured it, and brought her answer back."

Amaya looked at the leaf.

"Did Tesa say it was toward my cloth?"

"She said it was mine for the work. She said you must decide whether to take it toward what I owe."

"Then I will take it."

She untied the leaf, poured the grain into a small bowl, and set the empty wrapping beside the jars. She did not call the debt smaller. I had brought her something she could use that evening. It did not cover the jars she still had to protect.

"I will tell Tesa you took it," I said.

"Tell her if you see her. Do not spend another morning carrying that news when you have work to do."

I could hear the river beyond the houses. Someone was calling for a pole. Amaya folded the old measure.

"Are you going down again?"

"Only to see whether Raya has found Madan."

"Then go. If you promise her your hands, keep them there."

I went by the market path. The calls grew louder as I descended. At the landing a boat lay a short distance from the stones, held against the current by a pole at its stern. Its bow rose and fell where the water met the lower bank. Three baskets sat on the mud above the water, each covered with damp leaves. Sela stood beside them, arguing with a man in a short wet garment. Hiran held the pole in the boat. I recognized her from the meeting at the hall.

"The buyer is waiting," Sela said.

"The buyer is on land," the man answered. "My boat is in the water. I told you I would bring it here if there was a place to put the baskets."

"I was told the crossing had been marked."

"By whom?"

Sela turned when she saw me. She did not look pleased.

"Ask him," she said to the man. "He was at the meeting."

The man looked from me to the blocked upper step. He had a narrow scar above one eyebrow and a piece of cord tied around his wrist.

"Is that your landing?"

"I live on this bank. The step belongs to Raya's work, and she has closed it."

"Then it is closed."

"Yes."

"Why did the cord owner tell me there was a mark?"

"There are marks for one crossing and for a correction about it. There is no mark opening the step."

Sela lifted the covering on one basket. Inside were fish packed with leaves, still cool from the shade where she had bought them. These had not been dried. The buyer at the upper market had agreed to take them while they were fresh. She had paid for two baskets; the third belonged to a woman who had asked her to carry it on commission.

"The owner of that basket is waiting for her price," Sela said. "If the fish turn, she will lose more than I do."

The man in the wet garment looked at the baskets, then at the water.

"My name is Laman," he said. "Sela hired my boat to bring them from the lower bend to this bank. I said I would take them if there was a safe place to land and a man to lift them. I have brought the boat. There is no safe place. I will not put my bow against those stones."

"Could you take them farther up?" I asked.

"Where?"

I pointed to a narrow strip beyond the bend. He looked at it for a long moment.

"I have not sounded it. I will not bring a loaded boat into mud I do not know because you can see a patch of ground from here."

Hiran shifted her pole. The boat moved an arm's length downstream before she steadied it.

"I was hired to hold and pole this boat to the agreed landing," she said. "I have done that. If Laman wants another channel, he must ask whether I will work the farther journey. He cannot promise my hands because he owns the hull."

"I have not promised them," Laman said.

"You were about to ask him where to take us."

"I asked where there was ground."

"Then ask me before you decide what my arms will do."

Sela looked toward the upper path. A boy was coming down with an empty tray, sent by the buyer to see whether the fish had arrived. He stopped when he saw the boat still in the water.

"She says she will wait until the sun is above the palm," he told Sela. "Then she will buy from the next woman."

Sela covered the basket again.

"Can the fish go by the other channel?" she asked Laman.

"They can. It is farther from her house. I will need another payment for the time and water."

Hiran planted the pole in the mud and held it still.

"And you will need to ask me whether I take that journey. I have work after this tide."

"Will you?" Sela asked.

"I do not know the price yet."

"Then tell me."

Hiran named an amount of grain. Sela repeated it as if she had not heard correctly.

"That is nearly what I will earn on the two baskets."

"Then you may choose another way. I am telling you what it costs me to miss the work I have already been offered."

Laman did not lower his price. He was holding a boat in moving water, and the rope around his wrist was dark with spray. Sela had expected the upper step to take the baskets before the buyer's patience ended. She had hired two people to make a journey that could not finish where she wanted it to finish. None of them could get back the morning.

"Could we carry the baskets up the old stones?" I asked.

Raya answered from behind me.

"No."

She had come down carrying the short piece of timber across one shoulder. She lowered it on the firm bank and pointed to the lower stone.

"That stone turned when I put my weight on it. A man with an empty hand might step there and get away with it. A man carrying fish cannot see where his foot goes. I will not say it is safe."

"I was not asking you to say it was safe," I told her.

"You were asking whether you could do it. You asked where I could hear."

Sela pressed the leaf covering flat. She had stopped arguing with Laman. She was watching the boy who had brought the buyer's message.

"Could one basket reach her by the upper market path?" she asked.

"From here?" I said.

"From the place where we stand. On land. Without using the step."

"Yes, if someone carries it."

"How long?"

I knew the path from Tesa's awning. I had just walked it. I could carry one basket without putting it down, but the fish were heavier than the cloth and the path rose sharply beyond the jars.

"Longer than the boy's walk," I said. "I would have to stop at the turning to change my grip."

"Can you do it before the sun reaches the palm?"

"I think so."

"Thinking is not carrying."

I looked at the basket. I had made the same mistake with Madan's timber, offering hands without asking who would pay the other men. I could lift one basket. I could not carry three at once, or give the buyer more time than she had offered.

"I can carry one," I said. "If you want me to. I will not promise the other two."

Sela asked the boy which basket the buyer wanted most. He said the smallest fish, if they were still firm. Sela opened the second basket and showed him the catch. He touched one fish with a clean finger and nodded.

"Take that one," she said. "I will pay you from the sale if she accepts it. If she refuses it, I will give you a small measure for the carrying. I cannot pay the full price for a load that earns me nothing."

"I accept."

Laman looked at the basket.

"That is part of the cargo I agreed to carry," he said.

"Have you been paid for this part of the journey?" Sela asked.

"I have been paid to bring it here."

"Then it is here. I am choosing how it goes the rest of the way."

He looked at Hiran. She was still holding the pole. Neither of them spoke for a moment.

"I will take the other baskets back to the lower bend," Laman said at last. "That return is within what I agreed with Sela. After that, she must find another boat or another buyer."

Hiran nodded. "I will pole the return to the lower bend. I will not go to the other channel for the same pay."

Sela accepted the return. The third basket's owner would have to hear that her fish had not reached the upper market. Sela would tell her herself.

I lifted the chosen basket. Its weight settled against my shoulder, and the binding pulled across my palm. The boy went ahead to warn the buyer. I climbed the path with the leaves brushing my cheek. Twice I stopped where the ground was firm, keeping the basket above the mud while I changed hands.

When I reached the buyer's house, the sun had touched the lower leaves of the palm but had not passed its crown. The woman looked at the fish before she looked at me. She opened the basket, pressed the flesh near the gills, and held one to her nose.

"I will take this," she said. "Only this. I have already bought the rest."

She measured out Sela's price, then counted a smaller amount into a separate leaf for my carrying. I told her Sela had said she would pay me after the sale.

"Then take this to Sela," the buyer said, pointing to the larger measure. "She will decide your wage. I am paying for fish."

I took both measures back without mixing them. The smaller leaf was not mine yet.

The boat had gone when I returned. Sela was waiting beside the wet patch where the baskets had stood. Raya was farther down the bank, examining the lower stone again. The cord owner had arrived and was speaking with her. She had put the short timber between them as though it were the only answer she wished to give.

Sela counted the buyer's payment twice. She took out the carrying amount she had promised me and gave it back in the smaller leaf.

"You did what you said," she told me. "One basket reached the buyer. Two did not. The woman who owned the third will ask why. I will tell her."

"Will you bring fish here again?"

"I may bring fish to this bank when I know where to put them. I will not hire Laman on the strength of another man's mark."

The cord owner heard her. He came toward us with his measuring line wound tightly against his arm.

"I told the owner the keeper had recorded a crossing," he said. "I did not tell him Raya had finished the step."

"You told him there was a mark," Sela answered. "He heard a place to land."

"Then he heard too much."

"Or you said too little."

He looked at me, perhaps expecting me to defend the distinction.

"The keeper will hear what each of you says," I told him. "She cannot give Sela back her buyer, or Hiran the work she has missed."

Hiran had already gone with Laman. I could not ask her what she had lost by the time the boat reached the lower bend. I could say what I had heard: she had refused the farther channel without new pay, and Laman had refused to use the unsafe stones. Sela had chosen one basket to carry by land. None of those decisions belonged to me.

Raya called to the cord owner.

"If you want a step, bring sound wood and ask me what it will cost. Do not bring another boat to test the rotten one."

He gathered his line and went to her. Sela took her empty basket up the market path. I was left with a small leaf of grain in my hand and the knowledge that the buyer had accepted only one of three baskets.

I had earned that grain by carrying. The landing had earned nothing. Tomorrow the woman who owned the third basket would have a question for Sela, and Sela would have an answer that included my name. I could not make it a better answer by telling her the truth had been preserved in the hall.
