# Book II, Chapter 1 — Scene 3: The Empty Step

The two baskets were still in the boat when the owner told his men to take them away.

The man at the stern looked toward the platform. He had his pole in the water, holding the hull clear of the post. The young carrier stood above him, empty-handed and unpaid.

“Where?” the man at the stern asked.

“Down to the other landing.”

“There are boats there.”

“Then wait your turn.”

The woman who watched our receiving platform put one foot against the first basket. Its covering lay open. The cracked bowl rested on a fold of cloth beside it, where the owner had left it.

“What of this one?” she asked.

“Put it back.”

“Whose hands will put it back?”

The owner looked at the young carrier. The carrier did not move.

“I have done my carrying,” he said.

“You have done damage.”

“You have not paid me.”

The man at the stern pushed against the post. The boat shifted a little and came back. Behind it another hull was waiting to reach the steps, its bow held off by a boy with a paddle. He called to ask whether we were finished. No one answered him.

The older cord keeper rose from his place beside Daran. He had made a knot when the first basket reached the platform. He had not made another.

“If you take it back,” he told the owner, “I will say that you took it back.”

“And the bowl?”

“We found it cracked when the covering was opened here.”

“Because he struck the basket.”

The old man looked at me. I wished he had not.

“I heard the basket put down,” I said.

“You have said that,” the owner answered.

He was right. I had said it so often that I could hear myself becoming tiresome. The carrier's wages had not moved any nearer to his hand.

Daran brought the empty baskets away from the steps. He had to turn sideways to pass the owner. The owner did not make room for him until Daran stopped and waited.

“Open the next basket here,” the woman said.

The owner shook his head. “You have already found a reason to keep one.”

“We have found a broken bowl. I would rather find the others whole while the men who packed them can see.”

The man with the scar, who had tied the covering, called from the boat. “I can bring it.”

The young carrier turned toward him.

“You can carry that one,” he said. “I will not.”

The man with the scar put a hand on the second basket's handle. Another man joined him. They brought it up together, slowly enough that the boy with the waiting boat began to complain again. They set it beside the first. The owner watched their hands until the weight was on the planks.

The woman did not touch the covering. She told the packer to untie it himself.

He loosened the cord and lifted the mat. Dry leaves lay between the bowls, curled into small brown pockets. The top two were whole. So were the next three. The packer took them out one at a time and laid them in a row.

The owner folded his arms.

“Now you have seen how they were packed.”

The woman took a leaf from the bottom of the basket. It had darkened along one edge. She rubbed it between her fingers, then let it fall.

“Was this wet when you packed it?”

The man with the scar leaned close. “I don't remember.”

“Did water come into the boat?”

“Water comes into every boat.”

The owner gave a short laugh. “Now we are to blame the water for a broken bowl.”

I looked from the damp leaf to the cracked rim on the cloth. I wanted the leaf to mean something. Perhaps the first basket had been soaked. Perhaps its padding had slipped before the young man lifted it. I had not seen the leaves in that basket before the owner pulled them aside. A wet leaf would not tell me when a bowl had broken.

Daran came to stand beside me.

“You are looking for a mark,” he said.

“There might be one.”

“There are many marks on things that have traveled.”

He went back to his cord.

The man at the stern called that he could no longer keep the boat in place without blocking the one behind. The owner told him to move farther out. The second basket stood open now, its bowls spread across the platform. The man with the scar looked at his hands, as if he had only just understood how long he might be needed.

The owner turned to the young carrier.

“You see what your refusal has done? We are still here.”

The carrier looked toward the bowls. “I did not refuse to carry the first.”

“Then finish your work.”

“My payment.”

The owner drew the same small piece of silver from his fold. He held it between two fingers.

The young man glanced at it and looked away.

“You told me more before we came ashore.”

“You had not broken anything before we came ashore.”

The man with the scar stopped lifting bowls. I saw the carrier notice. It mattered to him that someone else had heard the words.

I moved toward the owner, though I had no idea what I could ask him to do. I could have offered my own food. It would not have paid the promised wage, and the man might have taken it as payment for my silence. I could have called on Siripada's name. I had no permission to make him answer for a stranger's basket.

A wet mat brushed my ankle.

“Move your foot,” Sulad said.

He and another man were carrying the mat between them. They had to cross the platform to reach the space behind the receiving house. I stepped aside. The other man went ahead, pulling more of the weight than Sulad could comfortably hold.

Sulad set his end down once they were clear.

“That boat has taken our place at the steps,” he said, nodding toward the hull. “We have another mat to bring in.”

“There is a dispute.”

“I can see the dispute.”

“The carrier has not been paid.”

Sulad looked at the young man. “Will you pay him?”

“I cannot.”

“Then don't make him wait to hear you say it.”

I felt my face grow warm. “I was trying to understand what happened.”

“He knows what happened to his payment.”

The man who worked with Sulad called for him. Sulad picked up his end of the mat and went. He did not stay to tell me what I ought to do. I watched him carry the weight through the shade, keeping his side level with the other man's.

The woman told the packer to return the sound bowls to their places. He began to nest them among the leaves. No one asked the young carrier to help.

The owner stood close enough to hear the waiting boatman's complaints. He could take his goods elsewhere, but the cracked bowl would travel with him. The carrier could leave, but his payment would remain in the owner's fold. The receiving house could refuse to name the cause, but our steps would still be blocked.

The owner turned to the older cord keeper.

“I want the count to show that one of my bowls was broken here.”

“It was found broken here.”

“Are you playing with words?”

“No.”

“Then put it in the count.”

The old man held up the cord. “One basket brought up. One cracked bowl found when it was opened. That is what the knot holds.”

“And who pays for it?”

“I cannot put a man into a knot.”

The owner looked as if he might strike the cord from his hand. Instead he reached for the cracked bowl. The loose piece of rim fell away. It hit the platform with a sound smaller than I expected.

Everyone looked down.

The owner bent to retrieve it. His clean shoulder cloth touched the planks.

“Now it is in two pieces,” he said.

No one answered. The bowl had been cracked before he lifted it. I had watched him lift it. I could not tell whether that made the loss greater or merely easier to see.

The woman crouched and gathered the small piece in her palm. She set it inside the bowl.

“If you want it back in your basket,” she said, “put it there yourself. If you want us to hold it until you decide, say so while these men are here.”

The owner looked at the two baskets. His boat had drifted away from the steps. The man at the stern was holding it with difficulty. The waiting boy had brought his own boat nearer.

“Hold the first,” the owner said at last. “I will take the second.”

“For how long?”

“Until I return.”

“That is no answer.”

“Until the next tide,” he said.

The woman looked at the older cord keeper. He nodded once. She turned to the owner.

“Then let everyone hear it. The first basket stays here until the next tide. Its cracked bowl stays with it. You take the second. Nothing has been decided about the cause.”

The owner did not repeat her words. He called for the second basket to be covered and carried back. The man with the scar tied it. The young carrier stood where he had stood before.

As the men lifted the basket, the owner spoke to him without turning.

“You will have your payment when I know what this has cost me.”

The young man answered so quietly that I almost missed it.

“Then I will be here when you know.”

The owner went down to the boat. He passed close enough to me that I could have stopped him with a hand. I did not. He looked at me once, and I understood that the man who might know Maynila had become less likely to speak to me. I could not even say that my refusal had bought the carrier anything.

When the boat finally cleared the steps, the waiting boy came in at once. His hull struck the post. He swore, pushed away, and tried again.

Daran handed me a length of cord.

“Stay with that basket,” he said. “If the owner returns, bring the woman before anything is moved.”

“Is that all?”

“It is a basket he has left in our keeping. Do you want to lose it?”

“No.”

“Then keep it.”

I sat beside the open covering. The young carrier sat a little farther away, where he could see the water. Neither of us had been given what he wanted. I knew less about the broken bowl than I had hoped to know, but I knew exactly where the basket was.

The next boat came alongside. Men began to lift its goods past us. I moved my knees out of their way and kept the cord in my hand.
