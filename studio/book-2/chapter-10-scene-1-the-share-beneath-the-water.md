# Book II, Chapter 10 — Scene 1: The Share Beneath the Water

**Packet:** RA-B2C10-SCENE-01. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows published Book II Chapter 9 and the proposed scoped Sulad/Laila retrospective bridges in PR #65. The paid inquiry boat remains out; Nasir is not confirmed alive or dead. Jamil permits Ache to carry questions, not command boats. Sulad's return does not constitute agreement to sail for Luzon. **Historical status:** Fictional scene and characters' private dialogue, not attested testimony.

---

Nasir's sister brought me a board with three names on it. She had written none of them herself. She had made the clerk write them again after he read the first copy aloud.

"The first man was seen on the bank," she said. "The second was taken aboard the foreign ship. The third was last seen in a boat. You have put them together."

"The headings are different."

"Then read them to me without looking at the headings."

I did. I had learned the cuts well enough to say which name belonged beneath which mark. When I came to Nasir, I stopped. The space after his name was still empty.

"Good," she said. "Leave it that way until somebody brings me a reason to change it."

Below the steps the tide had uncovered the mud around the posts. Small crabs came out of the holes and withdrew whenever a foot struck the planks. Tahir was counting the boats that had returned from the night fishing. The one Hasan had paid to go toward the traps was not among them.

"Should we send another?" I asked.

"With whose boat?" she said.

"I could ask."

"Ask the first owner whether he has finished the journey I paid for. If he has not returned, another boat may only bring back the same empty water."

I had come to help her with the names. I was already trying to give her a voyage.

A loaded timber boat touched the lower landing while we were speaking. The steersman called for someone to catch his line. A boy reached for it, missed, and nearly slid between the hull and the post. A man in the bow caught him by the back of his tunic. The boy climbed out laughing until the man told him how close his head had come to the wood.

The timber was bound in short rafts along the boat's sides. Its cut ends were marked with charcoal. One bundle had slipped below the surface and was dragging against the mud. Two men climbed onto the landing to find the owner who had agreed to pay them.

The third man was Sulad.

I knew him by the way he stepped from a moving boat. He waited until the hull lifted toward the planks, put one foot down, and brought his weight over it without catching the rail. I had watched him do it in Maynila. I had forgotten that I knew.

He saw me before I called his name.

"You are still here," he said.

"So are you."

"I came in this morning."

"From the work below the bend?"

"From the work beyond it. We moved twice when the bank gave way."

He looked past me to the woman with the board. She held it against her chest. Sulad gave her room to pass and watched until she reached Tahir. He did not ask who she was.

"I went looking for you," I said.

"I heard. The boy brought your question."

"He said you would speak when the work was counted."

"It has not been counted yet."

He turned toward the timber boat. The owner had arrived with two assistants and a measuring cord. He was pointing at the bundle beneath the water.

"Seven," the owner said. "I paid for eight lengths."

"Eight were cut," the steersman answered. "One was taken off at the upper landing."

"By whom?"

"Your men."

"Then show me where they put it."

Sulad went down to the mud. He pulled the submerged bundle clear enough to see the charcoal mark and called for the boy to bring a pole. The owner said he would count only the wood standing above the water. Sulad looked up at him.

"Then you will count less each time the tide rises."

The owner's assistants laughed. The owner did not.

I moved closer. Sulad had a narrow strip of wood tied to his belt, with cuts for the bundles brought from the work site. I recognized the marks. Hasan had shown me how to keep a delivery separate from a promise. I wanted to take the strip and explain the account to the owner.

Sulad held it out to the steersman instead.

"Read it," he said.

The steersman named eight bundles. He pointed to the seventh mark and said that one load had been taken from them above the bend by a man in the owner's employ. The owner denied that his man had accepted it. The steersman named the man and the landing.

"He is not here," the owner said.

"Then send for him," Sulad answered.

"You expect me to wait while my wood lies in the water?"

"We waited while your bank was too soft to stand on."

The owner looked at the measuring cord. He told his assistants to lift the bundles before they swelled any further. They did not move. They were waiting to see whether the men from the boat would be paid first.

I could have called Hasan. I could have offered to settle the count. The owner knew me as Jamil's troublesome young kinsman, and a word from me might have made him pay simply to end the quarrel. I was already thinking how useful that would be. Sulad would have his share and I would have done something for him after so long apart.

Instead I asked the steersman whether he had kept the cord from the upper landing.

He looked at me. "What cord?"

"The one used to mark the bundle they took off. You said the man accepted it."

"He cut his own length from the binding."

"Then there may be a piece still on the boat."

The boy who had nearly fallen into the water crouched by the stern. He drew a short cord from beneath a mat. One end had been cut clean. The other still held the knot by which the bundle had been fastened.

"That proves a cord was cut," the owner said.

"Yes," I answered.

He looked disappointed. He had expected me to say it proved his man had taken the timber.

"Can you tell whether it was cut above the bend?" I asked the steersman.

"I can tell you who cut it. He can tell you why."

The owner sent one of his assistants to the upper landing. The man went reluctantly, looking back at the timber in the water. We waited. The tide began to lift the submerged bundle, and the steersman pulled it against the side so it would not strike the planks.

Sulad took a seat on the lower step. I sat two boards above him. We had crossed a sea together. Now I did not know whether I was welcome beside him.

"Did you hear what happened at Laoe?" I asked.

"Men coming downriver told us you had taken houses."

"I was there."

"I know."

"We went to settle a claim. There was fighting. Some of the houses burned."

"Who lived in them?"

I could name Mara and the child she had carried. I could name the man who had been killed in the lane. I did not know how many people had lost their roofs. I told him what I knew. I did not tell him the attack had been necessary.

"And after that?" he asked.

"The foreign ships came."

"I heard you were taken."

"I was."

He touched the end of the strip tied to his belt.

"One man said you were dead. Another said the ruler had paid to bring you back. They both spoke as though they had stood beside you."

"Neither had."

"Then you know how the words travel."

I looked at the empty stretch beyond the traps. The inquiry boat had not appeared.

"Hasan is keeping the accounts of those who came back," I said. "I have been carrying questions for him."

"That is work."

"It is."

"Do you like it?"

I almost told him that I had been trusted with larger work before the foreign ships. He knew that already. He had heard enough men explain what I had once been allowed to do.

"I am better at it than when we came," I said.

"Then you have learned something."

The assistant returned with a thin man who had mud on his calves. He was the owner's receiver at the upper landing. He said he had taken one bundle and put it beneath a shed because the bank was too low to hold the whole load.

"Did you tell them it was received?" the owner asked.

"I told the steersman I had it."

"Did you mark it?"

"I was going to when the rest came."

The owner stared at him. He had spent half the morning disputing a bundle his own man had put away.

"Then count it," Sulad said.

The owner took the strip from the steersman, called for his measuring cord, and counted the seven bundles at the lower landing. He sent his receiver back to measure the eighth. He would pay for eight if it matched the others. The steersman demanded payment for the time lost while they waited. The owner refused that part. They argued over the number of hands needed to lift the wet timber, and in the end agreed that the owner would supply two men from his yard.

Sulad did not thank me. I was glad he did not. I had asked a question that could be answered. He and the others had kept the bargain from being rewritten.

When the owner went to fetch the payment, Sulad stood.

"You wanted my words before the witnesses," he said.

"Yes."

"Which words?"

"What happened before we left Maynila. What you saw. What you agreed to."

"I will tell Hasan what I saw. I will not have you put my name beside a promise I did not make."

"I have not asked you to promise anything."

"You asked me to follow you once."

I remembered how easily I had spoken for him when we were younger. We had left home with different reasons and I had been able to pretend they were one.

"Will you come back to Luzon?" I asked.

"Is there a boat?"

"No."

"Is there a crew?"

"No."

"Then you are asking me to follow a question."

He looked toward the owner counting out the first payment. The steersman was watching every piece.

"I will go to Hasan after I have my share," Sulad said. "I will tell him what belongs to me. If you find a passage, tell me its terms. I will decide then."

"Do you want to know what happened at home?"

"Of course."

"And if I go?"

"Going is not the same as bringing back the place we left."

He went to receive his pay. I stayed on the steps until the owner had counted it twice and Sulad had taken it into his hand.

Nasir's sister returned from Tahir's board. She had seen Sulad speaking with me.

"Did the boat from the traps come in?" she asked.

"No."

"Then keep his name where it is."

She meant Nasir. I knew she meant Nasir. I had been thinking about Sulad, about whether he would sail with me, and for a moment I had forgotten which name she had come to ask about.

I took her board and read the three headings again. There was still an empty space after her brother's name. She listened while I left it empty.
