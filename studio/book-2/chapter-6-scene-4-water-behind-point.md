# Book II, Chapter 6 — Scene 4: The Water Behind the Point

**Studio draft.** Ache POV. Follows Scene 3, *The Men Who Owned the Shore*. Expedition, local guide, and shore encounter are fictional reconstructions; no specific Loue route or dated campaign choreography is attested. Not published.

---

Ruma came to see the boats before she came to see Jamil.

She walked along the landing with Tahir beside her, looking into the hulls that had been drawn together for the voyage. A man asked whether she had come to carry the ruler's soldiers. She told him she had come to see whether anyone had put the water jars beneath the heavier goods.

He laughed. She did not.

When Jamil arrived, she named her price for carrying stores and a small party of men. She would not put fighters on her own vessel until they had told her where their weapons would lie. She would not leave her boat tied to another if the weather changed. She wanted a place to take her crew ashore if the embassy failed.

Jamil listened to each condition.

"Will you take the work?" he asked.

"If Tahir approves the load."

Tahir had been kneeling in the boat with his hand against a plank. He stood and said that two of the water jars must be moved forward and one basket left ashore. The owner of the basket protested. Tahir asked him to come aboard and stand where he expected the boat to sit when the tide fell. The man stepped in, felt the vessel shift, and climbed out again. He took the basket with him.

Ruma accepted the work.

I had imagined that the ruler's order would bring every boat to the landing. By the time we left, two owners had refused, one had offered a vessel without its crew, and another had agreed only to carry a messenger as far as the next settled shore. Jamil did not punish them. He had enough trouble with the owners who had said yes.

The guards carried spears and shields wrapped against the spray. They were fewer than Awan had wanted. Some belonged to households whose goods had been delayed; others were men Jamil trusted to hold a landing if the embassy needed a place to speak. None of them had been promised that Laoe would yield.

Awan watched from the upper steps. He had given the expedition food and expected the return of his cloth. He asked why Ruma's vessel was not at the front.

"Because she carries water," Jamil said.

"Water can follow."

"Only until someone needs it."

Awan looked at me, perhaps hoping I would offer him a faster arrangement. I was carrying a bundle of spare bindings to Ruma's boat. I passed him without speaking.

Dima had agreed to travel with us as far as the outer approach. He said he would tell us which landing his mother's household used and where strangers were likely to be challenged. He had refused to lead armed men directly to her door.

The court had accepted his condition. Awan had called it proof that he was hiding something.

Dima carried no timber now. He had left the broken post in the audience house. His hands were empty except for a small bag of food. I wondered whether he had brought enough to return if Ruma put us ashore.

At the last moment Nasir's sister appeared between two men carrying water. She had heard that I was leaving with Jamil. I set down my bundle.

"Have they found him?" she asked.

"No."

"Then why are you going?"

"To another shore. The ruler has sent men to ask about goods held there."

She looked at the weapons. "Those men are carrying questions?"

"They are meant to protect the ones who ask."

She watched a guard testing the edge of a spear against his thumb.

"Bring back the names of anyone who does not return," she said.

I had no answer that would make her look less tired.

"I will ask for them."

"Ask before you leave," she said. "It is harder afterward."

She went back up the landing. I stood holding the bindings until Tahir called for them.

We traveled by stages. At each stop the boat owners checked what they had used and what they still carried. I had been assigned to help keep Ruma's stores with the other supply vessel, whose owner was a broad-shouldered man called Banu. He had accepted Jamil's guards aboard but would not let them sit on his covered jars.

Safi traveled with the men who would go ashore to speak. Jamil moved between the vessels when conditions allowed. I was not told where to steer. I was told to make sure the stores did not become separated from the people who needed them.

The first time Banu moved ahead of Ruma, I called for him to wait. He did not hear me. I was about to wave a pole across the water when Tahir caught my arm.

"That is the signal for a boat to come alongside," he said.

"What should I do?"

"Tell Ruma what you see."

Ruma looked toward Banu's boat and said the space between us was safe while the water remained open. She would ask him to close it before we entered the next sheltered passage. I watched him moving farther away, but I held my pole down.

The next day the wind changed while we were under a low coast. Banu brought his boat close without being called. He said he had seen a squall building over the water behind us. Ruma looked at the same sky and asked Tahir to shorten the loose covering. We passed into shelter before the rain arrived.

I had thought Banu was the one who needed watching. He had been watching the weather.

Dima spoke little during the voyage. When men asked how many houses stood at Laoe, he told them he had not counted every house. When they asked how many fighting men it could raise, he said he knew only who had weapons near his mother's landing. A guard asked whether the people of Laoe feared the ruler of Burné. Dima asked which people he meant.

By the time we reached the last sheltered water before the disputed shore, the guards had stopped asking him questions.

I found him at the stern, looking at a line of trees above a narrow stretch of water.

"Is that where your mother lives?" I asked.

"Beyond it."

"Can we pass?"

"Not by the way Awan's men have described."

"Why?"

He pointed to the water. I saw only a dark line running across the surface. He made me wait until a small patch of foam moved toward it and broke apart.

"There is a hard place beneath," he said. "The water crosses it when the tide is high. The men who know it take their own boats through. They do not bring strangers with loaded vessels and armed men."

"Is there another way?"

"Yes."

"Will you show it?"

"I will show you where I stop."

He looked at the guards. One had taken off his covering and was drying it in the sun.

"I told your ruler what I would do," Dima said. "If you ask me to lead them to my mother's door, I will go ashore alone."

I wanted to tell him we had come to hear the quarrel. The guards had their weapons laid out where they could reach them. He could see that without my help.

Jamil came to the stern with Safi. They had been speaking to the owners about how far the vessels could go before the water turned.

Safi favored waiting in the outer shelter while a smaller boat carried the first message. He said the loaded boats should remain where they could leave without passing close to the hard ground. Jamil agreed that the message should go first, but he wanted the guards near enough to protect the messengers if they were pursued.

I watched the foam break over the hidden place again.

"If the guards go by that short water," I said, "the supply boats cannot follow them when the tide falls."

Safi turned toward me. "I did not ask them to go by it."

"No. Awan's men did."

"Then tell Awan's men to ask Dima."

Dima did not look pleased to have his name brought into the argument.

Jamil asked Ruma whether her boat could take the longer water and remain near enough to hear a call from shore. Ruma said she could do one or the other while the tide was falling, not both. Tahir agreed. Banu said he would not anchor on ground he had never seen.

The men argued over where the embassy could wait. The guard leader wanted the smaller boats close enough to rush a landing. Safi wanted room for them to turn away. Ruma wanted her water jars out of reach of anyone who might mistake them for a prize.

Jamil listened until everyone had said what he would refuse.

"Ruma and Banu will stay outside the narrow water," he said. "The speaking party will go in with two light boats. They will not land until Dima has shown them a place he is willing to approach."

Dima shook his head.

"I will show the outer marker," he said. "I will not choose the place where you put armed men ashore."

Jamil looked at him for a long moment.

"Then show us the marker."

I was given charge of carrying messages between the supply boats while they held position. Ruma would decide where her own vessel stayed. Banu would answer for his. If one had to move, I was to make sure the other knew before the distance became too great for a call.

It was a small authority. It was also the first time since Hamzah's boat had vanished that Jamil had asked me to carry a decision between two owners who might disagree.

Dima pointed out a low place on the shore where the water had worn a pale line across the rocks. He said his mother's household used it as a mark when coming from the outer sea. Beyond it the shore curved out of sight.

The smaller boats went forward. Safi rode in the first with two men who had been told to keep their weapons covered unless they needed them. Jamil stayed in the second. Dima sat beside the bowman, watching the water rather than the shore.

I could see people moving above the landing when the boats came near. A man stood with a long pole. Two others were carrying baskets away from the water. A woman drew a child behind a house. No one raised a greeting that I could understand from where I stood.

Ruma brought her boat to a place where the current ran steadily past the hull. Tahir tested the depth with his pole and told her they had room to swing. Banu stayed farther out. I moved between them in a small boat with one of Ruma's younger crew, a man who knew how to keep his vessel steady while I spoke.

"Tell Banu we will hold here," Ruma said.

We crossed the short stretch. Banu listened and said he would remain where he was until the tide changed. He would not follow if the guards went inland. I repeated his words to Ruma.

She nodded. "That is what he agreed."

From the shore came a shout. A man had walked down to the edge and was speaking to Dima. The words did not reach us. Dima stood in the bow of the first boat. He lifted both hands, empty. The man on shore pointed beyond the curve of land.

Safi's boat edged nearer. Jamil's followed at a distance. I saw the guard leader raise his arm, then lower it when Safi spoke to him.

The man on shore disappeared behind the houses.

"Are they letting us land?" I asked Ruma.

"We cannot hear."

I wanted to take our small boat closer. Ruma said no.

A second man came out carrying a bundle wrapped in cloth. He held it over his head. The first boat drifted toward him. Dima leaned forward. The man stopped at the water's edge and set the bundle down.

Then someone behind the houses shouted again. A cluster of people appeared on the raised path. One carried a spear. Another was pulling a boy by the arm. The boy was struggling to reach the bundle.

The man at the water turned and ran back up the path.

I could not tell whether he had been called away or driven off.

Safi stood. He called toward the shore. A stone struck the side of his boat. Another splashed beside it. One of his men reached for his spear, but Safi pushed the shaft down.

Jamil's boat began to turn. The guard leader shouted to follow him. The bowman tried to push away from the shallows. His pole caught beneath something and bent.

From our position I could see the bow shift sideways. The water was dropping. The first boat had come too close to the pale line of rocks.

"Can they get clear?" I asked Tahir.

"If they stop trying to turn against the current."

He spoke to Ruma, not to me. She called for the younger crewman to bring our small boat back alongside.

Banu had already seen the trouble. His boat moved closer, though he had said he would hold farther out. I thought he was coming to help. Ruma said he was moving to keep the guards in sight as the curve hid them. He had not agreed to enter the shallows.

I stood with one foot on the thwart, looking at Safi's boat. A third stone came from the shore. This one struck a guard on the shoulder. He dropped to his knees, and the man behind him caught his shield before it fell overboard.

Safi called to Jamil. Jamil shouted something back. I heard only the last word.

Tahir took the pole from my hands.

"Sit down," he said.

I sat. The smaller boat was coming back to us, and there was no room for my foot where it had been.

Ruma asked whether the guards had room to back away. Tahir watched the water around their sterns. He said one boat did. The other would need to be pushed off the hard ground while the water still covered it.

I could see Jamil looking toward us. He raised his arm and called for a boat to come, then pointed to the deeper water beside the stranded bow.

Ruma turned to me.

"Tell Banu we are sending the small boat to help the men push free," she said. "He must keep the outer passage open. Do not tell him to follow us."

I repeated the words to the younger crewman. He nodded, and we pushed away.

The water beneath us was clear enough to see dark shapes moving over the bottom. I watched the rocks come nearer. I could hear the people on shore shouting to one another. I could not tell whether they wanted us gone or wanted the boat held there until more men arrived.

When we reached Banu, he heard Ruma's message and gave us a coil of rope. He would not send his own boat inward.

"Take this," he said. "Bring it back if you can."

I held the coil between my knees as we turned toward the stranded boat. Safi had climbed into the water. It reached above his knees. He was trying to lift the bow while the men aboard pushed with their poles.

Dima stood in the boat with his hands on the gunwale. He was looking toward the houses, where someone had begun dragging the bundle away from the landing.

The guard who had been struck was still kneeling. Blood ran down his arm into the water.

I had come to this shore to carry messages. Now I had a rope in my lap, men trapped against stone, and a boat whose owner was waiting for me to return. I could see exactly where the rope needed to go. I could not yet see how to get it there without bringing another boat onto the same ground.
