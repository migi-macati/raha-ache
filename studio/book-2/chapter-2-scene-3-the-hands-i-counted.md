# Book II, Chapter 2 — Scene 3: The Hands I Counted

The remaining sacks came down the passage on two men's shoulders. The carrier who had been waiting on the steps stood to meet them, then stopped when he saw the man behind them carrying nothing.

“Where is the third?”

“Still in the yard,” one of the men said. “Its binding has come apart.”

“Then why did you tell me the rest was coming?”

“I said we had found them.”

The carrier looked toward the yard, where another man was calling for a length of cord. He had already waited long enough to miss the work he meant to take after this. I could see him deciding whether to follow the third sack or stay with the two that were here.

Jamil called us away from the board before the men tried to cross it. Safi had brought the repaired covering, folded so that its new edge was on top. He put it on a dry step and held one corner down with his foot.

The older boatman came to stand beside Jamil. The boy with the tally board stayed close enough to hear, but not close enough to be asked to lift anything.

“How much is still to go aboard?” Jamil asked.

The older man pointed out the sacks on the steps, the one in the yard, and the poles that lay along the landing. He named two bundles already under the covering which had to be moved before the poles could lie against the side.

“Who is taking the poles?” Jamil asked.

“The two who brought them.”

“And the covering?”

The man who had mended it raised his hand. He had a needle stuck through the fold of his cloth.

“I will fasten it when the long pieces are down. Not before.”

Jamil looked at Safi and me. “You have both been looking at this boat. Tell us what can be done without putting off the load already accepted.”

Safi spoke first. I was grateful for that. It gave me time to watch the men and think of an answer that sounded less like a request.

“Move the two bundles to the stern while the poles come in,” Safi said. “Keep the passage clear until the last sack is aboard. Then put the bundles back and fasten the covering. No one should stand waiting with a pole while a man is still crossing the board.”

The older boatman shook his head.

“The bundles cannot go all the way aft. The man steering will need that place.”

Safi glanced toward the stern. “Then as far as the next crosspiece.”

“That leaves enough,” the boatman said, though he looked at the men before he said it.

Safi nodded and continued. He had counted the two men with the poles and the man with the covering. He had not counted the carrier who was waiting for his pay.

I had been looking at the woman's baskets. The two marks on the boy's board were still apart from the others. If I could make them part of the load, perhaps she would stop looking at me as though I had taken something from her.

“When the bundles are moved,” I said, “the baskets could go beside them. The poles can be held up while they pass.”

The man with the needle laughed once, without much pleasure.

“Held up by whom?”

“The men bringing them.”

“They have to bring them,” he said. “Their hands will be on the poles.”

“Then someone could steady the far ends.”

I looked toward the carrier. He was standing beside the sacks, listening.

“You have been waiting here,” I said to him. “If you helped with the poles, it would be done sooner.”

“Would it?”

He looked at the older boatman, not at me.

“Who pays me for that?”

“Perhaps the boatman—”

“No,” the older man said. “I have paid for the sacks to be carried down. I have not hired him for the poles.”

“I can ask whether he will do it,” I said.

“You have asked,” the carrier answered.

He rubbed the strap mark across his shoulder. “I will bring the last sack if they mend it before I go. After that I have other work. I have lost enough of the day here.”

He had not raised his voice. That made the space after his answer harder to bear.

Jamil asked me to finish my proposal.

I could have said I had none. Instead I pointed to the place beside the covered bundles.

“Two baskets do not weigh as much as a sack. They are food. The men above the bend need them today. If we leave them here while we argue about one pair of hands, what is the use of getting the other goods away?”

The woman was still in the shade, too far off to hear every word. I could see her looking toward us. Her brother had not returned.

Safi turned toward me. “You are asking the boat to carry goods it has not accepted.”

“I am asking whether it can.”

“You have already told the woman it will.”

“I did not say that.”

The boy's thumb moved across the two marks on his board. He said nothing.

Jamil lifted a hand. “Let him finish.”

For a moment I was grateful to him. Then I saw the older boatman's expression. Jamil had stopped Safi for me, but he had not made any room in the boat.

“My grandfather has taken me into his house,” I said. “I am trying to see that the people who come to him are not sent away without an answer.”

There it was: the name I had held back at the receiving house, now offered as though it could keep a pole steady.

The man with the needle looked down at the covering. The carrier shifted his weight and turned toward the yard. Safi did not look away.

Jamil said, “Your grandfather did not accept these baskets for this boat.”

“I know.”

“Then leave him out of the count.”

I wanted to say that I had mentioned him only to explain why I was there. That was not true. I had mentioned him because the men were listening less readily than I had expected.

The older boatman crouched and put his hand against one of the sacks.

“There is another thing,” he said to Safi. “You have the long pieces going in before the last sack. If that sack is brought after they are tied down, the carrier will have to lift it over them.”

Safi knelt beside him. He traced the route from the board to the place where the sacks would lie.

“Then the last sack comes first,” he said.

“If it comes,” the carrier answered.

“It will need a sound binding,” Safi said. “I cannot count it as aboard until it is.”

Jamil's face tightened. He had wanted the boat made ready before the light changed, and I could tell he disliked how much time our answers were taking. He looked toward the yard.

“Send someone to hurry them.”

The carrier stepped away from the sacks.

“If you send the men with the poles, they will not be here to bring the poles,” he said.

Jamil looked back at him. The carrier stood still, as if he had already said more than was wise.

“Who can go?” Jamil asked.

The boy raised his hand.

“You have a board to keep,” the older boatman told him. “Stay where the goods come past you.”

A man who had finished carrying the first two sacks offered to go. He said he would bring back the answer, not the sack, and no one mistook the two.

We waited while he went. The sacks stayed on the steps.

Safi turned the folded covering over so its repaired edge faced the man with the needle.

“Let him check this again before we put it on,” he said. “I found the cloth, but he knows where it will take the pull.”

The man tested the seam between his fingers. He asked for another stitch at one corner. Safi held the cloth while he made it.

Jamil asked the older boatman whether Safi's order would work if the last sack came in time.

“It will,” the man said. “We move the two bundles, take the sack, then the poles. We leave the passage open until everyone is clear. After that we can close the covering.”

“And the food?” Jamil asked.

The boatman looked toward the shade.

“Ask the owner of this boat. I have said what I can load with the men I have. I have not said he will carry more.”

Jamil turned to me. “Your order needs another worker who has refused it. Safi's does not. We will use his.”

Heat rose beneath my collar. Safi had corrected his plan when the boatman objected. I had brought my grandfather into an argument over one pair of hands.

Safi did not smile. He was already helping the man fold the covering again.

The messenger from the yard came back alone. The binding on the third sack had been repaired, he said, but they needed the carrier to bring it down.

The carrier picked up his strap. He looked at Jamil.

“When it is here, I want to know what I am owed. I will not wait another hour to be told the owner has gone away.”

The older boatman agreed to speak to the owner before the sack was moved. The carrier went to fetch it.

The boy held up his board to Safi. “Do I take off the two marks?”

“No,” Safi said. “The baskets are still here.”

“Then what do I write beside them?”

“Nothing yet.”

I could hear the woman calling from the shade. She had seen the men begin moving the accepted load and thought that meant her food was next.

Safi glanced at me, then toward her. He could have gone to tell her the boat was full. He had won the right to speak about its loading. Instead he left the two marks where they were.

“You spoke to her,” he said. “She will ask you what you meant.”

I went toward the steps. I had an answer now, but it was smaller than the one I had given her before.
