## Historical evidence boundary: July 1521 Brunei

**Pigafetta's 29 July entry, available translation:** On 29 July 1521 the two surviving expedition ships saw a large group of local praus approaching near Brunei. Fearing they would be caught between those boats and junks behind them, the Europeans sailed against the junks, captured several, and killed people. One captured vessel held a son of the ruler of Luzon, described as Brunei's captain-general, returning from the reported conquest and sack of Laoe. The passage says Carvalho released him in return for gold without the rest's consent. The prince is **not named Ache** in the passage. The date belongs to the Brunei encounter, **not** the preceding Laoe campaign. Source: Antonio Pigafetta, 29 July 1521, translated account in Philippine Diary Project, https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/29th-of-july-1521/ .

**A materially different near-period narrative requires collation:** An English transcription attributed to an anonymous **Genoese pilot** describes five junks appearing at anchor, a large group of praus approaching the following morning, a European attack that captured a junk, and **another capture the next morning** of a larger junk carrying the son of the ruler of Lucam/Luzon. It gives a different sequence and additional crew/prisoner details. This is not interchangeable with Pigafetta's account. The online transcription's original manuscript, edition and translation lineage have not yet been authenticated. Source: https://www.historycentral.com/documents/Magellan.html (introductory provenance and Brunei passage).

**Francisco Albo's independent log (newly inspected 27 October 2026):** Albo's navigational log, in a dated-range English translation, reports difficult shoals on approach to Brunei, the need for a local pilot, several days of trading and an agreement of peace. It then describes **260 armed canoes** approaching, the Europeans hastily sailing away and capturing **one junk** with the son of the ruler of Luzon; he says the captain released that captive without consulting the others. This differs substantially from Pigafetta's **more than 100 praus in three groups**, capture of **four junks**, and associated casualties. Neither account can be silently treated as an exact corroboration of the other's numbers, targets or timing. Albo's entry is published under an **editorially bracketed 21 June–29 July 1521 range** rather than an independently verified single-day entry; do not quote the bracketed date as Albo's own dating. Source: Francisco Albo, [21 June–29 July 1521], Philippine Diary Project, https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/21-june-29-july-1521/ ; edition note https://philippinediaryproject.com/about-the-philippine-diary-project/about-the-diaries/about-francisco-albo/ . This strengthens the shoals/pilot and trade-then-alarm backdrop while increasing uncertainty over flotilla size and capture count. The log's assertion that canoes came 'to take us' is the European narrator's interpretation, not independently established intent.

**Crew and leadership gate:** Do not depict Elcano as the unquestioned captain of Victoria or the expedition at the moment of the July encounter. National Geographic's historical overview places Elcano's command of Victoria and Espinosa's election in **September 1521**, after the Brunei episode: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/this-man-was-actually-first-to-sail-around-the-world . The Philippine Diary Project's **29 July editorial introduction** appears to place the change soon after the encounter, while its **15 August–21 September** chronology places the change in September: https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/09/21/15th-august-21st-september-1521/ . The Genoese-pilot transcription names Espinosa as captain of Victoria during the Brunei reception. These sources differ in chronology and offices; inspect original passages and reliable editions before assigning a precise rank or command order to named Europeans in July. Carvalho's role and later release of the prince are attested in the Pigafetta translation but the exact mechanics require comparison.

**Fleet counts and identity gate:** The 100-plus praus observed on 29 July are not proven to be Ache's Loue force or a fleet he personally commanded. Do not carry over the numbers from one narrative context to another. The two European vessels are Trinidad and Victoria, following the loss of Concepción earlier in 1521, but their individual positions and which ship first engaged a particular junk require primary collation. Avoid invented precise cannon caliber, armor, number of casualties, boarding sequence, route coordinates, or named European commands as historical facts.

**Perspectives:** The European crews were short of provisions and wary of treachery after violence elsewhere. Brunei's ruler had his own local disputes; the local flotilla reportedly had a different purpose than attacking the Europeans. Ache's point of view cannot know what the strangers believe. A credible scene must show ordinary sailors and boat owners making partial, often rational judgments under fear. European firepower is dangerous but should not be written as inevitable superiority in every circumstance.

**Source follow-ups:** Compare Pigafetta's surviving French/Italian manuscript versions and the Robertson/Stanley editions at the relevant folio; identify the Genoese pilot's text and its original language/edition; read Francisco Albo's 21 June–29 July log entry for route and dates (https://philippinediaryproject.com/category/logbook-of-francisco-albo/page/5/); establish who held each command during July before writing a named European POV or dialogue. Do not treat later editors' parenthetical identifications as direct witness testimony.

## Hard continuity from Chapter 6

Ache returns from Laoe with **conditional responsibility** for a group of vessels carrying goods and messengers toward Luzon, subject to each boat owner's agreement and Jamil's oversight. He is neither owner of all the ships nor sovereign over Brunei's crews. Ruma, Tahir, Safi and Jamil have independent obligations. Laoe has been sacked; disputed goods and claims remain, some civilians have been harmed, Dima's mother's fate is unknown. Nasir is still missing. Ache feels genuine pride in his competence and growing standing, and is vulnerable to overestimating what that competence predicts. Chapter 7 cannot erase those consequences with spectacle.

## Six-scene architecture (provisional)

1. **A Ship That Did Not Turn.** Ache joins the outward movement from Brunei under his new limited mandate. A crew reports an unfamiliar high-sided vessel that holds its position differently from local craft. Ache's first inference is plausible but incomplete. **Decision:** go to see it without abandoning the vessels for which he is answerable. **End:** a second unfamiliar hull changes the risk.
2. **The Men Who Would Not Come Ashore.** Brunei intermediaries describe the strangers' recent trading and tensions. Ache hears mutually incompatible accounts about their demands and their reluctance to trust the port. European motives remain unknown to him. **Decision:** seek an identifiable messenger or shared signal instead of assuming a hostile mission. **End:** local boats and junks are already converging for purposes not everyone understands.
3. **Between the Two Waters.** The position of the unfamiliar ships, local junks and approaching praus creates overlapping lines of sight. Ache tries to keep his own group clear while another commander or boat owner refuses an unsafe movement. **Decision:** preserve the convoy rather than chase a vantage point. **End:** the strangers raise sail or otherwise move suddenly.
4. **The Answer No One Heard.** Ache's signal is read differently by his own crews and the strangers; translation and distance fail. A European captain's defensive action is visible but not intelligible. Safi and Jamil disagree over whether to hold or withdraw. **Decision:** Ache chooses a course based on his best available evidence, with a specific cost to a vessel he is charged to protect. **End:** one of the strangers attacks a junk.
5. **A Sound Across the Water.** The first attack changes the physical situation. Crew members react to unfamiliar weapons and smoke while still needing to steer, bail, and save people. Ache's attempted rescue or counter-maneuver can be tactically sensible and still fail. **Decision:** remain with endangered men rather than secure his own prestige or cargo. **End:** a vessel is cut off from the rest.
6. **The Wrong Side of the Hull.** The Europeans press their advantage against a separated vessel. Ache discovers that he cannot simultaneously preserve the convoy and reach the trapped crew. He makes a choice that leaves him exposed to capture; **do not complete the capture here** if Chapter 8 is to own the documented reversal. **End:** the threat is immediate and irreversible, but the actual boarding/capture and Carvalho bargain remain for Chapter 8.

## Literary and release gates

- Keep Ache first-person. His information is local and imperfect; avoid omniscient descriptions of what Elcano, Carvalho or Pigafetta thinks.
- Resist an automatic “guns defeat spears” lesson. Establish distance, wind, maneuver, hull construction, fatigue, communication and who can see whom, without fabricated exact specs.
- Retain the emotional and political cost of Laoe. The unfamiliar ships are a new event, not a cleansing adventure.
- Give Jamil and Safi choices independent of Ache; avoid making all other characters wrong so that he can be clever.
- Review the provisional title after complete prose. Do not use later historical fame as a spoiler in scene narration.
