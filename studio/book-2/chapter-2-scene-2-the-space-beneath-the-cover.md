# Book II, Chapter 2 — Scene 2: The Space Beneath the Cover

The boy reached the boat before me. He held up his board as if the marks on it would explain everything.

“There are two more baskets,” he told the man at the landing. “Ache said to leave room.”

“I said I would ask,” I answered.

The man looked from the boy to me. He had one foot on the steps and the other on a board laid between them and the boat. The board dipped when someone moved a sack behind him.

“Then ask,” he said. “But get off this board while you do it.”

I stepped back. The boy followed, holding his tally against his chest. The woman with the food waited beyond us. She had set her baskets down in a patch of shade, though she kept one hand on the loose binding.

A man on the boat called to the one at the steps. He wanted the covering lifted before the next load came aboard. The man answered that there were still people beneath it.

I had taken the boat for a large one from the passage. Close to it, the distance between the people working on either side seemed small. A narrow place ran down the middle, with bundles tied under a covering stretched above part of the load. A man was kneeling there, feeling along the edge of a woven mat. Every time another worker came through, he had to turn his knees aside.

The man from the steps called for someone named Jamil.

I had heard that name in Siripada’s house. It belonged to a man who could tell boatmen what they were to do, or so I had understood from the way others spoke of him. He came from farther along the landing with a younger man beside him. The younger man was carrying a folded length of cloth that had been patched in two places.

Jamil stopped before he reached us.

“Why is that still here?” he asked the man on the boat.

“The edge catches when we draw it over the sacks.”

“Did you try it?”

“Twice.”

“Then leave it open until you know where it catches. If you tie it again now, someone will have to undo it.”

He turned toward the boy and his board.

“Who has added a load?”

The boy raised the board. “Two baskets for the men above the bend.”

“Whose baskets?”

The woman came forward. “Mine to deliver. My brother bought the food. The men have been waiting since morning.”

“Who agreed to carry them?”

She pointed at me.

“I did not agree for the boat,” I said. “I told her I would see whether there was room.”

“You told me they had a place,” she answered.

Jamil looked at me. I wished he would first ask how I had come to be there, or who my grandfather was. He did neither.

“Have you been aboard?”

“No.”

“Do you know what is already promised?”

“No.”

“Then you can come and look before we speak further.”

He said it to me, not to the woman. She gathered the loose binding in her hand.

“Will you take the food?” she asked.

“I have not looked at it,” Jamil said.

She made a sound of impatience. “It is food, not stones.”

“The men who will eat it may care where it goes. The men carrying it will have to lift it first.”

He went down the steps. I followed him onto the board, waiting for the man ahead to clear it before I put my weight on it. The boat shifted beneath my foot. I caught the side with one hand.

The younger man with the patched cloth was already aboard. He set it down where it would not be stepped on.

“This is Safi,” Jamil said. “He has been asking the people whose goods are here what they expect of us.”

Safi gave me a short look. His clothes were finer than those of the men moving the sacks, but he had dirt on one knee and a red mark across his palm where something had rubbed against it.

“I know who Ache is,” he said.

I could not tell whether he meant it kindly. I had seen him once across Siripada’s courtyard. We had not spoken.

A man moved a bundle from the middle of the boat and laid it against the side. It looked to me as though he had opened enough room for the woman’s baskets.

“There,” I said. “Could they not stand beside that one?”

The man holding the bundle looked up.

“For how long?”

“Until you reach the upper bend.”

He pushed the bundle back with his knee.

“Then where will I put my foot when we take the long pieces aboard?”

I looked toward the steps. Three men were bringing poles down from a yard. They were longer than the space I had pointed to.

“They could lie across the top,” I said.

The man gave me the same look he had given the boy.

“On the food? On the bundles? Who will keep them from shifting?”

“I only meant—”

“I know what you meant. I am asking where you want them.”

Jamil held up his hand before I answered. He asked the man to show us what he had been trying to do.

The man drew one of the poles partway into the boat. Another worker caught its far end. Together they lowered it along the side, then lifted it again when it struck the edge of the covered load. The man who had been kneeling beneath the covering had to move out of the way. His mat remained half spread.

“Not there,” the second worker said. “The binding has to come over the top.”

He drew the pole back. The two men set it along the steps until they could move the other load.

The empty-looking place I had chosen was empty because they had been working through it.

Safi pointed to a stack beneath the covering. “Those sacks were accepted yesterday. The man who brought them has already paid for their handling.”

“Have they paid the boatman?” Jamil asked.

Safi looked toward the older man by the stern.

“He says they have agreed on payment after delivery. I have not heard it from the owner.”

“Then that is what you know.”

Safi's mouth tightened. He did not argue, though I saw that he disliked being corrected before me.

Jamil asked the man with the mat whether the covering could be secured with the poles aboard. The man pulled its edge down over the nearest bundle and showed him a worn place where the fastening would take the strain.

“If it tears here, what happens?” Jamil asked.

“We stop and tie it again, if there is enough left to tie.”

“Can you mend it before we leave?”

“If you give me the cloth and someone keeps people from walking on it.”

Safi picked up the folded piece he had brought. He held it out to the man.

“I found this in the yard. Is it enough?”

The man unfolded it, held the patches against the worn edge, and shook his head.

“Enough to cover the hole. Not enough to take the pull.”

Safi took it back without a word. I had expected him to know the answer before he asked. Instead he went to look for another piece.

I moved nearer the side of the boat. From there I could see the woman waiting with her baskets. She had begun to retie one of the covers. A boy who was not ours stopped beside her and asked whether she was waiting for the same boat. She answered him sharply. He went away.

Jamil stood beside me.

“Can you see the place you offered?”

“Yes.”

“Is it available?”

“Not while they are moving the poles.”

“Afterward?”

I looked at the sacks, the mat, the folded covering, the space through which the men kept passing. I wanted to say that the two baskets were small. I had carried one. I knew their weight better than anyone aboard.

“I don't know,” I said.

“Then we agree.”

He went to speak with the older boatman. I remained where I was. A carrier stepped onto the board with a sack across his back. He stopped when he saw me in his path.

I moved aside, but my foot caught the edge of the mat. The man who had been mending it pulled it free before I tore it.

“Stay where you can see without making another place for us to clear,” he said.

I stepped against the side and stayed there.

The carrier set down his sack, wiped his forehead, and asked the older boatman when the remaining load would come. He was being paid for the sacks he brought, he said, and had been waiting since the sun was lower. The boatman told him that another man had gone to fetch them.

“If I wait for him, I lose the next carrying job,” the carrier said.

“Then go,” the boatman answered.

“And leave these without knowing whether they will be taken?”

The boatman did not reply. The carrier stayed, but he sat on the steps instead of returning to the yard.

Safi came back with a different strip of cloth. The man under the covering tested it and nodded. Safi crouched to help him hold the edge steady while he worked. The red mark on Safi’s palm opened a little when he pulled. He shifted his grip and went on.

I could have offered to take his place. I thought of doing it, then saw that the man had already arranged the cloth against Safi’s hand. My help would have required them to stop and begin again.

Jamil returned from the stern.

“The baskets are for men working above the bend,” he said. “The woman says they cannot wait until tomorrow. Can this boat take them without putting off what was agreed?”

The older boatman looked at the cargo and then at the men waiting on the steps.

“Not until I know whether the last sacks are coming. If they are, I need the place by the side.”

“Could they be carried on a different boat?” I asked.

“Whose?” the boatman said.

I looked across the landing. Several boats were tied there. I knew nothing of their owners or where they were going.

“I could ask.”

“Ask if you like. Do not tell the woman they will take them.”

Safi glanced up from the covering. He had heard.

The older boatman turned to Jamil. “If you tell me to take the baskets, I will take them. Tell me which load I am to leave.”

Jamil said nothing for a moment. I watched him look from the covered sacks to the waiting carrier, and then toward the woman.

“No one is leaving an accepted load because Ache spoke too soon,” he said. “Finish the covering. We will decide when the rest is here.”

He did not tell the woman to go. He did not tell her to stay.

I went back across the board. The boy with the tally followed me, but he stopped when I turned.

“Did you take the marks off?” I asked.

“Which marks?”

“The two baskets.”

He showed me the board. Near the bottom, he had made two short cuts apart from the others.

“They are still there,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because the baskets are still waiting.”

He had not written that the boat would carry them. I had been angry with him for repeating my promise, but his marks told less than my words had.

The woman watched me approach.

“Well?”

“They are seeing whether the rest of the load is coming.”

“And if it comes?”

“Then there may be no place.”

She stared at me for a long moment. “My brother was told to bring the food here.”

“I know.”

“You don't know who told him.”

“No.”

“Then find out before you tell me anything else.”

She lifted the smaller basket and held it toward me.

“Take this back to the shade. It will spoil sooner if it stands in the sun.”

I took it. I had wanted to carry it onto the boat. Instead I carried it the few steps back to the wall.

Behind me, a man called that the remaining sacks had been found. The carrier on the steps stood up quickly and asked how many there were. No one answered him at once.

Safi was coming off the boat with the torn piece of covering in his hands. He saw me setting the basket down.

“Jamil wants us both here when the last sacks arrive,” he said.

“For what?”

“To decide what can be taken without leaving someone else's work behind.”

“You already know the loads.”

“I know what people told me. That is not always what comes down the steps.”

He went past me toward the yard. I looked at the woman's baskets, then at the marks on the boy's board. The space I had promised had never been mine to give. I still wanted to find a way to put the baskets aboard before Safi returned.
