# Book II, Chapter 10 — Scene 3: The Two Places

**Packet:** RA-B2C10-SCENE-03. **State:** Studio prose draft; not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Sulad has returned but has not agreed to sail; Laila offers possible paid store work subject to Jamil's permission. No northbound owner, vessel, crew, provisioning or royal authorization has yet been secured. **Historical status:** Siripada's private offers and court dialogue are fictional; no primary witness establishes these exact terms. Do not identify Siripada conclusively with Bolkiah.

---

Jamil sent for me when the water was low enough to walk beneath the houses on the eastern bank.

The messenger said the ruler would be there. I had expected to be called into the hall where men stood according to their place and waited for a name to be spoken. Instead I found Siripada in the shade of a raised house, watching two carpenters remove a rotten crosspiece from the platform above him.

The piece had been painted once. The color remained only where a beam had covered it. One carpenter was sawing through a peg while the other held the wood so it would not fall into the mud. Siripada stood with his hands folded behind his back. Jamil was beside him, holding a short board.

"Tell me whether they can use that timber again," Siripada said when he saw me.

I looked at the crosspiece. Its ends were black with rot. The middle still seemed hard.

"For a shorter span, perhaps."

The carpenter with the saw stopped.

"Would you put your foot on it?" Siripada asked.

"Not without testing it."

"Then why have you offered it to another man?"

The carpenter resumed cutting. I had wanted to give an answer that saved wood. I had not considered who would stand on the platform afterward.

Siripada waited until the peg came free. The crosspiece fell into the carpenter's arms, and he carried it into the light. Its underside was split along most of its length. He set it aside with the other waste.

"Jamil tells me you have been carrying questions for Hasan," the ruler said.

"Yes."

"Has he been able to use your answers?"

"He has sent me back when I brought only what I thought had happened."

"And now?"

"Now I ask who saw it."

Jamil looked at me. "Sometimes."

Siripada smiled at him, not at me.

A servant brought a mat and set it on the dry ground. We sat where the workers could still be heard. A bowl of water stood between us. Siripada did not ask for a full account of the foreign ships. He had heard men describe them until they disagreed over which ship had fired first. He asked what had happened to the people taken from the junk.

"Some came back," I said. "Others were still held when I left."

"Can you name them?"

"Some."

"Can you tell me what was paid for you?"

"I saw gold brought aboard. I did not hear the bargain."

"Can you tell me who gave it?"

"No."

He looked toward Jamil.

"The merchant says his chest was opened," Jamil said. "The people who carried the metal say they obeyed an order. No one has produced an account that both sides accept."

"Then the debt remains," Siripada said.

I had hoped he would tell me who had paid. I had hoped, too, that he would say the payment was a gift from the ruler and that I owed nothing more. He said neither.

"Men at the harbor are asking whether you will send boats toward Luzon," Jamil told me.

"I have asked owners about passage."

"One owner has agreed to speak to you," Jamil said. "That is all."

"Ruma knows him."

"Ruma has her own work."

"I know."

Siripada lifted the bowl and drank. The carpenters above us struck a new peg into place. Three blows sounded firm. The fourth made a different noise. The man with the mallet stopped, withdrew the peg, and looked into the hole before choosing another.

"You came to Burné with a story about the home you had lost," Siripada said. "You have told that story to my household, to merchants and to men who might carry your words. You have now been away long enough that the place may have changed again."

"That is why I must go."

"Must?"

I looked at Jamil. He did not rescue me from the word.

"I want to know who holds Maynila," I said. "I want to know what has happened to the people I left."

"Then a traveler might bring you an answer."

"A traveler might be refused."

"And a ship with armed men might be welcomed?"

I had imagined a vessel approaching the river mouth with my name carried before it. I had imagined the men who had once dismissed me standing aside when they saw that I had returned with friends. I had also seen Laoe after men came to settle a claim.

"I do not know," I said.

"Then you will have to learn before you decide how many men to take."

Siripada set the bowl down.

"There is work for you here. Hasan needs men who can carry a count from one bank to another without making it larger on the way. Jamil has a place for a clerk who knows the routes. You have learned the names of owners and the limits of their bargains. You could keep that work. There would be food and pay, and you would answer for what you did rather than for what people say your grandfather ought to give you."

I had expected him to tell me that I could stay if I gave up speaking of Maynila. He had offered something harder to dismiss.

"Would I serve Jamil?" I asked.

"You would work with his men. He would decide which duties you had earned."

"And my place in your household?"

"You would still be my kin."

Jamil shifted his board. "He would not command the harbor boats."

Siripada looked at him. "He would not need to."

I thought of Laila's storeroom and the jars we had moved above the water. I thought of the job she had offered after the next market. The ruler had given me a way to remain without pretending that my losses had made me a captain. I wanted to ask whether I could keep the work and still be treated as someone who might one day lead men. I knew Jamil would hear the question I was really asking.

"What if I choose to go north?" I said.

"Then I will hear what the owners offer you."

"Will you give me ships?"

"I will not give away boats that belong to other people."

"Men?"

"Not men who have not agreed."

"Then what can you give?"

Siripada's face changed. He had been patient with me, but I had begun to ask as though his first answer were an insult.

"A name under which you may ask," he said. "A letter for the people you hope to meet. Permission for any owner who wishes to trade on that route to hear your proposal without fearing that I have forbidden it. If there is a public reason to send goods, I may approve a limited share from my own stores. I will not pay for a quarrel whose end I cannot see."

"Would you give me authority over the voyage?"

"Over what voyage?"

I had no answer. The carpenter above us drove the replacement peg home. This time the sound did not change.

Jamil opened the board he carried. It held the names of boats with work already agreed. Beside each were cuts for the owner, the cargo and the hands.

"Choose a master who will go," he said. "Find out where he intends to sail. If he has room for your purpose, tell him what you will pay. If you need men to accompany you, let them hear the terms from you and answer for themselves. Then bring the agreement here. I can judge work that has been offered. I cannot judge a fleet you have imagined."

I looked at the board. I had spent months learning how to make marks like those. I wanted to throw it aside and ask for something grander. I did not.

"And if the owners refuse?"

"Then you have no voyage," Jamil said.

Siripada turned toward the carpenters. They had raised the new crosspiece into place. One was testing it with his weight while the other watched the joints. The man stepped across it twice before he called to the workers above that they could continue.

"Your return could help the merchants who trade with Luzon," Siripada said. "Or it could make their passages dangerous. If men in Maynila take your arrival as a threat, they may blame the ships that brought you. Those owners have a right to know what you intend."

"I intend to go home."

"That tells them where you want to land. It does not tell them what you will do after."

I could have said I would restore what had been taken. I could have said I would make Maynila answer to me. The words rose easily. I had carried them in my head since the day I left.

"I will have to speak to the people there," I said.

"That would be a beginning."

Siripada stood. Jamil rose with him, and I followed. The ruler placed a hand against the new crosspiece and tested it. It held.

"You do not have to answer today," he said. "If you stay, there is work. If you go, bring me terms that other people have accepted. Do not ask me to call a wish a ship."

He went up the steps into the house. Jamil stayed until the carpenters had begun clearing the old timber.

"Hasan says Sulad has come back," he said.

"He has. He will give his own account."

"Has he agreed to go with you?"

"No."

"Then do not put him on a crew list."

"I have not."

"Good. There is a place in my yard tomorrow if you wish to work. I will not hold it open forever while you ask about a northbound passage."

He left me beside the discarded crosspiece. Its painted face looked sound where the sun touched it. I turned it with my foot and saw the split running almost to the middle.

I had been offered two places. One had a roof, work I knew how to do, and people who would count whether I arrived. The other had no hull beneath it yet. I had wanted the second to look like a command. Siripada had made me see how much of it I still had to ask for.

I went toward the landing to find the owner Ruma had mentioned. I did not know whether he had arrived. I knew I would have to let him answer before I counted his boat.
