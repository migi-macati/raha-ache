# Book II, Chapter 9 — Scene 2: The Hull That Returned

**Studio draft. Not reader-facing.** Ache POV. Continues the verified harbor ledger and family inquiry in Scene 1; the junk's arrival is fictional, not a documented consequence of the 1521 encounter.

---

Hasan had agreed to pay a boatman for one visit to the traps, provided the boatman named the water he would take and returned with the witness's words. He had not agreed to send me.

I went to the landing at first light to find the man he had named. The tide was still below the lowest sound step. Men were lifting a boat from its supports so they could scrape the growth from its bottom. They worked with pieces of shell and a broad knife whose handle had been wrapped twice with cord. The owner stood by the bow and pointed out a split seam that needed binding before the boat went back into the water.

The man Hasan had named was among them. He listened to my question while he held the hull steady with his shoulder.

"I can go when this is finished," he said.

"When will that be?"

"When the owner says the seam holds."

"Nasir's sister has paid for an inquiry already."

"Then she knows what a boat costs. I will take the question. I will not take you as another passenger without asking."

I said I understood. He went back to the hull. I watched him for a time, wanting to find a way to make his work faster. Every suggestion I thought of had been made by someone already holding the boat.

A shout came from the farther posts.

Tahir was standing with his nephew at the water's edge. Beyond the outer shoal a junk had appeared, its sail set low and patched near one corner. It moved slowly, turning against the current in small corrections. A boat with two rowers followed beside it, close enough to catch a line if it lost its way.

I recognized the patched corner before I recognized the men. The junk's master had shown me the tear when I climbed aboard to help his wounded sailor. I had watched the foreign sailors draw the junk alongside their high ship. I had last seen it from above, with the boy at the injured man's head and the older woman carrying her basket.

Now it was coming in.

I went to Tahir.

"Have you counted it?"

"Not until it reaches the post."

"That is the junk."

"I can see that it is a junk."

"The one I was taken from."

He looked at me. "Then stay where the master can see you. If he wants to speak, he will."

The junk touched the outer post with a dull knock. The crew had put folded cloth between the rail and the wood. Two men held the bow off while a third carried the line to shore. No one moved quickly. The master stood near the stern with his headcloth tied loosely, giving short instructions to the steersman.

He saw me. His mouth tightened, but he did not call my name.

Tahir counted the crew first. The master answered for the men at the stern and made the others answer for themselves. The boy was there, thinner than I remembered, still holding the cup from which he had given the wounded sailor water. The injured man lay under a covering beside the mast. His splinted leg rested on a roll of sailcloth.

The older woman sat near him with her basket between her feet.

When the count was finished, Tahir asked who had left the junk after I was taken.

The master named two men who had been moved to the foreign ship and returned before the junk sailed. He named a third who had been carried away and had not returned. He would not call that man dead.

Tahir made a separate mark.

"Where did you come from?" he asked.

"From the high ship's side, then the outer anchorage. They held us while they searched the cargo. We waited for the tide and for permission to leave."

"Who gave permission?"

The master looked out toward the foreign vessels. "A man who spoke to the one with the writing board. I do not know his name."

Tahir did not write a name for him.

Ruma came down from the storehouse with two of her crew. She looked at the wounded sailor before she spoke to the master.

"I have cloth and water ashore," she said. "If he can be moved, we can lay him beneath the eaves."

"His leg was bound again aboard the foreign ship," the master said. "I do not know whether they tightened it correctly."

"Then do not pull him by it."

She inspected the sailcloth beneath him and asked two men to bring a second length of cloth from the storehouse. The master told his crew to wait until she was ready. The boy had begun to lift the wounded man's shoulder, but Ruma put her hand on the sailcloth.

"You have done enough lifting for now," she told him.

"He will want me there."

"Then come beside him. Let the men carry."

The boy hesitated. The wounded man said something I could not hear. The boy moved to his head.

I took a step toward the junk. The master held out his hand.

"Stay there."

I stopped on the lower step.

"I came to see whether he was alive."

"You have seen."

"Can I help?"

"You helped when he was hurt. Then they took you. My men kept him alive after that."

I looked at the boy, who had carried the cup from the foreign deck to this landing. The master was right.

Ruma's men spread the second cloth. The junk's crew shifted the injured sailor onto it, keeping his leg supported. The boy walked beside them as they brought him down the plank. He did not ask me to carry anything.

The older woman waited until the wounded man was clear, then stood and lifted her basket. She climbed ashore without assistance. I went toward her.

"Your son," I began.

She stopped. Her face was tired and deeply lined around the mouth.

"Have you found him?"

"No."

"Did you give his description?"

"To Hasan's clerk. I told him about the scar on his left shoulder and the place where you last saw him swim."

"Who has looked?"

"I do not know who has gone to that bank yet."

She stared at me. I had answered the question I wanted her to ask, whether I had remembered. She had asked who had looked.

"I can take you to the clerk," I said.

"I can find a clerk."

She stepped around me and went up toward the eaves. She asked Tahir where people who had come from the foreign ships could give their names. He showed her the board. She spoke to him herself, setting her basket on the dry stone beside his feet.

The master had come ashore. He walked along the side of his junk, testing each patch with his fingers. A plank near the stern had been scraped raw where the vessels struck together. He pressed it and looked at the seam below.

"Did the foreigners do that?" I asked.

"Some of it. Some came when we grounded in the outer water. Some was there before."

"Can you sail?"

"We sailed here."

"Can you take another passage?"

He straightened and turned toward me.

"That is what you came to ask."

"No. I came because I saw the sail."

"You were looking for a boat before we arrived. Men have told me what the court wants from those who can still move."

"Jamil has not ordered you to go anywhere."

"Then why are you asking?"

I had been thinking about the boats that might carry questions to the outer banks, the boats that might follow a missing captive, the boats that might eventually take men toward Luzon. I had begun to count the junk among them before its master had stepped ashore.

"I should not have asked," I said.

He looked at the raw plank again.

"My brother was at Laoe," he said. "He went there because he believed there would be work when the fighting ended. We have had no word of him. Yesterday I watched men on the high ship count my crew as though they were jars. I do not know whether one of my men will come home. And now I have a hull to mend."

"I remember your brother."

"You remember that I told you about him. You do not know him."

"No."

He bent to the seam. I crouched a little way from him and watched as he put a thin sliver of wood into the gap to judge its depth. The scrape ran farther than I had seen from the steps. It would need a dry landing, a place to work, fiber, resin and men who knew how to fit the plank.

"Who can repair it?" I asked.

"My people can. If I can pay them to stop carrying other men's goods."

"How much?"

He named a quantity of rice, a share of resin, and two days' labor if the plank held when dried. If it had split beneath the waterline, he would need a new piece of timber. He had not inspected that far.

"I have some rice," I said.

"Keep it. I did not ask you to buy my forgiveness."

"I wasn't offering that."

"What were you offering?"

I could not tell him without making the rice into a promise he had not asked for.

"Food for the men who would have to work," I said.

"Then ask the men what they need. Do not put it in my account."

He returned to the hull.

I went to the storehouse. Hasan's clerk was reading a board with the names of the returned junk's crew. I asked whether the storehouse had rice set aside for the injured.

"It has water and cloth for the people brought ashore," he said. "Food has been offered by two households. One has already sent it. We have not promised repairs."

"Would Jamil approve the resin?"

"That depends on whose goods were aboard and who has a claim against the master."

"He was taken by the foreigners."

"That does not tell me who owns his cargo."

I had heard that answer from other men before. I had begun to hate it because it did not make the work go away.

I took the rice I had been keeping for my own meals and carried it to the men lifting the junk onto the mud. I told them it was mine and that they could use it for their midday food if they wanted. One man accepted it. Another asked whether I expected a place on their boat in return.

"No," I said.

He waited, then took the rice from the first man and divided it into two baskets so it could be cooked with the food they had brought.

By midday the tide had fallen enough for them to brace the junk on its side. I held a pole where the master told me to hold it. He would not let me work on the seam, and I did not ask twice. The scraped plank was worse beneath the waterline. One of the workers ran his nail along a dark split and shook his head.

"Not two days," he said.

The master asked him to show the others. They gathered around the split. One argued for a narrow patch. Another said it would open again as soon as the hull took a load. The master listened to both and chose to replace the plank. He asked who could fetch timber before the next market day.

No one offered.

The man who had taken my rice said he could go if another worker covered his share of a cargo delivery. The master asked who would pay that worker. No one answered.

I had imagined that the junk's return would give us another vessel. Its return had given us a damaged hull, an injured man who needed care, a missing sailor, and people who had to decide which work they could afford to leave undone.

I walked back to the storehouse for the question Hasan had agreed to send to the traps. The boatman I had spoken to at dawn was still scraping his owner's hull. The seam had not been bound.

"Will you go today?" I asked.

"Look at the water."

It had fallen away from the steps. His boat was high on its supports, and the resin pot had not yet been opened.

"Tomorrow?" I asked.

"If the owner says the seam holds."

I took the question back to Nasir's sister's stall. She was hanging strips of fish beneath the roof, high enough to keep them from the cats. She listened while I told her that the junk had returned and that the inquiry boat had not gone.

"Did the junk bring Nasir?"

"No."

"Did it bring anyone who saw the cut?"

"No."

"Then we still need the man at the traps."

"Yes."

She looked at the board on which she had marked the cost of the first two inquiries. There was space for another line. She did not fill it yet.

"You have eaten?" she asked.

I shook my head.

"Why?"

"I gave my rice to the men repairing the junk."

She went back to hanging the fish.

"Then you will have to find work for your own meal," she said. "Do not spend tomorrow's boat fare to make today feel useful."

I looked toward the water. Beyond the landing the junk rested on its braces, its exposed plank marked for removal. The master and his men had chosen to repair it. They had made no promise about what it would carry afterward.

Nasir's sister handed me the empty basket from which she had taken the fish.

"Bring water," she said. "The drying boards need washing before dark."

I took the basket and went to the well.
