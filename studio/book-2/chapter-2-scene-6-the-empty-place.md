# Book II, Chapter 2 — Scene 6: The Empty Place

The boy had turned his board over to make room for the next load. I watched him draw three new marks, each one after a man had crossed the landing with a sack. When a fourth man came through without carrying anything, the boy held his hand still.

"Is he coming back?" I asked.

"Ask him."

The man had gone into the yard. I could see his bare feet beneath the hanging edge of a mat, but I could not see what he was doing there.

"You know him," I said.

"I know he carried one. I don't know whether he will carry another."

I left the boy to his work. Behind me, someone called my name.

Jamil stood by the wall with another covering folded over his arm. A long tear ran along one edge; this one had not yet been mended. Safi was beside him, holding a short length of cord with a knot at each end.

"The boat has gone," Jamil said.

"I saw."

"The owner took the load we agreed upon. The food stayed here."

"I saw that too."

"Then come with us."

I followed them into the yard. I had expected Jamil to take me to Siripada's house. Instead he stopped beside a smaller boat drawn up where the mud gave way to stones. It lay on its side, with one edge propped above the ground. A man sat beneath the raised edge, pulling at a fastening that would not come free.

"Is this the next boat?" I asked.

"It belongs to a man who has asked whether we can help him prepare it," Jamil said.

"For where?"

"He has not told me."

I looked along its length. A rolled mat rested inside. Near the bow, a coil of cord lay under a loose piece of wood. It seemed an easy vessel to ready after the one we had just watched leave.

"That fastening is worn," Safi said. He pointed with the cord in his hand. "The man asked for two hands to hold the side while he works."

Jamil set down the folded covering.

"How many hands have we?"

"Here?" I asked.

"That is one question."

Safi said, "The two who finished the covering have gone to eat. The mender said he might return."

"Did he say he would?" Jamil asked.

"No."

"The man beneath the boat is here," I said.

"He is working," Safi answered. "He asked for help. You cannot count him as the help he needs."

I looked toward the passage. A carrier I recognized was walking out with a strap wound around his wrist. He had been at the landing when we argued about the sacks.

"He is free now," I said.

The carrier heard me. He stopped.

"Free of what?"

"The last load is gone."

"Yes."

"Would you help lift this side?"

He looked at the propped boat, then at the strap in his hand.

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Who pays?"

I looked at Jamil. He did not answer for me.

"The owner may pay," I said.

"Then the owner may ask."

He went on. I watched him turn into the passage, angry at the way he had made me sound as though I had offered him nothing. He had no reason to stop.

The man beneath the boat pushed himself into the open. He held a wooden peg between two fingers.

"I need the side steady long enough to take this out without splitting the wood," he said. "I do not need a boy telling every man who passes that there is work."

"Will the peg come free?" Jamil asked.

"It may. The hole has swollen around it. I would rather wait for the owner than break it."

Jamil bent to look. His knee pressed into the stones. He reached for the peg, but the man moved it out of his hand.

"If you pull it now, the wood may go with it."

Jamil straightened. He did not seem pleased.

"Then leave it."

"I was leaving it," the man said.

Safi looked away. I thought he was hiding a smile. When he turned back, his face was plain.

Jamil picked up the covering again. "We have another length to mend before the next load can be covered. Safi, you know what has already been promised. Go and see whether the mender will take it, and what he wants for the work. Do not tell him he must."

Jamil handed him the folded covering. Safi wound the short cord around his palm.

"And the people who brought the poles?"

"Find out whether they need the covering returned. We cannot give it to someone else until we know."

Safi nodded. He had a task that could change what happened to goods already accepted. I stood beside a boat whose owner had not even told us where he meant to go.

"Come here," Jamil said.

He found a narrow offcut of wood beside the wall, rubbed dirt from one side with his thumb, and gave it to me.

"Find out what this boat needs before anyone offers men or cloth for it. Begin with the man who is working."

"What do I mark?"

"What he tells you. And what you see. Keep them apart."

I turned the wood in my hand. There was room for a few cuts along its edge. I could make the marks, but there were too many things to ask and no place to put the answers.

"How will you know which mark means which?"

"You will tell me. If you cannot remember, do not make the mark yet."

Safi moved toward the passage. Before he left, he glanced at the wood.

"Ask whether the owner has paid for the repair," he said.

"I know to ask that."

"You knew to ask the carrier too."

I wanted to answer him. Jamil was already walking away, and Safi followed with the folded covering. Neither waited to hear what I might say.

I crouched beside the boat.

The man had gone back under its side. I could see one hand braced against the wood and the other holding the peg.

"Who owns it?" I asked.

He gave me a name I had not heard before.

"Is he coming?"

"He said he would come when he had finished at the other landing."

"Did he say when?"

"No."

"What must be done?"

"Hold the side. Remove the peg. See whether the hole will take another without opening the split."

"Then the boat can go?"

He came out far enough to look at me.

"Then we will know whether the peg can be replaced."

I studied the edge he had shown Jamil. A thin dark line ran from the hole toward a place where the grain changed. It might have been a crack. I did not know enough to tell.

"Can you mend it?"

"Perhaps. If I can take the peg out whole."

"Do you have another?"

"No."

"Does the owner?"

"He says he does."

I almost made a cut. Instead I asked, "Have you seen it?"

"No."

The man watched my hand above the wood.

"That is why I am waiting."

He went back beneath the side. I stayed on the stones and tried to remember his answers. There was one worker here. He could not hold the side and remove the peg alone. The owner was absent. A replacement peg was said to exist, but no one had brought it. The repair might be small, or it might open the wood.

I cut one mark near the edge for the man who was here. Beside it I left a space for the hands he needed. I could have cut two more marks and called them expected. The wood was mine to hold; the men were not mine to put upon it.

From the passage came the sound of a quarrel. I turned, expecting another dispute over a load. It was only two boys arguing about which of them had been sent for water. One held an empty jar. The other said he had been told to carry it, not fill it. They both looked at me as I passed, waiting for me to decide.

"Who sent you?" I asked.

They named different women.

"Then ask them."

One boy laughed. The other scowled. Neither moved until I had gone back to the boat.

Jamil returned after the light had shifted away from the wall. He had no one with him.

"How many?" he asked.

"One man working. He needs two to hold the side."

"Two more?"

"That is what he asked for. I have not found them."

"What else?"

"The peg is caught. He says the owner has another, but he has not seen it. He cannot tell whether the hole will hold until he takes this one out."

Jamil took the offcut and looked at the single mark.

"Why only one?"

"That is the man who is here."

"And these spaces?"

"Those are the men he needs."

"Can they be found?"

"I have not asked enough people to know. The carrier who passed said the owner must ask him about pay."

"Good."

I felt a quick satisfaction. Then Jamil added, "You asked him before you knew the work or the pay."

"Yes."

"He may not listen the next time."

The man beneath the boat called that the peg had shifted a little. Jamil knelt again. This time he kept his hands clear while the man showed him the movement.

"Can it come out?" Jamil asked.

"Not until the side is held."

Jamil looked toward the yard.

"I thought the men from the first boat would still be here."

"They went to eat," I said. "Safi said the mender might come back."

"I know what Safi said."

He rose and wiped his hand against his knee. He looked tired, and I wondered how many people had asked him to decide something before he had seen it.

"Tomorrow you will come to this yard before the first load reaches the landing," he said. "Ask the people working which hands they have agreed to bring, what work has been accepted, and what is still waiting for an owner to answer. Tell me before you speak to anyone else about a place aboard."

"Am I to do that for every boat?"

"For the ones I send you to. If you are not sent, you may watch. You may not promise."

I looked at the offcut in his hand.

"Will I carry the count?"

"You may bring it to me. I will check it against the people who made the bargains."

"And Safi?"

"Safi will see to the accepted loads and the covering. He has earned that work."

I did not want Safi to hear that. He had already gone, and the words seemed to follow him anyway.

"Will my grandfather know?" I asked.

Jamil studied me. For a moment I wished I had asked about the peg instead.

"He may ask what you have done. Then I will tell him."

He gave back the wood. I had expected something more for my day's embarrassment. A word from Siripada, perhaps, or a task that would take me near a ship bound west. I had a piece of wood and an instruction to arrive before the carriers.

"What of the message I brought from Maynila?"

"Has a master agreed to carry it?"

"No."

"Then I have no voyage to put it on."

"Can I ask you to look for one?"

"You can ask me whether I know a man who will speak to you. Today I do not. If I hear of one, I will tell you what I have heard. I will not say he has agreed."

I wanted him to say he would try. He did not. I could not make him responsible for a message he had never offered to carry.

I turned the offcut over. There was room on the other side for more marks.

"Sulad has words to add before the witnesses," I said. "He is working farther along the bank."

"Then speak to Sulad."

"He may not come when I ask."

"Then you will know he has not agreed."

Jamil went toward the passage. The man beneath the boat asked whether he should wait any longer. Jamil stopped and told him the owner must answer for the extra hands and the peg. He did not tell him the repair would be finished tomorrow.

I stayed beside the propped boat while the man put away his tool. He drew a mat over the exposed fastening so no one would strike it in the dark. Then he took his things and went.

The boat remained where it was. I could see the gap beneath its side and the place where the peg had not come free.

On my piece of wood I had marked one hand. Beside it were two empty spaces.

I knew where Sulad had said he would be working. I could go there when the men were done and ask whether he wished to speak his own words. I could also return to the witnesses without him and pretend that asking was enough.

I tucked the wood beneath my arm. The empty spaces were still there when I reached the passage.
