The letter was shorter than I expected.

Siripada had it read to me beneath the same raised house where I had first asked for ships. The rotten crosspiece was gone. A new one held the platform above us, and the carpenters had taken their tools away. Jamil stood beside the ruler with the boards Harun and Tamin had marked.

The letter said I was kin to the ruler of Burné. It asked those who received me to hear my words and allow lawful trade if they wished. It did not say that Maynila belonged to me. It did not order anyone to give me a river, a house, or a throne.

"Is that enough?" Siripada asked.

"It is more than I had when I came here."

"That is not an answer."

"It is enough to ask to be heard."

He watched me while the reader rolled the letter. Jamil put Harun's board beside it.

"The trade goods are counted," Jamil said. "Four bundles of cloth and the resin Hasan marked as unpledged. The kitchens, the watch boats and the households with claims have been supplied first. Harun takes the goods on the return terms he has accepted. If he turns back, he owes only what those terms require. Tamin carries his own cargo and water. Neither owner sails under an order to fight."

"Who leads your party?" Siripada asked.

"I do."

"Who commands Harun's vessel?"

"Harun and the men he has appointed."

"And if he will not enter Maynila?"

"I will ask him where he can land us safely. I will not take his boat from him."

"Who will speak for you when you are ashore?"

"Sulad, if he chooses to speak. Isan where the traders need another tongue. I will speak for my own claim."

Jamil looked at the board.

"Isan has agreed to interpret for the passage and for talks he can understand," he said. "He has not agreed to fight. Sulad has accepted a working place aboard. Neither has pledged to your cause beyond the terms he heard."

"I know."

Siripada held out his hand. Jamil gave him the board. The ruler looked at the marks for a long time. He had made other decisions that morning, about boats to repair and men to feed. This was one more bargain among them. I wanted it to be greater.

"When you came here," he said, "you wanted me to send you back with a name that would make other people stand aside. I could have given you more men. They might have died for a claim they had never heard explained."

"I know."

"You say that often now."

"I have had reasons to learn it."

"You have also had reasons to forget."

I remembered the houses at Laoe and the smell of wet ash. I remembered the foreign deck beneath my feet, where I had been called a prisoner in a language I could barely follow. I had returned to Burné alive and wanted that fact to prove I had earned something.

Siripada gave the board back to Jamil.

"I approve the trade and the letter," he said. "You may lead the people who have agreed to your purpose. If you bring violence to merchants who travel under my name, you will answer for it. If you return, you will tell me what you did. If you do not return, the owners will still be owed what was promised them."

I bowed. The reader brought the letter to Jamil for its final mark. Siripada rose and walked toward the steps. He stopped before climbing them.

"Your home may have found a way to live without you," he said.

"I have thought of that."

"Think of it when you see the shore. Not only here, where it costs nothing."

He went inside.

Jamil stayed. He gave me the letter when it was sealed, but kept the cargo board.

"Harun gets this," he said. "The storekeeper gets a copy. Hasan will keep the account. Do not put all three in your own hands."

"I won't."

He looked at the empty place on the board where my name had been waiting.

"You may fill it," he said.

I wrote my mark beneath the terms I had accepted. It was small enough to fit between two other names.

At the landing, Harun's crew had already begun carrying water aboard. A jar slipped from a man's hands and struck the planks. Its bottom broke, and water spread between the boards before anyone could catch it. The man stared at the pieces.

Harun came from the hold.

"How many remain?" he asked.

"Four sound jars and the one we filled yesterday," the steersman said.

"That was the one."

They counted again. The crew had enough to reach the first stopping place only if they did not spend a day waiting outside the river. The wind had turned. Harun said they could leave on the next tide, but not with that count.

I had seen the boat ready and thought of the men in Maynila who might die before I arrived. The thought came without a name or a reason. I wanted to say we should go while the water was high.

Tamin was standing beside his own vessel, watching the broken jar being carried ashore.

"Can you spare one?" I asked him.

"Not without making my return uncertain."

"Can you sell us one?"

"Not one I have promised to my men."

Harun looked at me. I could feel the answer I had almost given: that the ruler's letter should be worth a jar, that the voyage mattered more than a crewman's thirst.

"Then we wait," I said.

Harun nodded once. He sent a boy to the market for a sound vessel and told the crew to lift the remaining jars into shade. I went with the boy. The first seller offered a jar with a crack concealed beneath a strip of cloth. The second wanted more coin than I had. The third had a jar she would sell if we brought back an empty one to replace it. We carried the broken pieces to show her why we could not.

She looked at the shards, then at the coin in my hand.

"I can give you a smaller jar," she said. "Bring me a sound one on your return, or pay the difference now."

I paid the difference. It was money I had saved for my own landing. I would have less to offer when I reached Luzon.

The jar was filled and sealed in front of the steersman. He tested the stopper, turned it once, and put his mark beside the count. Harun did not praise me. He told the men to load it where it would not strike the others.

By the time the tide rose again, the wind had steadied. Harun sent a man to the mast and another to the forward line. Tamin's crew were already aboard their own vessel, settling the baskets of salt they meant to trade. The two owners had agreed where they would meet after leaving the river. If one boat failed to appear, the other would wait only until the next safe departure. After that each would follow the terms of its own voyage.

I went once more to Hasan's yard.

Nasir's sister was there with Tahir. The three names from the inquiry boat had been copied onto separate boards. One witness had come to speak. He said he had seen a man carried toward the far bank, but he could not name him. Nasir's sister had asked him what the man wore. He described a cloth of a color half the men on the river owned.

"That is what he saw," she said. "Do not put Nasir's name beside it."

Hasan agreed. He gave her the board to keep.

"I am leaving," I told her.

"I know. Your boat is at the outer posts."

"I have left the questions with Hasan and Tahir. They know which men still need to be heard."

She looked at the marks.

"You carried them correctly," she said.

"I wish I could have brought you an answer."

"So do I."

She turned to the witness. He had more to say about the bank, and she wanted to hear it before he went home. I waited for her to look at me again. She did not. I left her asking her own question.

The older woman from the damaged junk was at the next landing. The master had found the timber he needed and was sealing the repaired seam. The boy who had once been held aboard the foreign ship carried resin to him. The injured sailor could sit upright now. He was not ready to work.

The woman had decided to stay while the master finished the repair. She would ask each boat coming from the outer settlements whether anyone had seen her son. I had left her name with Hasan as well. She did not ask me to promise that I would return with news.

At Laila's yard the receiving board hung beneath the eaves. A new worker was lifting baskets onto a dry shelf. Laila saw me from the doorway. She came out with a small cloth bundle.

"For the men who carried the jars," she said. "Their payment was short yesterday. Take it to Hasan on your way."

I almost thought she had brought food for me. I took the bundle and felt the weight of coin inside.

"Are you coming to the landing?" I asked.

"I have grain arriving before dark."

"I understand."

She looked toward the river.

"I hope the people you find will listen," she said.

"So do I."

For a moment I wanted to tell her that I might return when I had settled everything. I had no way to know what that meant. I gave her back the words she had given me.

"If I come back, I will tell you before I ask for a place."

She nodded. Then she went inside to check the baskets.

I delivered the bundle to Hasan. The men who had carried the jars would be paid. I had no reason left to walk the yard again.

Sulad was aboard Harun's boat, sitting beside the line he had mended. Isan stood near the steersman, listening while he named the first stopping place. Neither rose when I stepped onto the deck. They had work to finish.

"Did the ruler agree?" Sulad asked.

"He did."

"To what?"

I told him. He listened until I had named the limits of the letter. He asked whether I had paid for the replacement jar. I said I had.

"You will need money when we land," he said.

"I know."

"You say that often now."

I laughed. He did not. He reached for the line and drew it through his hands once more.

Ruma's boat passed behind us, returning from the upper bank. She raised a hand to Harun. He answered. She did not stop. Safi stood on the watch platform at the outer landing, counting the vessels that went past. He had his own boat to send out when the tide turned. He did not come aboard to bless our departure.

Harun asked whether I had brought my gear. I showed him the small bundle at my feet. He lifted it, judged its weight, and put it beside the others.

"Then we can go," he said.

The steersman waited for Harun's order. The crew had the lines in their hands. I looked back toward the houses, the yards, the palace roofs beyond them. Somewhere inside those streets were people whose names I had learned because they needed something from me. They would continue to need it after I was gone. I had left their questions with people who could answer them. That was the most I had done.

Harun asked me where I wanted the first message sent if we reached the northern coast.

"To the people who still know my family," I said. "Ask who holds the river. Ask who can receive us. Tell them I have come to speak."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then we learn why."

He studied me, then turned to his steersman.

"Cast off," he said.

The first line came free. The boat moved a little, held by the second. Tamin's vessel was already turning in the current. Harun called again. The second line dropped into the water, and the crew drew it aboard before it could catch beneath the hull.

I stood with Sulad near the stern. I had not given the order to move Harun's boat. I had chosen to be on it when he did.

The houses of Burné passed slowly at first. Men were calling from the landings. Smoke rose from the kitchens. A child ran along the bank until the planks ended and stopped with both arms raised. Farther out, the river widened. The boats that had gone before us were small dark marks on the water.

Sulad looked north. I looked with him.

Maynila was somewhere beyond the coast and the islands, beyond the names Harun's steersman could give us. I had left it believing I knew what belonged to me. I was returning with a letter that asked to be heard and men who had agreed to travel only so far.

The wind filled the sail. Harun's boat leaned and gathered speed. Burné began to disappear behind the bend.
