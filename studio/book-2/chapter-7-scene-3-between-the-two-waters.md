# Book II Chapter 7 — Scene 3: Between the Two Waters

**Studio prose, unpublished.** Ache first person. The boat positions, signal customs and named crews are fictional. This scene approaches the 29 July Brunei alarm without claiming a historical fleet count, local flotilla identity, or exact European ship maneuver.

---

The seam held when Ruma poured the first bowl of water over it.

She poured a second. I watched the line where the new binding disappeared between the planks. A drop formed, hung, and fell into the hull. Ruma waited. No second drop followed.

"Will you sail?" I asked.

"I will load it."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I can promise."

She told two men to lift the water jar back aboard. One went to fetch a wooden wedge. The other asked whether the jar should be put where it had been.

"No," she said. "I moved it because the weight was wrong. Do you want to make the same hole twice?"

The man set it nearer the middle. She shifted it again by a hand's breadth and made him test whether it would move when the boat leaned. He pushed with both palms. It stayed.

I had been ready to call the seam repaired as soon as the first bowl ran off the outside. Ruma had been testing the whole boat.

Tahir came to the landing carrying the smaller message he had agreed to take. Hasan's clerk had wrapped it in oiled cloth and tied a second cord around the first. Tahir asked me to read the mark on the outer covering. I could recognize the carrier's sign but not the full account inside.

"Then do not tell me what it says," he said.

"I was going to tell you whose message it was."

"That I know."

He placed it under a cover where it would stay dry and asked whether I still intended to leave with the tide.

"That depends on the passage," I said.

"It always depends on the passage. Who is watching it?"

I had been to see the foreign ships. I had spoken with a watchman and merchants. I had not asked who would give the boats leaving our landing a safe way through the traffic.

We walked to the end of the boards. Beyond the houses, the tall spars stood over the harbor. I could see a broad sail on a local junk drawing air, then collapsing when the vessel turned. Several small craft were passing behind it, too close for their owners to be pleased.

"I will ask the harbor watch," I said.

"Ask before my men lift another basket."

I went with Safi to the watch post. Jamil was already there, speaking to a man whose shirt was wet across the back. The man had been sent from the outer anchorage. He said the junks had shifted closer to the strangers during the night. Their owners were waiting for a place to unload. Some said they were there to protect their goods. Others said they had been ordered to remain.

"Ordered by whom?" Jamil asked.

"I did not see an order."

"Then tell us who told you."

The man named a harbor officer. Jamil sent a runner to fetch him.

Safi asked about the boats coming from the far side of the water. The man said they had been gathering since morning. Some carried armed men. Others had baskets and rolled mats. He had not spoken to their leaders.

"Have they threatened the foreign ships?" Safi asked.

"Not where I could hear."

"Have the foreign ships threatened them?"

"They shout when a boat gets close."

"Have they struck anyone?"

"Not that I saw."

The watchman held his palms apart as though we had asked him to measure a thing that would not stay still.

Jamil took us to a higher platform above the harbor. The ruler's men used it to watch arrivals and departures. From there I could see more of the water, but I could also see how much of it lay hidden behind sails and roofs.

The foreign ships were farther apart than I had thought from the landing. Between them lay an open stretch of water crossed by small craft. Behind the nearer hull, several junks had anchored in a loose line. Beyond those, the harbor opened toward the outer passage.

Ruma's boat was a small shape near the inner shore. I found it by the pale cover over her jars. Tahir's was beside it. A third owner, Banu, had brought a shallow vessel with a load of goods that belonged to several merchants. He had agreed to carry them only as far as the next trading stop. He had not agreed to wait indefinitely while I looked at foreign ships.

"Can they pass behind the junks?" I asked the watchman.

"At low water, perhaps. There are shoals."

"Could they go outside the foreign ships?"

"They could. Whether they should is another question."

I looked at the passage again. I had navigated crowded water before. I knew how a boat moved when a current caught its stern. I could not see the bottom beneath that water, or the line of stones that the watchman said lay near the far side. The familiar shape of the harbor did not make every path through it safe.

Jamil asked what I intended to do.

"Hold the message boats at the inner landing until the outer passage clears."

"Who will tell them?"

"I will."

"And if one owner decides to leave?"

"I will ask him to wait."

"Ask him why he is leaving first."

I looked at him. He was not smiling.

"Ruma's boat has been delayed by a seam," I said. "Tahir is carrying a message that can wait one tide. Banu has cargo that he must deliver by the next market day. He may not wait."

"Then you know three reasons," Jamil said. "Now find out what each owner chooses."

He sent me down with Safi. We found Banu already moving his load.

"Where are you taking it?" I asked.

"To the far landing. I will wait there for an opening."

"The passage is crowded."

"I have eyes."

"There are shoals beyond the junks."

"I know where the shoals are. I was carrying goods here before you learned to count them."

His crew had lifted two baskets from the boat and were carrying them along the shore path. He had decided to move the load by land to another vessel rather than risk the passage. I had mistaken activity for an attempt to sail.

"Will you still carry the goods onward?" I asked.

"From the far landing, if the owner there agrees. I have already paid for the carrying."

I wanted to tell him that the message boats ought to remain together. He had not agreed to carry a message, and the goods were not mine.

"Then I will put your name on the new account," I said.

"Put the other owner's name there too."

He went to help the men with the baskets.

Ruma said she would wait. Tahir said he would wait if Hasan's clerk accepted that the message would leave on the next tide. The clerk did. We sent a runner to Jamil with the three answers.

Safi had been watching the outer water while I spoke. He told me a group of boats had moved closer to the junks. Their sails were furled; men worked poles along their sides. Another group remained farther out. I could not tell whether they were joining or passing.

"Do you think they mean to fight?" I asked.

"Some of them carry weapons."

"So do we."

"That is why I did not answer."

He called to a man on the watch boat and asked him to carry a warning to the inner landings: keep clear of the junks until the passage was opened. The watch boat's owner refused to go between the foreign ships and the anchored vessels.

"I will go along the shore," he said. "The men at the far landing can pass the word."

"It will take longer," I said.

"It will reach them."

Safi agreed. He did not ask the man to prove his courage by taking the shorter route.

I thought of the man at Laoe who had refused to sail until his brother's name was accounted for. We had called the delay costly. We had been able to leave only because the man finally chose to board. The water did not owe us a quick answer because our business was important.

A small craft came in from the outer harbor. Its rowers were sweating, and the man at its bow called for the watchman. The boats beyond the junks were approaching in separate groups, he said. One of the foreign ships had sent men to its deck and gathered its lines. He had heard shouting, but he had not seen a weapon fired.

Jamil came down to the landing to hear him. He listened without interrupting, then asked whether the foreign ship had raised sail.

"Not when I left."

"How long ago?"

The man looked toward the sun and gave a time that could only be an estimate.

I watched Jamil decide what to do with an answer that had already begun to grow old.

"Keep the inner boats where they are," he told me. "Safi, take the watch boat along the shore and tell the outer posts not to let traffic press toward the anchored junks. If the strangers want room, give them room."

Safi went at once. He did not wait for me to approve the order.

I stayed beside Ruma's boat while the men finished lashing its load. A breeze came down the channel and moved the loose end of the cover. Tahir's messenger sat on a basket, holding the wrapped letter between his knees. He had been ready to leave since morning.

"Are we going?" he asked.

"Not yet."

"Will the message be late?"

"It will be later than we hoped."

He looked down at the cloth. "That is not the same thing."

I had no answer.

A cry came from the outer landing. Then another. People were pointing beyond the junks. The nearer foreign ship had begun to move. Its bundled cloth opened high above the deck, taking the wind in a shape I had not seen before. The great hull turned slowly, then faster, and the water broke white along its side.

For a moment I admired the skill of it. Men on that ship had found a way to make so much weight obey a sail.

Then I saw where its bow was pointing.

The junks lay between it and the inner harbor, and our small boats were waiting on the shore behind them. The stranger was moving into the same water that we had spent the morning trying to keep clear.
