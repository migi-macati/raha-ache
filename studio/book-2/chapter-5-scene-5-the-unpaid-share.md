# Scene 5 — The Unpaid Share

In the morning the six rolls were still beneath the raised house. I knew because the two watchers had woken the older woman before the first light and made her count them.

She came down carrying a bowl of water and asked why men who had spent the night guarding cloth could not count it without her.

"Because one of them says the other moved a roll," Saliha told me.

"Did he?"

"He says he heard something. Neither says he saw it."

The older woman lifted the door covering. All six rolls lay where we had left them, but one had been turned. Its binding was wet on the side that had faced the floor. The watcher chosen by Yusuf said he had shifted it away from a drip. Rafiq's cousin said the man had moved it after everyone slept.

"Then you should have woken me," the older woman said.

"Would you have come?" Rafiq's cousin asked.

She gave him the bowl. "I am here now."

No one laughed. She sent both watchers outside while she inspected the mat and the knots. I had made six marks the evening before. They told me nothing about who had touched the sixth roll.

The men who had been repairing Saliha's roof were already at work. They had put the thatch over the frame before dark, but a corner remained open. The older woman looked up at it each time the wind moved. She said we could have her dry room for one more day if we paid again. After that she wanted her space back.

Yusuf was not at the house. He had gone to look for Karim.

Rafiq's kinsman returned with a witness before Yusuf came back. The man had carried goods on the earlier voyage, though not for Rafiq's uncle. He had a scar along one shin and stopped to rub it before stepping onto the raised floor.

His name was Danu. Saliha knew him. She asked him to sit and gave him water. Rafiq wanted him to speak at once.

"Tell them where the two rolls went."

Danu drank first.

"I saw four taken from the boat at Karim's landing," he said. "I saw two still under the cover when I went ashore."

"Who was with them?" I asked.

"Men of the boat."

"Did you see those two brought off later?"

"No."

Rafiq looked toward Yusuf's watcher. "You hear?"

Danu turned to him. "I saw four carried ashore. I did not say your uncle took the others."

Rafiq's mouth tightened. His uncle had been accused of taking cloth because he had been among the last to handle the cargo. Danu could not clear him, but neither could he say he had seen him steal.

Saliha brought out the cord with three knots. "My brother was told the loss would be made good."

"By whom?" Danu asked.

"Yusuf's man."

"Was Karim there?"

"No."

"Then Karim may say he never heard it."

"He has said so already," she answered.

I made a separate mark for each man's statement. The new witness had not given us the missing rolls. He had given us a place where they had last been seen.

Jalal came from the house with the unfinished roof, carrying a length of thatch on his shoulder. He set it down when he saw Danu.

"My aunt wants her room back," he said. "She has kept other people's goods all night while the roof was open."

"Then help her finish it," Saliha said.

"I was doing that."

"You put down the thatch to come here."

He picked it up again. He carried it to the men at the roof without looking at me.

I followed because I had begun to understand that the argument was not only happening beneath the raised house. The two roof workers had lost a day of work on another dwelling. One said he could no longer finish that work before the owner returned. He would have to give back the rice he had taken in advance, or find someone else to do it.

"Yusuf has paid you for carrying," I said.

"He paid us for six rolls. We carried six rolls. The roof did not finish itself."

I asked what it would cost him to make up the lost day. He named an amount of rice. I had less than that left in my food parcel.

"Could I work with you?" I asked.

He looked at my hands. "Can you bind thatch?"

"I can learn."

"Then you will be slow. Slow work is another cost."

He went back to the roof. I stayed long enough to hold the loose edge while he fastened it. He corrected my grip twice. I could feel the wet fibers slipping under my fingers.

When Karim arrived, he did not come alone. Two men carried a basket of fish behind him. He greeted the older woman first, and she told him to take his quarrel out of her house before noon.

Karim was older than Jalal, with gray in his beard and a careful way of stepping over the planks. He listened to Saliha's account, then to Rafiq's, then asked Yusuf whether he had brought the goods that were promised.

"Six rolls," Yusuf said. "All counted."

"Then I will receive six."

"Two are claimed against the old loss," Saliha said.

Karim looked at her cord. "That was between your brother and Yusuf's man."

"My brother carried for you."

"He carried to my landing."

"Then pay him for the carrying."

"I paid the men who brought the load."

"Not Rafiq's uncle," Rafiq said.

Karim turned to him. "Your uncle left before the account was settled."

"He left because you held his share."

"He was named when the goods went missing."

"Named by whom?"

Karim did not answer. He asked for Danu's testimony. Danu repeated what he had seen, no more. Karim said that four rolls had been received and that two were missing. Yusuf said the two could not be his responsibility because his man had put six aboard. Saliha asked why her brother had been promised replacement cloth if no one admitted a loss.

The words came faster. I had heard them all before, except that Karim now stood in the middle of them.

Jalal asked whether Karim would release the unpaid carrying share to Rafiq's uncle.

"When the missing goods are accounted for."

"His hands carried the four you received," Jalal said.

"Then let him come and say so."

Rafiq stepped forward. "He will not stand before you while you call him a thief."

"I have not called him that."

"You have kept his pay."

Karim's two men put down the basket of fish. One moved closer to him. Rafiq's kinsmen stood. Their spears remained against the house, but I saw where their hands went.

Samad had come up from the water. He took one look at them and called Tarin from the boat. Tarin did not come ashore. He stood where he could release the line.

"If this becomes a fight," Samad told Yusuf, "my crew will leave the goods. I will not wait for men to decide who was struck first."

Yusuf asked Karim to take all six rolls and settle the older quarrel afterward. Karim agreed at once. Rafiq said his kinsmen would stop the rolls before they reached the house. Saliha said she would not ask her carriers to cross between them.

I looked at the six rolls. Their bindings were the same color. I had made the count myself. I could have handed Yusuf the list and let him say I had seen all that mattered.

"Could one roll stay here?" I asked.

Karim stared at me. "For whom?"

"Until the earlier loss is heard with the people who were aboard. Not given to anyone. Held here under the same watchers."

"That makes me pay for a loss I deny."

"It keeps five moving."

Saliha shook her head. "I claimed two."

"I know."

"And you would leave one."

"I cannot make him give two."

"Nor can you make him give one," Karim said.

He was right. I looked at Yusuf. He had brought me to count his goods, and now I was asking him to surrender use of one of them.

"If you leave one under watch," I said, "the five can go to Karim today. If the witnesses cannot agree, the sixth stays until a new term is made. It is still yours to claim. I will write that."

Yusuf looked toward the boat. "I have already paid for waiting."

"You will pay again if no one carries the cloth."

Karim said he would accept five only if the remaining roll was counted against no debt until the hearing. Saliha said she would allow five to move only if the sixth stayed and her brother's claim was read aloud before the witnesses. Rafiq demanded the pay owed to his uncle for the four rolls received on the earlier voyage.

Karim refused.

"Then your men can carry today's cloth themselves," Saliha said.

The carriers put down their baskets. I saw Jalal look from them to Karim. He did not speak for them.

Karim turned to his two men. They looked at the six rolls, then at the planks. One had a sore hand wrapped in cloth. The other said he would not carry all five without help.

The bargaining took longer than the quarrel. Karim agreed to release part of the older carrying share from his own stores, with the rest still disputed. Rafiq accepted the part on behalf of his uncle only after Danu said aloud that he had seen the uncle work on that voyage. The payment did not clear the accusation. Rafiq insisted I mark both facts.

Saliha agreed that her carriers would move five rolls. She made Yusuf pay them before they lifted the first. The sixth would remain beneath the raised house until the people who had made the earlier promise were heard. Karim's receiver and Rafiq's cousin would watch it, subject to the older woman's permission. The older woman agreed to one more night and named a new price. She refused to guarantee the cloth itself.

Then she asked who would finish her roof.

Yusuf said he had paid for carrying, storage, watchers, and his crew's delay. He had no reason to pay for a roof.

"You took the workers from it," Saliha said.

"They chose to carry."

"Because you offered pay."

"And I paid it."

The roof worker who had spoken to me earlier stood beside the unfinished corner. He was waiting for an answer about the rice he owed elsewhere. No one offered one.

I took the remainder of my food from the boat. Tarin made me count it in front of him, because some had been set aside for the return passage. I told him I would leave it with the workers and stay to help finish the roof.

"You will have nothing to eat when we sail," he said.

"I know."

"Then do not come aboard and ask for ours."

"I won't."

Samad heard us. He said he would not carry a passenger who could not feed himself, and he would not keep his boat waiting for my generosity.

"I understand," I said.

I had imagined the sacrifice before I made it. In my imagination the workers had been grateful. The man at the roof took the food, counted it, and told me it did not replace the day he had lost. He would take my labor too, if I could follow instructions.

Jalal came to stand beside me.

"You could have asked me for rice," he said.

"You offered to pay the carriers yesterday."

"And they asked what I would pay them tomorrow."

"Yes."

He looked at the roof. "My aunt will not thank either of us for promising her other people's time."

He went to help the men bind the open corner. I followed.

By afternoon five rolls had crossed the planks under the eyes of people who did not agree on their ownership. The sixth stayed behind. Rafiq had received a part of his uncle's unpaid share. Saliha had a witness to the promise made to her brother, but no replacement for the missing two rolls. Karim had five rolls and a public account he disliked. Yusuf had less cloth to sell and another day of costs.

I had given away the food that would have taken me home. I had thought it would feel like settling something. My fingers were cut by the binding fiber, and the man on the roof kept telling me I had tied it too loose.

When Karim passed below us, he looked up.

"Your marks will be carried back to Burné," he said. "Do not make them say I agreed that anything was stolen."

"I won't."

"Then write that I did not."

He went on without waiting for an answer. Jalal watched him go.

"He will remember you," Jalal said.

"For keeping one roll here?"

"For making him say in front of his people that he could not settle it alone."

The worker beside me pulled at the binding I had made. It came loose in his hand.

"Again," he said.

I began again.
