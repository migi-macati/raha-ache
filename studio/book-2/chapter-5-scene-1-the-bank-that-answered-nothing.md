# Scene 1 — The Bank That Answered Nothing

I had been given a clean piece of wood for the names of the missing. By morning I had rubbed it so often with my thumb that the edge was smooth.

There was only one name on it.

Bakar had agreed to take his boat to the lower bank, where a man who tended fish traps said he might have heard shouting the day Hamzah's boat was taken. He had not agreed to search the inner passage. He had not agreed to take me.

I waited while he and Tuma loaded three jars. The man who owned them had come down twice to ask when they would reach the outer landing. He was there a third time when Bakar saw me.

"If you have another route for us," Bakar said, "you can tell the owner."

"I have no route."

"Then what do you want?"

"Jamil said I could ask whether you would take me."

"He said you could ask?"

"Yes."

Bakar turned to the jar owner. "You heard him. He has come to ask. I have not said yes."

The owner put a hand on the nearest jar. "Will he make us late?"

"He might."

"Then he can stay here."

I wanted to say that we were looking for a man who had been taken or drowned, and that three jars could wait. I knew Bakar would ask me whether I owned them. The owner was already watching my mouth.

"I will not ask you to stop," I said. "If you have to pass the bank to make your delivery, I can look from the boat."

"And if you see something?"

"I will tell you."

"You will not tell me to turn?"

"No."

Tuma lowered the last jar into its bed of folded matting. He had heard enough to look at me without smiling.

"He can hold the cover down," he said. "It keeps lifting at the corner."

Bakar inspected the cover, though he had tied it himself. Then he pointed to a place between the jars.

"Sit there. If I tell you to move, move."

I climbed aboard. The jar owner watched until we were clear of the post. I could still see him standing with his hands on his hips when the houses hid the landing.

The water was lower than it had been the morning we lost Hamzah's boat. Mud showed on the roots along the banks. Tuma took the forward pole. Bakar steered us past a line of stakes set out from a house, then held the boat away from a shallow place where two children were gathering shellfish. I had seen the stakes before, but I had never known how far they ran beneath the water.

I kept looking for a place where a man might have climbed ashore.

"Not every opening is a landing," Bakar said.

"I know."

"Then stop leaning across the jars to count them."

I sat back. The corner of the cover lifted. I caught it before the wind could turn it over.

At the outer landing the buyers were not ready. One had gone to find the man who would carry the jars uphill. The other argued that the clay had been left in the sun too long and should cost less. Bakar waited while the owner of the jars, who had come by another boat, argued back. Tuma went to find drinking water. No one asked me to help settle the price.

I took the clean wood from my belt and looked at the single name.

Nasir's sister had said that if we learned where he was, we were to tell her ourselves. She had not asked us to bring back a story. I wondered what I would say if Bakar returned without going to the place where the man at the fish traps had heard voices.

The jar owner came over while I was still looking at the wood.

"You wrote my three jars on that?"

"No."

"Then what?"

I showed him Nasir's name. He read nothing from the mark, but he recognized the space I had left beside it.

"The boy from Hamzah's boat?"

"Yes."

"I heard he had gone into the trees."

"Who told you?"

"Someone at the market."

"Who?"

He frowned. "Do you want my jars or my memory?"

"Your memory."

"Then it is worth no more than that. I did not see him."

He went back to the argument. I made no mark beside Nasir's name.

When the jars had been carried off, Bakar asked Tuma how much water remained. Tuma showed him the jar he had filled. They spoke about the tide, the time needed to reach the fish traps, and whether the buyers might have another load waiting. Only when they had finished did Bakar look at me.

"We can go as far as the outer bank," he said. "Not beyond it."

"Thank you."

"You have not heard what it costs me yet."

I had thought the trip was already being made. I looked toward the empty place where the jars had stood.

"There is another load I could have taken before the water fell," Bakar said. "I will miss it if we go."

"I can pay you for the time."

"With what?"

I named what I had. It was less than he would have earned.

"Keep it," he said. "I want you to know what I am leaving."

He pushed us off. I could not tell whether he meant to be kind.

The man at the traps was mending a torn basket beneath a tree. He had heard shouting on the afternoon of the attack, but he had not seen Hamzah's boat. He said there had been two voices, perhaps three. One had called from the water. He had gone to the bank and found nothing but a broken branch caught against the roots.

"Was it a man calling for help?" I asked.

"It was a man calling."

"Could you tell which way he went?"

"I could tell where the sound came from. That is not where a man went."

I asked whether he had heard a name. He shook his head.

"Did you see another boat?"

"I saw a boat after the shouting stopped. It was far off."

"Was it the one that took Hamzah?"

"How would I know?"

I felt Bakar watching me. I wanted to ask whether the boat had been large, whether men had stood in it with weapons, whether it had come from the cut. The fisherman had already told us what he saw. I had been about to give him the rest of the picture and ask him to agree.

Tuma moved along the bank, lifting leaves where the water had laid them against the roots. He found a strip of cloth caught beneath a branch. It was dark with mud, and one edge had been torn.

"Nasir wore a cloth at his waist," I said.

"So did the men who carried your jars," Tuma answered.

He washed the strip and held it out. There was no mark on it. Bakar told him to leave it on a dry branch where the people who used the bank could see it.

"Should we take it to his sister?" I asked.

"And tell her what?" Bakar said.

"That we found it near the place where someone heard shouting."

"You can tell her that without carrying it away. If it belongs to a man here, he may come looking for it."

The fisherman had gone back to his basket. He listened while I repeated his account: voices after midday, a boat later, no person seen, no direction known. He corrected me once. The boat had passed before the light began to fall, he said. He could not tell whether it was before or after the shouting.

I scratched a small mark on the wood for the account, leaving Nasir's name untouched.

On the way back I watched the strip of cloth until the bend hid it. The bank seemed crowded with places where a man might have stood and been unseen. I knew that was not the same as evidence that he had stood there.

At the landing Nasir's sister was waiting with a basket of fish. She had brought them to sell and had stayed after the buyers went. Her hands smelled of the water. I had expected her to be watching the river. She was watching us.

"Did you find him?" she asked.

"No."

"What did you find?"

I told her about the man at the traps. I said he heard voices and saw a boat, but he could not place them together. I told her where Tuma had left the cloth and why we had not brought it. I did not tell her that I had almost taken it.

"Did you ask him if he knew my brother's voice?"

I looked at Bakar. He did not answer for me.

"No," I said.

She tightened her grip on the basket.

"Then you will have to ask him another time. Nasir used to shout across the water when he came home. The men at the traps might have heard him before."

I had asked the fisherman so many questions that I had thought there were none left. Hers was the first that could have changed what we knew.

"I will go back," I said.

"You will ask whether someone can take you back," she corrected. "That is what you told me yesterday."

"Yes."

She looked at Bakar. "I can pay for a man to carry a question if your boat has work. You need not lose another load for it."

Bakar said he would ask the fisherman when next he passed. He did not promise a day. She accepted that, and asked him to send the answer to her stall rather than through Jamil's house.

After she had gone, I went to Hasan with the wood. He listened while I gave him the account and asked me to repeat which words were the fisherman's and which were mine. When I had finished, he laid the wood beside the other strips.

A merchant was waiting at the doorway. He had a folded list of loads and a dispute with a man who said two bundles belonged to him. He had come to ask Hasan for someone who could keep the names apart on a voyage beyond Burné. The men who owned the goods wanted a witness who would not become their debtor merely by writing for them.

Hasan said he could not spare a man from the work of hearing Hamzah's owners.

"What about the prince?" the merchant asked, looking at me.

I felt my face warm. It was the first time that day anyone had called me that.

"He is not carrying my instructions," Hasan said. "And he cannot promise anything in Jamil's name."

"Can he count?"

"He can make a count and tell you when it is uncertain."

The merchant looked at the strip of wood in my hand. "Will he tell me when it is uncertain?"

I thought of the torn cloth, of the boat no one could place in time, of Nasir's sister asking the one question I had missed.

"If I do not know," I said, "I will say so."

"That is easy here," he answered. "Ask me again when a man has put a knife beside the goods."

He left his list with Hasan. He would return the next day, after speaking to the owners and the crew. No place had been promised to me. I did not even know whether the men who worked his boat would want another passenger.

Hasan gave me a second piece of wood for the disputed bundles. I placed it beside the one with Nasir's name. On the first, there was a mark for a voice that might have been his. On the second, two men claimed the same goods.

I had once thought a name written down could make a thing certain.

I began by leaving space beside both.
