# Scene 6 — The Price of Passage

I slept beneath the roof I had helped bind. The older woman allowed it because I had carried the loose thatch inside before the rain, and because I was not one of the men guarding the cloth.

The rain came before dawn. It entered at the corner we had not finished. A drop struck my cheek, then another. I lay listening until the worker beside me sat up and told me to move the mat.

"We will have to lift that edge again," he said.

I had spent the afternoon tying it. He had spent the afternoon untying what I tied.

When the light came, the sixth roll was dry. The older woman inspected it before she looked at her roof. Rafiq's cousin and Karim's receiver had both stayed awake for part of the night. Each claimed the other had slept longer. She told them that their quarrel had not changed the count.

"Six yesterday, one here now," she said. "If you want to argue about sleep, do it after you carry the water."

She put a jar in each man's hands. Neither refused.

I had kept a copy of Yusuf's account on a thin board. The five rolls delivered to Karim had been marked separately from the one still held. Beneath them were the rice and work owed for carrying, the payment made toward Rafiq's uncle's share, and the cost of keeping the last roll under cover. I had not written that the old debt was settled.

The older woman looked at the marks, then asked where her payment for the second night appeared.

I showed her.

"That is the payment Yusuf agreed to give," she said. "Where is the payment I received?"

"He has not brought it yet."

"Then you have written a promise where there should be a payment."

I scraped the mark away. I had been proud of how neatly I had divided the account. The place where I rubbed it was rougher than the rest.

Yusuf arrived with Samad as the workers began repairing the leaking corner. Yusuf had brought rice for the room, but less than the older woman had named. He said the fifth roll had been received late, and Karim's people had delayed his own payment. The older woman asked whether her house had also arrived late.

Samad waited while they settled the difference. He had already told Tarin to make the boat ready at the next usable water. The crew had been paid for the delay, and he would not promise another one.

"Are you coming?" he asked me.

"I gave the food away."

"I know. Tarin told me."

"I can work on the return."

"You worked on the outward passage. You still ate."

I had no answer. I had thought he might let me return because I had been useful to Yusuf. He did not say I had been useless. He said the food had been counted before we left Burné, and that he would not take it from his men to correct my decision.

Yusuf stood beside us, listening. I asked whether he would pay for my place.

"You have cost me a roll of cloth," he said.

"It is still yours to claim."

"It is not in my hands."

"Nor is it in Rafiq's."

"Do you think that comforts me?"

I told him I had made two accounts, one for the people here and one to carry back. He asked to see the second. He read it slowly, moving his finger along the marks. When he reached the part about Karim's payment to Rafiq's uncle, he stopped.

"That is not a payment I made."

"I wrote that Karim released it."

"And the cloth?"

"Five received by Karim. One held under the older woman's roof, with both sides choosing watchers. Ownership not decided."

Yusuf handed the board back. "You will make me answer for that in Burné."

"You sent the goods."

"I sent you too."

I thought of saying that he had brought me because two disputed bundles had been left behind and there was room. It was true, but it would not make the return fare appear. He had given me a place when I needed one. I had helped make his voyage longer and less profitable. Both things could be true.

Samad asked whether I wanted him to take the account to Hasan. I told him Hasan had agreed to receive reports through Yusuf's returning carrier, but I did not know when that man would next pass the landing. Samad said he could carry a copy himself, if he reached Burné before the carrier.

"Who will know that it came from you?" he asked.

I asked Saliha and Rafiq to hear the account before it went. They came with the older woman. Karim sent his receiver instead of coming himself. Yusuf stood beside Samad. I read every line aloud, including the part where the witness Danu had seen four rolls brought ashore on the earlier voyage but had not seen what became of the other two.

Rafiq interrupted when I said his uncle had received part of the carrying share.

"He has not received it," Rafiq said. "I have. I must take it to him."

I changed the line.

Saliha interrupted when I said her brother had been promised replacement cloth.

"He says he was promised it," she told me. "You did not hear the promise."

I changed that too.

Karim's receiver would not confirm that Karim owed anything for the earlier loss. He confirmed only that Karim had accepted five of the new rolls and had agreed that the sixth could remain under watch until the earlier witnesses were heard. The older woman confirmed the room and its price. She would not say the goods were safe from theft. She had not promised that.

I read the account again. It took longer. No one thanked me.

When I had finished, Samad asked each person whether the words describing their own actions were accurate. The receiver said he would repeat them to Karim. Rafiq said his uncle might still reject the terms. Saliha said her brother might too. The older woman said she wanted the room cleared before the next market day.

I made two copies on separate pieces of wood. They were not identical. One stayed with Saliha and the older woman, because they needed the names of those who would return for the last roll. The other went with Samad for Hasan. I read the differences aloud so that no one would later say I had hidden them. Samad made me tie his copy inside the covered basket where the crew kept dry things. He would not carry it loose against his body.

"Hasan may ask you questions," he said.

"I told Jamil I would answer."

"Then find a way to get back when he asks."

He turned toward the water.

I followed him as far as the planks. Tarin was moving the last water jar into place. He saw me and pointed to a rope lying across the boards.

"Do not stand on that."

I stepped aside. I had learned at least that much.

Yusuf came down after us. He told Samad that he expected the boat to return with the remaining goods. Samad told him which stops he would make if the water allowed it. Yusuf asked whether he would take a different route to save time. Samad said he would decide after speaking with the pilot.

I watched the exchange without offering a shorter passage.

When the boat moved away, Yusuf was aboard and I was on shore. The men at the mast and steering place looked toward the water, not toward me. Tarin raised a hand once, then returned to his work. I had come east to learn what I could do without Jamil's order. I had learned that I could lose my passage without anyone taking it from me.

Jalal found me carrying the empty baskets back to Saliha's house.

"You have stayed," he said.

"I could not pay for the return."

"That is one reason to stay."

"Do you have another?"

"My aunt says the roof still leaks. She has work for you if the men will let you help."

"She has already had my work."

"Then you know what it is worth."

He took two baskets from me and carried them to the house. I had expected him to tell me that I could travel with his people, or that he would speak for me before someone important. He did neither.

When we reached the shade, I asked whether his kin sent boats farther along the islands. He said they did when the owners had goods to carry and reasons to go.

"Would they take me?"

"Where?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then how could they agree?"

I told him I had wanted to see the coasts beyond this landing. I did not tell him that I had once imagined returning to Burné with men who would follow my orders. He had already seen me lose the place I had bought for myself.

"You have spoken to my aunt," Jalal said. "You have spoken to Saliha. You have spoken to Rafiq. You have not spoken to the men who own those boats."

"I could ask."

"You could. They might say no."

"Would you speak for me?"

"About what?"

"That I kept the account."

"Yes. I saw that."

"That I did not let the cloth be taken."

He stopped walking.

"One roll is still under my aunt's roof. Karim has five. Rafiq has part of his uncle's pay. Saliha's brother has no replacement. Which taking did you prevent?"

I wanted to answer that no one had been struck. It was the one thing I had feared most when the spear-bearers came. But the workers had lost time, Yusuf had lost the use of a roll, and Karim had made it plain he would remember me. I had prevented a fight that might never have happened. I could not count it as though it had.

"I don't know," I said.

Jalal waited. He did not help me finish.

"I kept them talking long enough to make a partial bargain."

"That is something you can tell the boat owners."

"And will you help?"

"I will tell them what I saw. I will not promise their boats."

He looked toward the raised house. "Karim is my kin through people who do not like being named in public. You made him answer in front of workers. He will say you brought an old quarrel into a new bargain."

"Was the old quarrel not already here?"

"It was. That will not make him like you."

"And you?"

He shifted the baskets in his arms. "I think you listen better when someone can put you ashore."

I was ready with a reply. I let it go. He smiled a little, then turned toward the roof.

The older woman had found another leak. She stood beneath it with her hand raised, feeling for water. The workers had gone to fetch fresh binding fiber. She asked me to climb up and hold the thatch until they returned.

I did. The edge was wet and heavy. From the roof I could see the river beyond the planks, Samad's boat already a small shape against the farther shore. I could also see the raised room where the sixth roll lay beneath two watchers' eyes.

Saliha came with a new piece of fiber. She asked for my account board, the one that would remain here. I handed it down. She checked the marks for the carrier's payment, then asked where I had recorded the labor still owed on the roof.

"I have not," I said.

"Then leave room."

She made a mark in the blank space herself, where she could show the workers what had been promised. I watched her do it. It was the last mark on the board, and it was not mine.

I held the thatch in place until she told me to let go.
