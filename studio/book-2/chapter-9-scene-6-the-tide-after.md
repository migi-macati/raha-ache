# Book II, Chapter 9 — Scene 6: The Tide After

**Studio draft. Not reader-facing.** Ache POV. A narrow, owner-approved inquiry departs; no general command or guaranteed Luzon passage. Chapter 10 retains Ache's consequential choice between Brunei and Luzon.

---

Three mornings passed before the boat to the traps was ready.

On the first, its owner found another wet seam after the hull had been lowered. On the second, the man who was to row with him was called to help carry a sick child to his mother's house. On the third, the resin held when they pushed the boat into the shallows and let it rest there until the sun stood above the palms.

I had gone to the landing each morning. I had learned to ask whether the boat was ready before asking whether it would go.

Nasir's sister came on the third day with a small board wrapped in cloth. She had written no words herself. The marks were those she had made with the boy and his mother to distinguish the first inquiry from the second. She carried a little packet of dried fish for the rowers and a separate portion for the man at the traps if he agreed to speak.

"Where is Hasan's man?" she asked.

The boatman stood beside his owner, testing the line at the bow. He came when he heard her voice.

"You have been paid for one question," she said.

"One journey," he corrected. "I will ask what you have given me to ask. If the man is away, I will not go farther without another agreement."

"That is fair."

She took a thin strip of wood from the cloth and held it before him. The boy who had carried her earlier questions stood at her side. He had come because he knew the path to the traps and could name the households along it, though the owner had not agreed to carry him.

"Tell me what you will ask," she said.

The boatman repeated the question he had heard from Hasan: whether the trap keeper had heard a voice at the cut before the attack or after it. He added that he would ask whether the man saw anyone there.

"Do not add the second question as though he has already seen someone," she said. "Ask what he saw, if anything. Then ask what he heard and when."

The boatman nodded. She made him repeat it. He did, this time without putting a person into the place where only a voice had been reported.

"Who will hear the answer?" she asked.

"My cousin. He is rowing."

"Will you bring the answer to Hasan?"

"To you first, if you are here when we return. Then to Hasan, with the same words."

She looked at the boy. "Listen. If he returns when I am at market, you will bring him to me. Do not tell me the answer yourself before he does."

The boy agreed.

The owner came forward and asked for the packet of food. He opened it and divided the fish into two portions, one for each rower. Nasir's sister told him the smaller packet was for the witness. He wrapped it again and set it beneath the seat.

I had brought a second board. On it Hasan's clerk had marked, at my request, the names of people whose families still sought an answer from the outer banks. The older woman's son was among them. I had asked her whether she wanted the question carried on this journey. She had said she wanted to know first whether the boat would pass the place where he had entered the water.

It would not.

I left her board wrapped in my cloth.

"Is there anything else?" the owner asked.

"No," I said.

He looked surprised. "You have been here three mornings. I thought you would ask to come."

"I would like to."

"That is not an answer."

"I will not come unless you agree."

He glanced at his rowers. The cousin was already sitting at the stern, one hand on the steering paddle. The boatman had stowed a water jar beneath the forward seat.

"I do not agree," the owner said. "Two men can carry the question. A third would use food and space we did not count."

"Then I will stay."

He nodded once and turned to the bow line. I had hoped that saying it would cost me less than it did. I wanted to see the cut. I wanted to ask the trap keeper myself. I wanted to be present when someone finally spoke a word that changed Nasir's place on the board. The boat did not belong to that wanting.

Tahir came down with his nephew. The young man's injured hand was still bound, but he could now hold the board against his chest without wincing. Tahir read the names of the two rowers aloud. He marked the boat's intended bank and the time of departure.

"What if they come back after dark?" the nephew asked.

"Then we will hear them when they come," Tahir said. "We will not mark them missing at sunset."

Nasir's sister looked at him. "Good."

The owner pushed the boat into the water. The rowers stepped in one at a time, keeping their weight low while the hull rocked. The boatman set the packet for the witness under a mat. The cousin tested the paddle against the current.

"One journey," the owner called. "No cargo on the return unless you have room and I have agreed."

The boatman raised his hand. They moved out from the landing, first sideways, then forward when the current took the bow. The cousin corrected with two strokes. They passed the first post and turned toward the channel that led to the traps.

Nasir's sister watched until the boat was beyond the bend.

"Now we wait," I said.

"We work," she replied. "Waiting does not dry the fish."

She went back to her stall. The boy followed with the empty wrapping cloth. I remained until the boat had disappeared.

Jamil found me beside Tahir's boards.

"Hasan says you prepared an account," he said.

"I prepared two. One for the inquiry that has gone. One for the people who may still be on the outer water."

"Show me the second."

I unwrapped it. I had kept the names apart according to the last place each person had been seen. The woman in blue and the younger woman with the injured ear were listed among those last seen aboard the foreign ship. The older woman's son was recorded as having entered the water from the junk. The junk's missing sailor had been taken away and not returned when the master left. Nasir's name stood alone beneath the cut, with two witnesses who had not seen him reach the bank.

Jamil read the headings. He did not ask why I had made them.

"Which of these people have you promised to recover?" he asked.

"None. I have told their families what I know."

"Which families have agreed to let you carry their questions?"

"Nasir's sister has given hers to the boatman. The older woman asked about the bank where her son swam. I have not asked the other families for permission to send anything in their names."

"Then do not send it yet."

"I won't."

He turned the board over. On the back Hasan's clerk had marked the two owners' terms for journeys they might make later, leaving blank the places they had not agreed to visit. One was willing to carry messages upriver. Another might go to the outer settlements after his cargo had been delivered. Neither would cross to the foreign ships. I had not written Ruma's name beside either offer.

"Who pays?" Jamil asked.

"Hasan has paid for the inquiry already under way. The other journeys have no payment yet. I have no claim on the merchant's store."

"Who would hear the answers?"

"The households that sent the questions. Hasan's clerk can keep copies if they agree."

"Who would decide when a boat turns back?"

"The owner and the man he puts in charge of it."

"Not you?"

"Not unless an owner chooses me for that work."

Jamil looked toward the bend where the inquiry boat had gone.

"You could have made this account before the foreign ships came," he said.

"I did not think I needed to."

"No."

He held the board out to me.

"Hasan may use you to carry questions between the families and his clerk. He will tell you which ones. You will be paid for work done, not for a title. If an owner asks you to sail, you will bring his terms to Hasan before claiming any court authority. That is what I can offer."

"Will the ruler agree?"

"He has agreed that the inquiry must continue. He has not restored your command. Do not make my words into his."

"I understand."

"I hope you do."

He started to leave, then stopped.

"The merchant's son returned yesterday. His account of the gold is different from his father's. Hasan will hear him. You are not to settle the difference by repeating what you saw aboard the ship."

"I couldn't."

"You have tried to settle other things you could not know."

I had no answer. He went up the path toward the storehouse.

The work he had offered was smaller than the work I had once held. It would not put a sail under my hand or a captain's place beneath my feet. It would let me carry questions from people who wanted them carried. I could do that without taking a boat from anyone.

At midday I found the older woman beside the repaired junk. The master and his workers had lifted the damaged plank free. The gap in the hull was wider than it had looked from the water, and the new timber lay beside it, still rough along one edge. The boy had been sent to bring resin from a household beyond the market. The injured sailor remained under Ruma's care.

I told the older woman that the inquiry boat would not pass the place where her son had swum.

"Then it will bring me no news," she said.

"Not today."

"Will another boat go there?"

"I have no agreement for one."

She ran her thumb along the handle of her basket. "The master says he may have to leave before the plank is finished if the men cannot be paid. He wants to find another place to work. If he goes, I must decide whether to stay and look for my son or travel with the people I know."

"What will you do?"

"I will decide when he tells me where he can take me."

She looked at the open hull. "You have been telling people you may go to Luzon."

"I have asked about passage."

"Then you may leave before I know whether my son is alive."

"Yes."

"Do not say you will remember him as though that is the same as looking."

"I won't."

She took her basket and walked toward the eaves. I watched her go. I could not promise to remain until her question was answered. I could promise to leave the question where another person could find it, with the name of the one who had asked.

Ruma returned late in the afternoon with a bundle of baskets balanced across her boat. She had gone upriver and back on the same tide, collecting part of the payment she was owed. Her crew unloaded the baskets before she stepped ashore. She saw me beside the posts.

"The owner I told you about has sent word," she said.

"Is he coming?"

"After the next market, if the water is high enough. He may have room for one passenger bound toward the mouth. He has not said where he will go after that."

"Will he speak to me?"

"If you are here. I told him you wanted to ask."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me for a place he has not given you."

She handed me a small bundle of cord to carry up to the storehouse. I took it. She went back to her crew to count the baskets.

When the sun dropped behind the roofs, the inquiry boat had not returned. Tahir left its name on the board beneath the heading for boats out on agreed journeys. He had not moved it to the missing list. Nasir's sister brought him a little food and asked whether anyone had seen the boat beyond the bend. No one had. She did not ask him to change the mark.

I carried Hasan's questions between the landing and the storehouse until there was no light left to read them. The board with the names of the missing stayed wrapped beside me. I had made no promise that the woman in blue would come home, or that Nasir had reached the bank, or that the older woman's son was dead. The names remained.

The owner Ruma knew might take me toward Luzon. Jamil would let me work in Burné without giving me back the authority I had lost. Neither path was closed. Neither was ready.

Below the last steps, the water turned and began to rise again. I watched for the small boat from the traps. I could not see it yet.
