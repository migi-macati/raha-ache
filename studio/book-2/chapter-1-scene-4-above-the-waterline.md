# Book II, Chapter 1 — Scene 4: Above the Waterline

The boy from the waiting boat brought us a basket of his own before the owner of the bowls had gone far enough to be out of sight. His was filled with lengths of split wood, each piece dark where it had rested in the bilge. He put it down where the cracked bowl had been.

“Not there,” the woman said.

He lifted it again. The bottom dripped onto the planks.

“Where, then?”

“Where someone can pass.”

He looked along the platform. There was scarcely a place left that did not belong to somebody's goods. Daran came and showed him a patch of dry boards beside the wall. The boy took the basket there and began laying the wood out to be counted.

The young carrier who had been refused his wages watched him work. He had stopped asking the owner for payment once the boat moved away. He had not stopped watching the water.

I sat with the first basket of bowls and the cord Daran had given me. The cracked bowl lay inside it, with the loose piece of rim nested against its side. I could see the place where the owner had pressed his thumb. I could not tell whether he had made the crack worse.

A line of wetness crept across the board beneath the basket. At first I thought it came from the boy's wood. Then I saw water lifting between the planks.

“Daran.”

He was helping the boy arrange his pieces by length. He glanced over.

“The water is coming up.”

“It does that.”

“It will reach the basket.”

He came closer, bent, and touched the board with two fingers. When he straightened, he looked toward the woman.

“Move the first basket?”

She had been watching a pair of men bring sacks through the rear passage. She came at once. The water had not reached the covering, but the bottom of the basket was damp.

“Into the room,” she said.

I put my hands on the handles.

“Wait,” Daran said.

I let go. He did not explain. He looked at the older keeper of the cords, who was sitting beyond the water jar.

“The owner left one basket until the next tide,” Daran told him. “We are taking it inside before the water reaches it.”

The old man came over slowly. He looked into the basket and counted the bowls that could be seen without lifting them.

“You did not open it again?” he asked.

“No,” I said.

“The covering was left open,” the woman said. “The owner saw that.”

The keeper looked at the carrier. “You were here when he left?”

“Yes.”

“Did you see anyone touch the broken bowl after that?”

“The grandson sat beside it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

The carrier looked at me. He had been watching the water as often as I had.

“I saw no one take it out,” he said.

The keeper nodded, but he did not reach for a cord.

“Now move it.”

Daran took one handle. I took the other. The basket was lighter than the loads I had carried in the mornings before this dispute, but it was awkward because we had to keep it level. The cracked rim knocked softly against another bowl when we lifted.

“Slow,” the woman said.

We stopped. Daran looked at the broken piece. It had shifted but had not fallen.

“Is it worse?” I asked.

“I do not know.”

We carried it through the narrow passage. The room beyond smelled of damp matting and the sweet dust of old packing leaves. Baskets were stacked along the wall, some empty and some covered. The woman pointed to a low stand.

“Here. Nothing above it.”

We set the basket down. I had thought the room dry. A dark line ran along the base of the wall, where water had reached it on other days. The stand was higher than the line.

The woman looked at the broken bowl, then at the passage.

“Tell the owner where it is when he returns.”

“I will.”

“Tell him we moved it.”

“I will.”

“And tell him why.”

I nodded.

“Do not tell him we kept it untouched,” she said. “We did not.”

She went back to the platform before I could answer.

Daran knelt to check the basket's bottom. A few wet fibers clung to his fingers.

“You were right about the water,” he said.

“I almost moved it alone.”

“You would have moved it to keep it safe.”

“And then?”

“And then someone would have asked whether you broke it while moving it.”

He rubbed the wet fibers from his hand. “Now they can ask both of us.”

“That is not much better.”

“No. But there are two of us.”

He rose and went to help the men in the rear passage. I stayed by the basket. Outside, the boy was calling out the lengths of his wood. I heard the older keeper repeat each count. Their work had not stopped because a merchant was angry.

The carrier appeared in the doorway. He did not enter.

“Did you move it?”

“Yes.”

“Did it break more?”

“I don't know.”

He looked past me at the bowl.

“I should have watched.”

“You were watching.”

“Not every moment.”

He leaned against the doorpost. His shoulder was bare where the carrying strap had rubbed it. There was a narrow patch of skin near his collarbone that looked raw.

“Will you work for him again?” I asked.

“For whom?”

“The owner.”

He looked at me as though I had asked whether he would work for the tide.

“If he pays what he said.”

“And if he doesn't?”

“Someone else may need hands.”

“Here?”

“Here, or somewhere else.” He glanced toward the platform. “You have a place already.”

“For now.”

“Then for now you have a place.”

I had been thinking of my mother's message again. It had been spoken and witnessed, but it still waited in Siripada's house for a man who might carry it. The merchant had offered the possibility of a contact and taken it away when I would not give him the words he wanted. I had lost a hope. This man had lost the use of his hands for the better part of a morning, and might lose the wage he had already earned.

“Do you have someone waiting for the payment?” I asked.

“Do you?”

The question startled me.

“Yes,” I said, before I thought. Then I shook my head. “No one waiting here.”

He nodded once. He did not ask whom I had meant.

A man came through the passage with a coil of rope over his shoulder. The carrier moved aside to let him pass. When he settled back against the post, he was farther from me.

“If the owner says you broke it,” I began.

“He has said it.”

“If he says we broke it moving it inside—”

“Then he will have two stories.”

I looked at the bowl. The little piece of rim lay where the woman had placed it, inside the curve.

“Which one will people believe?”

“Whichever one they hear from the man who pays them.”

He said it without anger. I wanted to contradict him. I had nothing with which to do so.

From the platform came a sharp call. The woman was asking who had left a wet sack across the passage. Daran answered. The sack was moved. The boy with the wood began counting again.

The carrier straightened.

“I will go to the other landing.”

“Will you come back?”

“When the owner does.”

“How will you know?”

“He said the next tide. Everyone here knows when that is.”

“I can tell him you waited.”

He studied me for a moment.

“You can tell him I waited until now,” he said. “You cannot tell him I waited when I wasn't here.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He went down the passage. I heard his steps on the planks and then lost them among the men carrying the sacks.

I remained with the basket until Daran returned. He brought a narrow strip of matting and put it beneath the wet side.

“Did the carrier leave?” he asked.

“He is going to the other landing.”

“He has work to find.”

“He said he would come back.”

“Perhaps he will.”

Daran turned the basket slightly so that the damp side faced the open air. He did not touch the bowls.

“You can go and eat,” he said.

“The woman told me to stay with it.”

“She told you to keep it. I can keep it while you eat.”

I hesitated. I had begun to treat sitting beside the basket as if it were a duty no one else could perform. The thought embarrassed me, but I still wanted him to ask me to stay.

“Did you eat?” I asked.

“Before the boy brought the wood.”

I stood. My knees were stiff from the boards.

At the outer steps the water had risen high enough to wash over the lowest plank. The boy's boat had gone. Another had taken its place. Men were moving without shouting, passing loads along a line that left the center of the platform clear. The woman stood where she could see both the landing and the rear passage.

She gave me a small portion of rice wrapped in leaf. I ate beside the wall and watched her settle a disagreement over which sacks belonged to which boat. She asked the men to identify their own loads, then sent each to a different place. No one invoked Siripada's name.

When I finished, she was still working.

“The basket is inside,” I told her.

“I know. I sent you there.”

“Daran is with it now.”

“I know that too.”

I held the empty leaf between my fingers.

“The carrier went to find work.”

“He is free to go.”

“He says he will return at the next tide.”

“Then we will see him if he does.”

“What if the owner comes first?”

“Then we tell him what happened here.”

“He may say we broke it.”

She looked at me. “Did we?”

“We moved it.”

“That is what we did. Do not make a second quarrel before the first has ended.”

She took the leaf from my hand and pointed me toward the place where the waste was kept. I went.

By the time I returned, a boat was approaching the outer steps against the current. Its stern moved sideways before the poleman brought it straight. I recognized the owner's folded shoulder cloth before I could see his face.

The woman saw him too. She called Daran's name, then looked at me.

“Bring the keeper,” she said. “And remember where the basket was when we found the water.”

I went to fetch the old man. The owner was already calling from the boat, asking why his goods were no longer on the platform.
