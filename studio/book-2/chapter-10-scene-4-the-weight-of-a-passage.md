# Book II, Chapter 10 — Scene 4: The Weight of a Passage

**Packet:** RA-B2C10-SCENE-04. **State:** Studio prose draft, not reader-facing. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Continuity:** Follows Scene 3. No owner, crew, provisions or political permission were committed when Ache left Siripada. Nasir's paid inquiry remains unresolved. **Historical status:** Fictional maritime agreements; terms are narrative inventions, not documented 1521 Brunei contracts.

---

The owner came after the market, when the boats that had brought vegetables were already leaving with their baskets empty.

Ruma pointed him out from the landing. He was kneeling beside a low cargo vessel, drawing a cord beneath its bow while two men held the other end on the bank. The hull had been dragged high enough to show a pale scrape where the bottom had touched stone. A boy with a pot of resin stood behind him.

"That is Naim," she said. "He goes toward the mouth."

"Does he go farther?"

"Ask him. I have told you what he told me."

She carried her own basket past him and went to settle an account. I waited until Naim had finished measuring. He took the cord from the men and made a mark on a short piece of wood.

"Ruma said you might speak with me," I said.

"She said you wanted to travel."

"To Luzon."

He looked at my feet, then at the clean cloth Jamil had given me for the palace. The hem was already stained from the yard.

"My boat goes to the outer settlements," he said. "After that I return with fish and timber. It is too small to carry your quarrel."

"I have not told you my quarrel."

"You have told enough people that it has reached me."

I crouched beside the scraped plank. A seam had opened where the bow rose from the keel. Resin had been pressed into it once, and the new crack ran through the old repair.

"Will you replace it?" I asked.

"Would you take passage if I did not?"

"No."

"Then perhaps we agree on something."

He stood and told the boy to fetch a broader pot. The boy brought the same one back. Naim turned it upside down. A finger's width of resin fell into the sand.

"We need more than that," he said. "And the man who sells it wants payment before he gives it to us."

I offered to carry the pot to the market. Naim shook his head.

"Money first. Carrying it will not make it full."

I had come expecting to bargain for a place aboard. Instead I watched a man decide whether he could put his own boat in the water. I knew the cost of resin no better than I knew how much rice a crew would eat beyond the mouth. I had spent years learning how to read the counts after someone else made them.

At the next landing, two owners were examining a sail spread between posts. One pointed to a patch that had been sewn across a tear. The other pulled at the cloth until a row of stitches gave way.

"That is not my sail," the second man said. "It is yours now."

The first man swore at him. Neither looked pleased when I approached.

I asked where their boats were going. One said upriver. The other said he would sail north when the wind allowed it. His name was Harun. He owned the deeper vessel drawn alongside the outer posts, where three workers were taking jars from the hold and testing them for cracks.

"How far north?" I asked.

"Past the islands where my buyers wait. Perhaps to the coast of Luzon if there is cargo worth bringing home."

"I need to reach Maynila."

"Then you need more than a place on a boat. I can take a passenger to a coast. I cannot promise the people there will let him go where he wants."

I told him who I was. He knew. He asked what I would pay.

I named the amount of coin I still had. Harun looked at the sail on the ground.

"That would feed you," he said. "It would not pay for turning away from my buyers. If you want my boat to enter a river whose rulers may not welcome you, I need to know who will answer if the cargo is seized."

"I can give you a letter from Siripada."

"Will his letter replace my cargo?"

"No."

"Then it may help us speak when we arrive. It will not feed the crew on the way home."

I thought of the gold carried to the foreign ships. The merchant still claimed it had come from his chest. I had seen the metal and wanted, for an instant, to spend it again. I told Harun that the payment was disputed.

"Then it is not yours to offer," he said.

He turned to a jar with a hairline crack near its foot. He tapped it once and handed it to a worker.

"Put this one ashore. If it breaks at sea, it will wet the sacks beside it."

The worker said there were only four sound jars left. Harun told him to count again. They counted together. There were five. Harun gave the worker a look I recognized from Hasan's yard. It meant the count had been corrected and would be remembered.

I returned to Naim at the lower landing. His men had lifted the bow higher. He had sent the boy to borrow a wedge from a carpenter. He listened while I described Harun's offer, which was no offer yet.

"Harun will not risk his whole cargo for you," Naim said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He gave me a mallet and pointed to a block beneath the hull. We struck it into place while the others steadied the boat. When it held, he let go and walked around the bow to see whether the weight had settled evenly.

"Why do you want to take armed men?" he asked.

"I want people I can trust."

"That is a different answer."

"I may not be welcomed in Maynila."

"Then you must decide what you will do if you are refused."

I had told Siripada I would speak to the people there. I had not told Harun how I would keep a quarrel from becoming another Laoe.

"I would land outside the river first," I said. "Send a message. Ask for those who know me. I will not ask a merchant to force his way upriver on my account."

"Tell Harun that. Then let him decide whether he believes you."

At midday Hasan sent Tahir to find me. He brought a board with the names of the boats still out, and a message from Nasir's sister. The inquiry boat had returned from the traps. Its crew had found a broken paddle and two fishing baskets. Neither had a mark that proved who owned them. They had spoken to men on the far bank and brought back three names to be checked. Nasir's name was not among them.

"She has paid for another question," Tahir said. "She wants Hasan to hear the men separately before anyone tells the market that the paddle belonged to her brother."

"Does she want me there?"

"She said you could carry the names if you were here. She did not ask you to take a boat."

I took the board to Hasan's yard. I copied the names for the men who would question the witnesses and returned the original to Nasir's sister. She checked the marks while I waited.

"Will you be here when they speak?" she asked.

"I may go north."

"Then tell Hasan which names you carried. I will not have a man questioned twice because you have gone."

"I will."

"Do it before you leave."

She kept the original board. I went back to the boats without it.

Harun had moved his sail into the shade. I found him sitting with two men who had been measuring dried fish into baskets. One was his steersman. The other owned a smaller vessel that carried water and goods between the outer settlements. His name was Tamin. He had come to ask Harun for space on the northern passage, not to follow me.

I put my proposal before them. Harun would keep his cargo and his authority over his vessel. I would pay for my own food and for the men who chose to travel with me. We would seek a landing where his trading interests were not exposed to an immediate quarrel. I would send word toward Maynila before asking him to enter its river. If he judged the approach unsafe, he could refuse. I would not take his boat from him.

"What do you bring that helps us?" Harun asked.

"Knowledge of the river and the people who traded there when I left."

"When you left," he repeated.

"Yes."

"That is worth hearing. It may no longer be worth following."

Tamin said he could carry water to the outer islands and return with a cargo of salt. He would not go all the way to Maynila. Harun asked whether Tamin could accompany him as far as the northern stopping place, so the two vessels could exchange supplies if one lost a jar. Tamin looked at his steersman, who shook his head.

"Not unless we have enough water for our own return," Tamin said.

Harun asked me to count the jars that had passed inspection. I counted five. Tamin had three sound jars aboard his vessel. One had been promised to another household. We measured what each crew would need to reach the first agreed stop and return if the northern passage failed. The men disagreed about the time the winds would allow. We did not settle the weather by arguing.

I noticed that Harun had counted a jar as full when its stopper was loose. He had done it while speaking to me. I took it up and felt the wet line below the rim. The water had already gone down.

He frowned when I set it apart.

"You have made my count worse," he said.

"I have made it smaller."

He sent the worker to refill it and seal it properly. When the man returned, Harun asked me to repeat the route with the first stop and the place where he could turn back.

I did. He drew the line with a finger in the dust, then crossed out the direct approach to Maynila.

"This is the passage I might sell," he said. "A trading voyage that may carry you toward your home. No promise of a fight. No promise of a throne. You must find your own companions and bring their names. I will hear them before I take them."

Tamin agreed to discuss the first leg only after his crew had checked the water. He would take no letter from the ruler as payment for a jar.

I went to Jamil with two boards. One named what Harun was prepared to consider. The other named the goods and hands still missing. Jamil read both without speaking. He asked why I had left a space after the number of companions.

"Because none has agreed," I said.

"And the stores?"

"Not counted yet."

"And Harun's return cargo?"

"His. He will not pledge it."

Jamil set the boards beside his own. He did not say I had earned a ship.

"Go back and finish the count," he said. "Then I can tell Siripada what is real."

I went back to the landing. Harun's men were lifting the repaired sail into the late light. They had sewn a new strip where the stitches failed. The cloth held when the wind caught it, but the mast was still bare. I could see the shape of a voyage now. I could also see every space where someone had yet to say yes.
