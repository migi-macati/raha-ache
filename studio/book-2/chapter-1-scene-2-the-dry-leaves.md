# Book II, Chapter 1 — Scene 2: The Dry Leaves

The man who had tied the covering came up from the boat with a piece of leaf caught against his wet forearm. He was older than the carrier, with a narrow scar beside his mouth. The owner pointed to the cracked bowl before the man had both feet on the platform.

“Tell them how you packed it.”

“I packed the baskets,” he said.

“Were the bowls sound?”

“The ones I saw.”

The owner looked at the woman as if the answer had settled everything.

“How many did you see?” she asked.

The man looked back toward the boat. “I took them from the mat and put them in.”

“All of them?”

“There were others helping.”

“Who?”

He named two men. One was still aboard. The other had gone ashore before the unloading began. He spoke without lowering his eyes, but each answer made the owner's face harder.

The young carrier had moved into the narrow shade behind the post. He kept his hands under his arms. He was waiting for payment, and the waiting had begun to look like another kind of work.

The owner picked up the bowl and turned it until the crack faced us. The broken part of the rim lifted slightly beneath his thumb.

“This was worth something when it came to my boat.”

“Was it?” the woman asked.

“I paid for it.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He put the bowl down too sharply. I expected the loose piece to fall, but it stayed in place.

The man who had tied the basket knelt and drew a handful of dry leaves from between the bowls. Some were crushed almost to dust. He held one twist up to the light.

“We put these between the rims,” he said. “If one struck another, it would be quieter.”

“Quieter?” the owner said.

“So they would not strike hard.”

“Then why is it broken?”

The man looked at the bowl. “I cannot tell you.”

The owner turned toward me again. He had done so often enough that I had begun to stand straighter whenever his head moved. I disliked knowing that he could still make me do it.

“You saw the carrier stumble,” he said.

“Yes.”

“And you heard the basket strike.”

“I heard it set down.”

He opened his hands. “You can choose the words. The sound will be the same.”

Daran, beside the cord keeper, gave a short cough. He was not ill. He was warning me that the owner had made a space for an answer.

I thought of the men the owner said knew Maynila. If I pleased him, I might speak to one. I could ask about the river, the markets, the house my mother had been left to govern. I might hear something true. He had offered none of this, yet I was already measuring how much of my answer I could give away.

“I did not see the bowl before the basket came ashore,” I said.

“You saw how it was handled.”

“Part of it.”

The owner glanced at the older man. “Is this how Siripada's grandson speaks for your house?”

The older man tightened a knot and laid the cord across his knee. “He has not spoken for the house.”

The owner stared at him.

“One basket came onto the platform,” the older man continued. “The covering was opened here. One cracked bowl was found. I can put those things into the count.”

“And the man who broke it?”

“I did not see that.”

The owner took a step toward him, then stopped. His cloth had slipped from one shoulder. He drew it back into place with slow care.

“I cannot stand here until every man remembers where he put his hands.”

“Then leave the other two baskets aboard,” the woman said. “Or bring them up and let us open them while your men are here.”

“I have another landing to reach.”

She looked at the sunlit water. “Your boat has not moved.”

He called for the two baskets to be brought up. No one obeyed. The man who had tied the first covering was still kneeling among the leaves. The younger carrier stayed in the shade.

“Get up,” the owner told him.

The carrier came forward, but stopped beyond the cracked bowl.

“My payment first.”

“You have damaged my goods.”

“You have not shown that.”

The owner turned to the woman. “Now he will not work.”

“He has already carried one basket,” she said.

“Badly.”

“Then pay him for the work you say he did, or tell him plainly that you will not.”

The carrier's jaw moved. I could see that he wanted to say more, but he was looking at the other men in the boat. Whatever happened to him would be repeated there.

The owner took a small piece of silver from his fold and held it out. The carrier did not move.

“That is not what you said I would have.”

“You cost me a bowl.”

“Then keep the bowl.”

The owner drew his hand back. For a moment I thought he would throw the silver into the water. Instead he put it away.

The man with the scar stood and brushed leaves from his knees. “The first basket was tied before the boat came alongside,” he said.

“Of course it was,” the owner snapped.

“We had to shift it once. A pole came in over the side when another boat passed us.”

The owner rounded on him. “Why did you not say that?”

“You did not ask when we shifted it.”

“Did the basket fall?”

“No.”

“Did it strike the side?”

“I was holding the other handle.”

The young carrier laughed once, without amusement.

The owner looked from one man to the next. There were now more people who had touched the basket than he had wanted us to know about, and none would give him the one answer he needed.

I looked at the dark cord still holding the covering in place. I had seen its knot before the basket was opened. It had seemed important then. Now I understood that a sound knot could hold a broken bowl as securely as a whole one.

Daran came to collect the empty baskets I had left by the wall. He put one in my hands.

“Take it around,” he said.

I carried it past the steps. From there I could see the boats at the lower landing. Sulad was on the far side of a narrow hull, helping two men pull a wet mat clear of the gunwale. He had his feet braced against the bank. He did not look up.

I wanted to call him. He might know the boatman, or the carrier, or someone who had seen the loading. I had already begun to make him useful in my mind.

I set the basket where Daran had pointed.

“Do you know that boat?” I asked him.

“Which?”

“The one with the bowls.”

“I know it is holding up the steps.”

“Could someone have seen it loaded?”

“Someone did.” He nodded toward the man with the scar. “He is standing there.”

I looked back. The owner had drawn the older cord keeper aside. Their heads were close. The carrier remained unpaid. The man who tied the covering was turning the cracked bowl in his hands, no longer certain he ought to touch it.

Daran took the empty basket from me.

“You have a question,” he said. “Ask it of the person who was there.”

“I did.”

“You asked what you wanted to know. That is different.”

He carried the basket away before I could answer.

When I returned to the platform, the owner had moved away from the others. He called me toward the steps.

“I was sharp with you,” he said.

I had not expected an apology, and did not know whether he meant to give one.

“I have goods waiting in other places,” he continued. “I have men who must be paid. When one thing breaks, every man says he saw nothing.”

I looked toward the carrier. “He says you have not paid him.”

“I know what he says.”

The owner lowered his voice.

“There is a man who comes through here from the western waters. He has bought goods near Maynila. If he comes again, I can tell you where to find him.”

“When?”

“I do not know. Men come when the wind and their business allow.”

“Would he carry a message?”

“You would have to ask him.”

It was less than I had hoped for, and more than anyone had offered me since I arrived. I hated how grateful I felt.

The owner glanced toward the platform. “I only need you to tell the old man what you heard.”

“I have.”

“You heard the basket hit.”

“I heard it put down.”

“Those are the same words to anyone who was not here.”

“They are not the same to the carrier.”

The owner watched me. The kindness left his face without any change in his voice.

“Then perhaps the man from Maynila will have no reason to speak to you.”

He went back to the boat. I stayed beside the steps until the woman called me to move the last empty basket. The owner had not promised to introduce me to anyone. He had only shown me how easily a hope could be taken back.

Behind me the carrier asked again for his payment. This time the man who had tied the basket stood beside him. The owner told them both to wait.

The other two covered baskets were still aboard. No one had yet brought them up.
