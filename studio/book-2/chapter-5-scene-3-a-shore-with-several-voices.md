# Scene 3 — A Shore with Several Voices

By the time I learned which jar held the crew's drinking water, the man at the mast had stopped telling me to move my feet. He still moved them for me when I stood in the wrong place.

His name was Tarin. I had mistaken it for something Yusuf called him in respect, until Samad shouted it while the man was asleep and needed to take the sail. Tarin woke, cursed Samad, and came forward without looking at me.

The boat had been away from Burné long enough for my rice to taste of the sack. On the first evening I had counted the jars twice and written the same number both times. By the next morning one had been opened, and a man had drunk from it without telling me. I made a mark for what remained. Tarin took the wood from my hand and asked whether I meant to follow every man's mouth with a tally.

"Samad told me to keep an account."

"Of the goods. If we begin counting thirst, you can have my share."

He gave the wood back. I thought he was mocking me. Later, when a jar shifted and nearly crushed his foot, he made me hold it steady while he found another place for it. I did not ask how many jars there were after that.

We passed low land and then water in which I could not tell where one channel ended and another began. At night Yusuf named places I could not see. He had been this way before, he said, but he asked Samad where we would make our next stop, and Samad asked a man who had come aboard before we left the last shore. The man pointed with his chin, then took the steering place while Samad watched.

I had expected the voyage to make me useful. Mostly it showed me how much work the others did before there was anything for me to record.

One morning we came toward a shore where houses stood above a strip of wet ground. Small boats lay farther in, and two men were carrying baskets across a plank that bent under them. The smell of smoke reached us before their voices. Yusuf stood beside me and straightened his clothing.

"These people know me," he said.

"Do they know what you brought?"

"They know what I usually bring."

I looked at the list. Two of the spaces remained empty where the disputed bundles had been left in Burné.

"Should I tell them about the missing cloth?"

"Tell them what is aboard."

He went forward to speak to Samad. I could see he wanted to be the first man ashore.

The boat slowed before the shallows. A narrow craft came out to meet us. The woman at its stern wore a faded wrap tucked above her knees. She looked at our load, at the water under the bow, and then at Samad.

"You have come on the wrong side of the falling water," she called.

Samad answered that he would wait.

"You may wait there. You will not wait against my posts."

Yusuf leaned over the side. "Saliha. I have cloth for your household."

"I can see cloth. I cannot see who will carry it."

"Your people, as before."

"My people are carrying a roof today."

She pointed toward the houses. Two men had stopped with their basket and were watching us. Behind them a broad piece of thatch lay on the ground. I had thought they were waiting for cargo. They were working on a house.

Yusuf told her he would pay for the carrying. Saliha asked how much. He named an amount. She looked back at the shore, called to one of the men, and waited while he answered. Only then did she say they would take the first load when the water rose.

"Can we put the jars ashore?" I asked Samad.

"Not until she tells us where."

"The jars belong to Yusuf."

"The shore does not."

I had heard him say much the same thing about the boat. I was beginning to dislike the ease with which he found another owner whenever I wanted something done.

We waited while the sun moved above the roofs. Tarin made me help lift the smaller goods out of the way so the men could reach the water jars. Yusuf complained that the cloth would be late for its buyer. Tarin told him the buyer could come aboard and carry it himself. Yusuf did not repeat the complaint.

When the water returned, Saliha brought two men in her craft. They took a line and showed Samad where it could be made fast. I tried to help Tarin pass the line. It caught beneath a bundle and pulled it toward the side. Tarin seized the bundle with his foot and shouted for me to let go. I let go so quickly that the line struck my palm.

Saliha watched us from her boat.

"Is he your new sailor?" she asked.

"He is the man who writes down what I lose," Samad said.

I wanted to say I had not lost anything. The bundle was still aboard. Tarin showed me the mark the line had made across its covering. I wrote that down.

The first baskets went ashore under Saliha's eye. She named the carriers and told Yusuf who would receive their payment. He said he had an arrangement with a man named Karim.

"Karim does not carry baskets," she answered. "If he promised you my hands, ask him to lend you his."

She was not angry. She had work to finish, and she was telling him what it would cost to interrupt it.

I followed the third basket onto the planks with the list tucked beneath my arm. The wood shifted under my weight. I looked down and found that the gap between two boards showed the water running below. A carrier behind me waited without speaking. I stepped aside, and he passed with twice the load.

On shore I counted six covered rolls laid on a mat. Yusuf had listed six for this receiver. I put a mark beside them, then noticed that Saliha had set two apart.

"Those are for the house," she said.

"Which house?" I asked.

"Mine."

"Yusuf says the six are for Karim."

"Yusuf can say that when Karim comes."

She took a short cord from a peg beside the mat. Three knots had been pulled tight in it. She held it out for Yusuf to see.

"Your man brought this last season," she said. "He said the loss on the crossing would be paid when the next cloth came."

Yusuf took the cord and looked at it.

"That was another boat."

"That was my brother's boat."

"I did not own it."

"You sent the load."

Yusuf turned to me. "Write that the cloth is mine until the buyer receives it."

Saliha held out her hand for the cord. "Write that he will not let me have what his man promised."

I could hear the carriers moving behind us. One of them had set down his basket. The others were waiting to see whether they should bring the next load.

I had seen Yusuf put six rolls aboard. I had not seen the earlier crossing or the knotting of the cord. I knew which words I could write as facts, and which would only be the words of the people standing before me.

"I can mark the six rolls," I said. "I can mark that Saliha claims two for an earlier loss and that Yusuf disputes the promise. I cannot mark who owns them."

Yusuf looked at me as if I had turned my back on him.

"Then you have brought me a witness who cannot finish a count," he said.

"The count is six," Saliha said. "The argument is over two."

A young man who had been standing in the shade of a house came toward us. He wore no weapon. Two others followed him, though they did not walk as if they had been summoned. One carried a coil of rope; the other stopped to ask a carrier where to put an empty basket.

"Why is the cloth still on the mat?" the young man asked.

Yusuf's face changed. He greeted him warmly and gave his name as Jalal.

Jalal looked at Saliha before answering Yusuf. She told him about the cord and the missing payment. He listened, then asked whether the carriers had been paid for the loads already brought in. Yusuf said they would be. Jalal asked when.

"At the end of the receiving," Yusuf said.

"Then there is not yet an end."

Jalal smiled. It made Yusuf smile too, although neither man had agreed to anything.

"Karim will hear your claim," Jalal told Saliha. "I can let Yusuf keep the goods under cover until he comes."

"Under whose cover?" she asked.

Jalal glanced toward the house behind him. "Ours."

"And who will stand there when Karim says the rolls have gone missing?"

The smile left his face. He asked the man with the rope whether there was space in the raised store. The man said there was room if they moved the fishing gear. He would not move it until the owner returned.

Jalal turned back to Yusuf. "You may leave the goods on the mat until the shade reaches them. After that they go aboard unless someone agrees to watch them."

Yusuf protested that the receiving had already begun.

"It has," Jalal said. "That is why you owe the carriers."

I had expected someone like Jalal to settle the quarrel by speaking. Instead he had found another person whose permission was needed. I disliked that too, until I remembered the men carrying the rolls.

Jalal saw the list in my hand.

"Who writes for Yusuf?"

"I do."

"Then tell me what he brought."

I read the six rolls and the other goods we had counted. I left out the two bundles that had never left Burné. Jalal noticed the space in the list.

"Did you forget something?"

"Two men claimed the same cloth. It stayed behind."

"Then there are people waiting for it."

"Yes."

"And now there are people waiting for these."

He took the list, looked at the marks, and handed it back. "You have traveled a long way to learn how to keep people waiting."

He said it lightly. I almost laughed. Yusuf did not.

From the water Samad called my name. I went to the edge of the planks. He wanted to know whether he should send the remaining baskets ashore. I looked toward Jalal. Jalal looked toward Saliha.

"The carrying is hers," he said. "The boat is his. You are the one with the list."

I told Samad what had happened and asked him to hold the remaining goods. He asked whether Yusuf had agreed. Yusuf came to the edge and shouted that the goods were his. Samad said he would not unload another basket into an argument until he knew who would pay for its return.

The carriers began moving the six rolls out of the path of the other work. Saliha put the knotted cord beside them where all of us could see it. She would not surrender the claim, but she would not take the cloth by force while the count was open.

I went back to Jalal. He had stepped aside to speak with the man who carried the rope. I heard him promise to find another place for the fishing gear. The man shook his head. Jalal promised again, then stopped when the man turned away.

"Do you command this shore?" I asked.

"My mother's kin have a house here," he said. "That gives me somewhere to sleep."

"And the goods?"

"Karim receives them. Saliha's people carry them. Samad keeps his boat. If I could command all three, I would be sitting down."

He looked at my palm, where the line had raised a red mark.

"Samad has given you work."

"He has given me a place."

"That is not always the same thing."

I did not know whether he meant to insult me. He was already looking past my shoulder.

Three men had come along the wet edge of the shore from beyond the houses. Two carried spears. The third had nothing in his hands and walked ahead of them. People made room without running. Saliha put the cord beneath her mat, then stood beside the cloth.

Jalal said something to the man with the rope. The man went toward the houses, not toward the newcomers.

"Are they yours?" I asked.

Jalal looked at me sharply.

"No."

The unarmed man stopped at the end of the planks. He looked at the six rolls, then at Yusuf.

"Which of you speaks for the goods taken on the last crossing?" he asked.

Yusuf opened his mouth. Saliha spoke first.

"That is what we are trying to find out."

The man glanced at her cord, half hidden beneath the mat. He did not come closer. The two spear-bearers stood behind him with their points toward the ground.

I held the list against my chest. Six rolls lay between the water and the houses. I knew how many there were. I still did not know who could let them move.
