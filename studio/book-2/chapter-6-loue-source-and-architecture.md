# Book II Chapter 6 — Loue source gate and causal architecture

**Packet:** RA-B2C6-RESEARCH-01 / RA-B2C6-PLAN-01. **State:** preliminary Studio research and planning, not prose, canon or publication. **Working title in main:** *Tactics and Conquest*. **Title under review:** *The Other King's Shore*. **POV:** Ache, first person. **Chronology:** before the July 1521 encounter. **Immediate prior chapter:** Book II Chapter 5, *The Price of Passage*, public PR #51, pending final deployment verification when this packet was drafted.

## Source boundary

**Near-period reported account:** The existing Universe entry for Loue attributes to Pigafetta's 29 July 1521 account a report that a Luzon prince serving as captain-general to Brunei's ruler attacked and sacked **Laoe/Loue** because its people obeyed the ruler of **Java Major** rather than Brunei. Pigafetta did **not** witness the campaign. This is reported information, not an eyewitness description of its tactics, size, casualties, administration or even exact geography. Primary text must be inspected against a critical edition before precise quotation or choreography. Source record: `src/content/places/loue.md`, with link to https://philippinediaryproject.com/1521/07/29/29th-of-july-1521/ .

**Later retrospective account:** The existing Aganduru Moriz record claims that Ache gained ships and men in Brunei and pursued power before returning toward Maynila. The Spanish original and exact CODOIN passage remain uninspected. Aganduru's fleet counts and command title must remain attributed retrospective claims, not independently verified muster records.

**Geography:** Loue's modern location is unresolved. No automatic identification with Tawau or any modern port; no precise coastline, headland, depth chart, sailing time, or distance. The reported allegiance to Java Major does not establish a Majapahit garrison, fixed provincial administration, or the inhabitants' own interpretation of the relationship. A Java-linked merchant, local court or allied faction may make claims the Brunei ruler contests, but such actors are invented unless separately sourced.

**Fictional reconstruction clearance:** It is permissible to dramatize conflicting loyalties, local supply, a maritime expedition, the loss of noncombatants' goods and the politics of a sack. The particular councils, guides, leaders, crews, signals, negotiations, battle sequence and post-campaign obligations are fictional. Avoid imported nineteenth-century raiding systems, standardized vessel specifications and modern naval doctrine.

## Hard continuity gate from Chapter 5

Ache remains at a fictional eastern shore **without provisions for a return berth**, having voluntarily given his remaining food to compensate delayed roof workers. Samad sails with a witnessed cargo account for Hasan; there is **no guarantee** when it arrives. Five disputed rolls reached Karim, one remains under independent watch, and earlier loss/pay claims are unresolved. Jalal explicitly **refused to pledge other people's boats**. Nasir remains missing and his sister is to receive verified information directly from Hasan. Jamil has **not** restored Ache's passage-order authority.

The novel cannot jump from that ending to Ache commanding a Bruneian campaign. Show how he works for his passage, returns or obtains reliable communication, makes restitution or otherwise answers Chapter 4's liabilities, is tested again under other people's supervision, and earns the confidence of distinct decision-makers. A meaningful passage of time is acceptable and likely necessary; do not invent a calendar date before chronology is fixed.

## Six-scene causal architecture (provisional)

1. **The Cost of Coming Back.** Ache works at the shore for food and a limited berth, and must honor the older woman's and Saliha's remaining labor claims. Jalal may introduce him to a vessel owner, but cannot order that owner to accept him. A return or traceable message costs Ache further time and bargaining power. **Decision:** finish the obligation before claiming a favor. **End:** a narrow route back to Brunei becomes possible, with no political endorsement.

2. **The Account at Court.** Hasan and Jamil examine the separate accounts from Hamzah's loss and the eastern landing. Nasir's sister retains her direct channel; no sudden rescue. Ache faces the injured worker and the owners whose property was lost. Safi's competent independent service stands. Jamil can assign a bounded logistical task after a concrete test; no instantaneous restoration of command. **Decision:** accept an assignment where others can stop him. **End:** an actual Brunei dispute involving Loue reaches court, with rival versions of allegiance.

3. **Two Claims on Loue.** A Brunei sponsor demands action; a trader or envoy argues that Loue has obligations elsewhere. Local Loue interests must be intelligible and non-uniform. Ache tests the sponsor's claims against who controls supplies, boats and the approach. **Decision:** reject an easy but false single account of the city's loyalty. **End:** the expedition proceeds under an explicit limited mandate, not a blank authorization to plunder.

4. **The Unseen Side of the Headland.** The expedition's movement depends on a local guide, tide, stores and crews who may disagree. Ache earns a limited tactical decision by correctly identifying a constraint and paying its cost, while a rival commander or Safi has a defensible competing plan. Avoid a miraculous modern maneuver or unsupported exact ship type. **Decision:** accept the guide's refusal of a dangerous passage. **End:** an opportunity to enter or isolate Loue creates risk for inhabitants as well as fighters.

5. **What Victory Takes.** An organized attack or coercive entry succeeds, but the historically reported **sack** cannot be washed into bloodless brilliance. A crew takes goods or harms people beyond the intended objective; Ache must confront complicity and the impossibility of controlling all men by order. The opponent's resistance has rational grounds. **Decision:** protect a limited group or vital stores at a measurable military/political cost, without pretending the campaign was harmless. **End:** Loue is overcome; the victory leaves obligations, grief and enemies.

6. **The Other King's Shore.** Settlement requires terms for goods, captives, dependents, local authority and the claims that brought the fleet. Ache may earn greater command trust, but Brunei's political claim and the inhabitants' loyalties remain contested. Jamil or another superior assigns responsibility that can be revoked; no blanket sovereignty is proven by the victory. **Decision:** name what he cannot guarantee after winning. **End:** he departs at a new high point of authority, preparing Book II Chapter 7's encounter with unfamiliar ships, with the cost of Loue still attached to his reputation.

## Literary and ethical gates

- Avoid six scenes of accounting again. Chapters 4–5 already concentrated on cargo claims, testimony and labor. Chapter 6 must show a different scale: campaign mobilization, maritime movement, command under risk, violence, local resistance, and the moral price of victory.
- Ache remains capable of error, ambition and self-justification. His restraint cannot make him innocent of a sack. Show how he is implicated in a victory he wants.
- Give Loue's defenders and households motives that make sense without declaring a historically unverified ethnic or religious identity.
- Preserve Jamil, Safi, Jalal and working crews as independent agents; Jalal's support remains unpromised. No automatic romance or age assumption.
- The Chapter 7 first-contact reversal must feel earned by Ache's genuine competence at Loue, while remaining contingent on incomplete knowledge.
- Review title after full prose. Do not use abstract slogans or repeatedly moralizing dialogue. Prefer objects, sensory detail, spatial choreography and consequential choices.
