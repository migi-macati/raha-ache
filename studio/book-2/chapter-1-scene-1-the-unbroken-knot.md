# Book II, Chapter 1 — Scene 1: The Unbroken Knot

By the time I could tie the receiving cord without looking at my fingers, Daran had stopped watching my hands. He watched where I stood instead.

“Move.”

I stepped back. Two men came through with a basket between them, their arms stiff under the handles. They set it beside the platform and went for the next. Daran pulled the cord across his knee and waited until the woman at the far end had seen the load.

There were mornings when she spoke more than anyone on the landing. This was not one of them. She lifted the covering from a stack of empty baskets, counted what was there, and told a boy to take three back to the boats. He brought two.

“Where is the third?” she asked.

“The man at the steps wanted one.”

“Did you give it to him?”

“He said he would bring it back.”

She held out her hand for the cord. Daran passed it to her. She untied the last knot, looked at the boy, and waited until he went down to fetch the basket.

I had learned that she would not spend breath chasing a promise she had not made.

A boat was coming alongside the outer steps. Its hull rubbed against the post, and a man at the stern pushed it away with his pole. The owner had already come ashore. He wore a clean cloth over one shoulder, folded carefully enough that its edge had not yet been darkened by the water. He was talking to the older man who kept our receiving cords.

I knew his voice before I knew his face. He had come the day before to ask when his goods could be brought up. The older man had told him to come when the men and the water allowed it.

Now he was here, and the men were busy.

“Those first,” the owner said, pointing to three covered baskets in the boat.

The woman looked toward them. “What is inside?”

“Bowls.”

“How many?”

He named a number. She asked whether he had counted them before they were covered.

“My people did.”

“Then your people can tell us what we are taking.”

He frowned. “I have already told you.”

She looked at the older man. He said nothing. The owner called to someone in the boat, and a younger carrier came up the steps with the first basket balanced against his side.

I moved to clear his path. The basket was wider than the space between the rail and the post. He turned it, shifted his feet, and brought the near handle past the wood. The covering was tied down with a length of dark cord. I saw the knot as it passed my shoulder.

The boat moved against the steps. The young man bent his knees. For a moment the basket tipped toward me. I reached out, but Daran caught my wrist.

“Let him find his feet.”

The carrier steadied himself. The basket came level again. He set it down on the planks with a sound that made the owner turn.

“What did you strike?” the owner demanded.

“Nothing.”

“I heard it.”

“So did I,” the carrier said. “It was the bottom against the wood.”

The owner came across the platform. “Lift it.”

The young man lifted one handle. The owner pulled the covering back himself. Beneath it were bowls nested with dry leaves, their rims separated by narrow twists of fiber. The first bowl looked whole. He took it out and set it beside the basket. The second had a pale line running from its rim toward the base.

He pressed his thumb against the line. A piece of the rim moved.

“Look,” he said.

The carrier leaned forward. The owner held the bowl away from him.

“You dropped it.”

“I set the basket down.”

“You struck it on the step.”

“I didn't.”

The owner turned to the woman. “You heard it.”

“I heard a basket set down.”

“You saw him lose his footing.”

“I saw the boat move.”

He looked past her and found me.

“You were standing here.”

“Yes.”

“You saw it.”

I looked at the bowl. The broken piece was still held in place by the owner's thumb. He could have removed it with almost no effort.

“I saw the basket tip,” I said.

“And strike the step.”

“I didn't see it strike.”

The owner stared at me as though I had answered in a language he did not know. Then he looked at the older man.

“This one is the grandson,” he said.

The older man gave a small nod.

“Then he knows the cost of a careless hand.”

I had heard that tone in Maynila. It was the voice men used when they wished to remind me who I was while asking me to agree with them. I was ashamed of how quickly I wanted to give him an answer he would like.

The owner set the cracked bowl on the platform. “The boy's payment can cover it.”

The carrier's face changed. Until then he had been looking at the basket and the man's hands. Now he looked at the woman.

“I carried what they gave me,” he said.

“Did you inspect it?” she asked.

“No. It was tied.”

“Did anyone tell you to open it?”

“No.”

The owner spread his hands. “If every carrier opens my goods, who will answer when something disappears?”

“Then you cannot ask him what was inside before he carried it,” she said.

“I can ask him not to break it.”

Daran had not moved from the cord. I expected him to speak. He had once stood before me while I insisted he had lost a bundle that was lying where I had failed to look. I had apologized, but I still felt the shape of the accusation in my mouth.

The younger carrier stood with both hands at his sides. There was a wet mark across his shoulder where the basket had rested. He did not try to touch the bowl again.

“Who tied the covering?” the woman asked.

“One of mine,” the owner said.

“Which one?”

“He is still aboard.”

“Then ask him to come up.”

The owner did not move.

“We are wasting the morning,” he said.

“We will waste more of it if the next basket is opened after the men have gone.”

He called sharply to the boat. A man looked up from the stern but did not come at once. The pole had caught between the hull and the post, and he was helping to free it.

The owner watched him struggle. Then he turned back to me.

“Your grandfather receives men who bring goods here,” he said.

“He receives many people.”

“And you are learning how their goods are handled.”

“I am learning where to stand.”

He smiled at that, though I had not meant it to amuse him.

“I have men who know the markets across the water,” he said. “Some have bought in Maynila.”

I thought of the words waiting in Siripada's house. My mother's name had been spoken before witnesses, and no one had yet said who might carry it. The owner had not offered to take a message. He had not even said that one of his men would go there again. I heard the place and began making a promise out of it.

“Do they go there now?” I asked.

“When there is reason.”

“Could I speak to one of them?”

He glanced at the cracked bowl.

“We can speak of many things when this is settled.”

I felt Daran look at me. He had been silent long enough that I had almost forgotten he was beside me.

The woman set the sound bowl back into the basket. She left the cracked one on the plank.

“Nothing else comes out of that covering yet,” she said. “I want the man who tied it, and I want the other two baskets kept aboard until we know what is being handed to us.”

The owner opened his mouth. She went on before he could speak.

“I can tell you what came onto these planks. I cannot tell you what happened inside your boat. If you want to keep back the carrier's payment, tell him that yourself. Do not say this house decided it.”

The older man lifted his cord and drew the newest knot toward him. He had counted one basket brought to the platform. He had not counted a broken bowl.

The owner looked at the cord, then at the cracked rim. I could see him deciding which of those two things would matter when he spoke of this morning elsewhere.

“Ask the grandson,” he said.

The woman turned toward me.

“What did you see before the basket reached the steps?”

I could see the water beyond the post. I had been watching the boat come in, but I had looked away to move out of the carrier's path. I remembered the basket turning against the rail, the sudden bend of the young man's knees, Daran's hand on my wrist. I had not seen the covering opened aboard. I had not seen the bowl before it came out broken.

“Not enough,” I said.

The owner made a small sound of disgust.

Daran loosened his hold on the cord. The younger carrier looked at me, not gratefully, but with the same impatience he had shown the owner.

“Did you see me drop it?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then say that.”

“I didn't see you drop it.”

He waited.

“I didn't see what happened before you brought it up,” I added.

The owner turned away. He called for the man who had tied the basket, this time by name. A head appeared above the boat's gunwale.

The woman told Daran to keep the first basket where it was. She asked me to move the empty ones out of the path before another carrier came up.

I took them one at a time. I could hear the owner questioning the man from the boat behind me. The young carrier was answering too. Their words rose over the water and crossed one another until I could no longer tell which man had spoken first.

I had kept my hands off the broken bowl. I had given no one a reason to thank me.

When I returned for the last empty basket, the younger carrier was still standing beside the first. The owner had not paid him, and the woman had not sent him away. The knot around the covering was whole. I had seen it pass my shoulder before the bowl was found.

That was all I knew about the knot.
