# Book II Chapter 7 — Scene 5: A Sound Across the Water

**Studio prose, unpublished.** Ache first person. This scene invents a local rescue attempt within the historically attested attack on junks. It does not assert the identity or intentions of the foreign commanders, the number of casualties, or which particular junk carried Ache.

---

The first man to fall was not on the junk.

He had been standing at the end of the landing with a basket of fish at his feet. When the gun sounded he tried to run, caught his ankle in a mooring line, and fell against the boards. His basket went into the water. The fish floated for a moment before the current carried them beneath the steps.

He got up without help. I watched him look for the basket, then for the path home.

A second sound came from the foreign ship. This time I saw the smoke before I heard the report. Something struck the side of a junk. A splintered board flew into the water. Men on its deck threw themselves down.

Ruma caught my sleeve.

"Get away from the edge," she said.

"I can see the junks from here."

"So can the men who are firing."

She pulled me behind a post. A line of people was already moving up the shore path, carrying baskets, children and bundles they had not stopped to tie. A woman shouted for her husband. He was on a boat outside the landing, too far away to hear.

Tahir had climbed back onto his grounded hull. He was taking off the last of the rice. The youngest man was still bailing.

"Leave it," Tahir told him. "The water can have what we cannot carry."

The boy hesitated, then put the bowl down and climbed out. Tahir handed me the wrapped message.

"Give this to Hasan's clerk," he said. "Tell him I have not sailed."

"I will."

"And tell him my nephew's hand was hurt because I moved the boat."

"Because I asked you to."

"He can hear both."

I carried the message up the landing and found the clerk beneath the eaves of a storehouse. He took it and checked the binding. The outer cloth was damp but the inner wrapping held. He made a mark on his board and asked whether Tahir's boat could still leave.

"Not this tide," I said.

He did not ask whether I was ashamed. He had another message to protect.

Jamil was speaking to the harbor men near the storehouse. They had reports from three landings, each describing a different part of the attack. One said the strangers had taken a junk. Another said a junk had driven itself ashore. A third said the armed boats coming across the harbor had turned back.

"Which report is first-hand?" Jamil asked.

The harbor man could not tell him.

Safi came up the path with a wet sleeve. He had been helping turn a watch boat away from the moving ships. His rowers were waiting below.

"There are men in the water beside the nearer junk," he said. "I saw two. There may be more behind the hull."

"Can you reach them?" Jamil asked.

"From the shore side, perhaps. The current is running toward the outer passage."

"Can your boat carry them?"

"Three if they can sit. Fewer if they cannot."

Jamil looked at the men around him. Some were already being sent to keep the inner landings clear. Others were needed to carry messages to the ruler. He could not send every boat to the same place.

I knew the junk Safi meant. Its sail had been furled when we first saw it, and a patch of red cloth hung from its stern. A man from Laoe had used a similar cloth to mark his family's goods. It might have meant nothing. I could not stop looking at it.

"I can go with Safi," I said.

"You are responsible for the message boats," Jamil said.

"Tahir's message is back with Hasan's clerk. Ruma will not sail until the harbor clears. Banu has moved his goods by land."

"That tells me where the cargo is. Who will answer for the people?"

"Ruma can answer for her crew. Tahir for his. I can ask them to keep the accounts until I return."

"Ask them, then."

I went back down. Ruma had already moved her people under the houses, away from the water. She would take wounded men from the watch boat if it came back, but she would not put her repaired hull in the line of fire. Tahir agreed to hold the list of carriers and to tell Hasan's clerk if any owner left.

"You will not be able to order me from out there," he said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

I had no answer he would have believed. He took the board from my hands.

When I returned, Jamil gave me permission to help the rescue, not to command an attack. He made me repeat the distinction. Safi's watch boat owner, a narrow-faced man who had been listening from the steps, said he would take us along the sheltered side of the junks. He would turn back if the strangers came between him and shore.

"It is my boat," he said. "I decide when it has gone far enough."

Safi agreed. So did I.

We pushed off with four rowers. The water close to shore was full of floating wood and small pieces of woven cover. Farther out, a broken pole rose and fell with the current. One rower tried to hook it aside. The owner told him to leave it. He needed both hands on the oar.

The gun sounded again. The report came from beyond the junk, hidden from us by its high side. A man on the junk's deck stood up, looked toward the foreign ship, and disappeared below the rail.

Safi told the rowers to keep close to the shoreward side. The current caught us as we passed the stern. We turned hard enough that water came over the gunwale. I grabbed a bowl and began bailing.

"Not yet," the owner said. "Hold the side."

I put the bowl down and caught the gunwale instead. The rower nearest me leaned back. His oar struck something beneath the water and shuddered in his hands.

A man appeared beside the junk. He was holding a piece of timber and kicking with one leg. The other hung below him. He shouted when he saw us.

We came alongside, but the junk's hull moved toward us with the swell. The owner shouted to keep our hands clear. Safi threw the man a line. He caught it once, lost it, then caught it again.

I leaned over to pull him in. Safi seized the back of my clothing and held me.

"Wait until the boat rises," he said.

The man came close enough for me to see blood on his shoulder. He had a thin scar across one cheek. I did not know him. I had wanted him to be someone whose name I could carry back to Laoe.

We lifted him aboard when the hulls parted. He cried out and tried to stand. Safi told him to lie down. The owner counted the people aboard and looked at the distance to shore.

"One more," he said. "Then we go."

There was another man in the water, face down. A rower reached him with the end of an oar. He did not move. We pulled him close and turned him over. His eyes were open. I put a hand under his jaw to keep his face above water while the others brought him in.

He did not breathe.

I had carried dead men after the fighting at Laoe. I knew how a body changed when the person inside it was gone. I still waited for this man's chest to rise.

The owner turned the boat toward shore.

"Go," Safi said. "I will come with you."

I looked up at the junk. Men were calling from its deck. A rope ladder hung over the side. Someone was trying to lower a third injured man toward the water, but the ladder was twisting and he could not hold it.

"We can take him," I said.

"The boat is full," the owner answered.

"I can climb up and help them lower him. You can bring these two ashore."

Safi looked at me.

"You will be on that junk when we leave."

"I know."

"Do you know who commands it?"

"No."

"Then ask before you climb."

I shouted to the men above. One answered in a speech I knew only in pieces. I called again in the trade language used at Burné. He pointed at the ladder and made a gesture for me to come. I could not tell whether he was inviting help or asking me to take the injured man away.

The owner brought us close once more. I caught the ladder when the hulls parted, put my foot on the lowest rung, and climbed.

The ladder twisted beneath me. For a moment I hung with my back against the junk's side and water beneath my feet. A man above caught my wrist and pulled until I could reach the rail.

I landed on a deck slick with spilled water and broken pottery. The man who had helped me up had a cut above his eye. He pointed to the injured sailor, who lay against a basket with one leg twisted beneath him.

I knelt to help. From the far side of the junk came another gunshot, followed by a crack that ran through the boards beneath us.

The men on deck began shouting toward the stern. I could see Safi's boat turning for shore with its two wounded men. The owner had kept his word.

Between the junk and the landing, another vessel moved across the water. It was low and crowded, its crew trying to turn against the current. For a few moments it hid the watch boat from me.

When it passed, I saw the open water where Safi had been. The junk had begun to drift, and the gap between us and the shore was growing.

I had climbed aboard to help lower one man. I was now on the wrong side of a vessel I did not command, while the strangers' guns were still sounding beyond it.
