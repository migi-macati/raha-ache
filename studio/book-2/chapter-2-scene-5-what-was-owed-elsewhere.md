# Book II, Chapter 2 — Scene 5: What Was Owed Elsewhere

The boy was still looking at the two marks when the woman's brother came back.

He had nothing in his hands. I knew what that meant before his sister asked.

"The man who sent us has gone to the work," he said. "The people at his house don't know whether he found another boat."

She put the covering back over the smaller basket. The leaves had darkened where the food pressed against them.

"Then we take these home."

"They may still want them."

"They wanted them before the men stopped for food."

Her brother lifted the heavier basket. She took the other herself. When I offered to help, she turned her shoulder away.

"You have helped me carry them already."

The boy waited until they had passed the corner. Then he put his board on his knee and made a short cut beside each of the two marks. He held it up for me to see.

"Refused?" he asked.

"Yes."

"By the owner?"

"Yes."

"And the rest?"

I knew what he meant. He had heard me tell the woman why she had waited. He was asking how much of that answer belonged on his board.

"Ask Safi what the marks are for," I said.

The boy gave me a look I had begun to recognize. It was the look he wore when an older person told him to find another older person.

"You're the one who told me to put them there."

I could have told him to make another cut. I had already learned what happened when I used a board I did not keep to make a decision I did not own.

"Tell him what happened," I said. "Don't say I sent you to change it."

He went toward the landing with the board against his ribs.

The boat was almost ready. The mender had drawn the covering tight along one side and was feeling beneath it for the fastening he had missed. Safi stood near the stern, speaking to the owner. Neither looked toward me.

I went through the yard behind the receiving house, where a woman was counting small bundles on a mat. Each was tied with a strip of leaf. Another woman had opened a covered jar beside her and was drawing out dried fish in pieces no longer than her hand.

"Six for the lower steps," the first woman said.

"That is what you told me yesterday."

"Yesterday there were six men."

"Today there are eight."

The first woman looked at the bundles, then toward a figure standing at the far side of the mat.

I had seen her in the larger house, though not near enough to speak. She wore a plain outer cloth, with a narrow band at the edge that had been worked more carefully than the rest. She had been listening without moving the bundles.

"How many were promised?" she asked.

"Six," the first woman said.

"And who sent the other two?"

"Jamil asked for men to finish the fastening before the boat went."

"Then someone should have told me before I sent the food."

The woman by the jar folded her arms. "We have enough fish. The rice is for the evening household."

"How much rice can be taken without leaving the people here short?"

"I can tell you what is in the pot. I cannot tell you what the cook will permit."

The figure beside the mat looked up and found me standing in the passage.

"You have been watching for some time."

"I was passing through."

"Then pass through. We are counting food."

I might have done as she said if I had not recognized her. Someone had called her Laila in the house, and a servant had moved aside when she came through a doorway. I had imagined that someone with such a place would find it easy to ask for two more portions.

"Are those for the men at the boat?" I asked.

"Some of them."

"Jamil has finished deciding the load."

"That does not feed the men who finished it."

I looked at the six bundles. She saw me count them.

"There are eight men now," she said. "That is what I am trying to settle."

"I could carry a message to the kitchen."

"The woman standing beside the jar has already gone there."

I glanced at her. She looked tired enough to have made the journey more than once.

Laila lifted one of the bundles, tested its binding, and set it back. "If I take two portions from the evening food without asking, the cook will have to answer for them. If I leave the men hungry, the person who promised them food will have to answer."

"Who promised it?"

"I did."

She did not say it proudly. She sounded irritated that the answer had to be repeated.

"Can you tell the cook it is your decision?"

"I can. I would rather tell her how I intend to replace what I take."

The woman by the jar smiled at that, but only a little.

I thought of the two baskets being carried away. There was no way to make them useful here. Their food had been bought for different people, and their owners were already gone.

"I was looking for someone who might carry words to Maynila," I said.

Laila took her hand from the mat.

"From this yard?"

"From someone who knows the boats."

"I know who brings food to this house. That is not the same as knowing who will cross the sea."

"I thought you might have someone going west."

"Why?"

I had no answer better than her place in the house. I did not want to say it.

"My message has been waiting for days."

"Then it will still be waiting if I send it to a man who does not know where he is sailing."

"I am asking whether you know one."

"You came to ask me while I was counting food for men I have already promised to feed."

I felt my face grow hot. "I can wait."

"That would help."

She turned back to the woman with the jar. They agreed that the six bundles would go at once. The other two men would be told that food was being prepared, not that it was ready. Laila sent a boy to find the cook and ask what could be taken from the evening pot and what must be replaced. He repeated the request before he left.

I recognized the care with which she made him repeat it. I did not enjoy recognizing it.

When the six bundles were carried away, Laila stayed beside the mat. She rubbed a spot of dried rice from its edge with her thumb.

"Who told you I could find a carrier?" she asked.

"No one."

"Then you have been making guesses."

"Someone said a western trader might arrive."

"Has he?"

"Not that I know."

"Does he carry letters?"

"I don't know."

"Does he go to Maynila?"

"I don't know."

She looked toward the passage where the boy had gone.

"I have a small boat that crosses to my mother's people," she said. "It is used to carry food and people who have business there. If you ask me to send a message across that water, I can tell you who would take it and who would receive it. I cannot turn that crossing into a voyage to your mother."

I had not asked her to do so. I had wanted her to offer a way that I could not name.

A man came through the passage carrying a narrow board under his arm. His beard was threaded with grey. He stopped when he saw Laila.

"Your messenger found me," he said. "He said there was a question about the words used at the landing."

"Hasan," she said. "That question belongs to Ache. He can ask it himself."

I had seen Hasan speaking with men from more than one trading boat. He listened closely even when the speaker stumbled. I had thought that made him a man who could tell what people meant.

"I told a woman I would see that her baskets had a place," I said. "She heard that they would go. The boy who carried the words heard the same thing. I meant that I would ask."

Hasan set his board down.

"Which words did you use?"

I repeated them as nearly as I could. He asked what the woman had said before I answered. I told him that too.

"Did you say you had asked the owner?"

"No."

"Did you say the owner had agreed?"

"No."

"Did you tell her to wait?"

"She said she would wait. I let her."

"Then I can tell you what you did not say," Hasan answered. "I cannot tell her that she ought to have heard the words you kept in your head."

I waited for him to go on. He did not.

"The boy heard them differently."

"Did you ask the boy what he understood before he went?"

"No."

"Then we have three accounts, and you have brought me only yours."

Laila was looking toward the yard. She had not asked Hasan to defend me, and he did not seem inclined to do it.

"If we all spoke the same tongue," I said, "would this have happened?"

"Perhaps. People who speak the same tongue still promise more than they can do."

I had hoped that a difference between words could carry some of the blame. Hasan gave me no such comfort. He looked down at the board he had brought.

"Was anyone present when you corrected the promise?"

"The woman, her brother, the boy. Safi heard part of it. A man with a covering heard me say I had no authority."

"Then those are the people who know what was corrected. I was not there."

He lifted his board again. A man called for him from the passage. Hasan answered that he would come when he had finished speaking, and the caller went away.

"Can you tell me whether a trader will take my message?" I asked.

"Not without speaking to the trader."

"Can you ask him?"

"When there is one to ask."

He moved toward the doorway, then stopped.

"Bring Sulad when you go back to the witnesses. You said he has words of his own."

"I know."

"Does he know when you intend to come?"

"No."

"Then begin there."

He went out before I could ask another question.

Laila watched him leave.

"He enjoys making people state a question twice," she said.

"He gave me very little answer."

"He had very little to answer."

The boy she had sent to the cook returned with a woman carrying a covered pot. She set it down beside the mat and lifted the lid. Steam came out, and I could smell rice beneath it.

"The cook will give two portions," the boy said. "She says the dried fish for tomorrow must come from your store, not hers."

Laila's mouth tightened. "She was told I would replace what I took today."

"She said tomorrow is when she will see whether you meant it."

The woman by the jar laughed once, then stopped when Laila looked at her.

"Take the two portions," Laila said. "I will speak to her before evening."

"You said you would send six," the woman answered.

"I sent six. These are for the men who were added."

The woman filled two leaf wrappings and tied them. Laila did not take them from her. She waited while the boy who had come from the cook counted both aloud.

I moved aside to let him pass.

"Do you want me to go with him?" I asked.

"No. He knows where the men are. You would be another person to feed if you stayed."

I could not tell whether she meant to be unkind. The boy was already carrying the portions out of the yard.

Laila gathered the loose ties from the mat and placed them in the jar for reuse. She had found food for the two men. She still had to account for it to the cook, and tomorrow's fish would come from her own store. I wondered whether the cook would believe her when she came.

"I could speak for you," I said.

"To the cook?"

"To whoever asks why you took it."

"Would you tell them what you saw, or would you tell them I had a right to take it?"

I thought of the man with the covering and the boy with the board. I had been asked nearly the same question all day, though no one had used the same words.

"What I saw," I said.

"Then if someone asks you, answer that. Do not go looking for a person to persuade."

She took the empty jar from the mat and went into the house. I stayed long enough to hear the woman who had counted the first six bundles ask whether there would be enough for tomorrow.

"Ask Laila," the woman with the jar said. "She has said it will come from her store."

The first woman did not look satisfied.

I went back toward the landing. The place where the two rejected baskets had stood was empty. The boat was gone. The boy's board lay on the step beside him, its marks still visible. He was watching another load come through the passage.

I wanted to tell him what Hasan had said. I wanted him to know that there had been more than one way to hear my promise.

Instead I asked whether he had found Safi.

"Yes."

"What did he tell you?"

"To leave the marks until the woman had gone. Then to show they were refused."

"Did you tell him why?"

"I told him what you said."

I looked at the two short cuts beside the marks. They were smaller than the first marks and would be easy to miss unless someone asked.

"Good," I said.

He picked up the board.

"Is that what you wanted?"

I had come to the steps looking for a way to send words home. I had spent the day discovering how far my own words had travelled without me.

"That is what happened," I said.

The boy turned the board over and went to meet the next carrier.
