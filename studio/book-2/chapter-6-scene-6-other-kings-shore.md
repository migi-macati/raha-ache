# Book II, Chapter 6 — Scene 6: The Other King's Shore

**Studio draft.** Ache POV. Follows Scene 5, *What the Doors Held*. The post-sack settlement, delegated command and individual households are fictional; no source verifies a Loue treaty or the names of participants. Provisional chapter title: *The Other King's Shore*. Not published.

---

The first list of the dead had six names.

The second had nine. The third had eight, because a man who had been counted among the dead had been found beneath a house with his leg broken. Another name had been written twice. Hasan would have made us start again.

I sat on an overturned basket with a board across my knees, asking the men who brought the wounded which boat had carried them. A guard gave me the name of a man he had seen fall in the water. Another said he had helped the same man ashore. I sent them to find him before I marked him dead.

The men were impatient. Some wanted to return to Burné before the wind turned. Others wanted to go back among the houses and look for goods. Awan's receiver was still asking which bundles had belonged to his employer. No one had counted the goods in the boats yet.

Ruma came from the water with the woman whose child we had carried out of the fire. The boy's leg had been bound between two pieces of wood. He was awake, but he kept his eyes closed when men passed him.

"She wants to go back to her house," Ruma said.

"Is it standing?"

"No."

"Then where will she go?"

"She has kin beyond the houses. She will not tell us which house."

The woman looked at me. I had washed the smoke from my face, but my hands still smelled of it.

"Give me food for him," she said. "And let me go."

I called for a portion from the stores. The man guarding them said they were for the crews. I told him the boy had been hurt when our men entered the settlement.

"Then ask Jamil," he said.

I went to Jamil. He was standing beside a pile of goods that had been taken from the houses. Some were still wrapped. Others had been opened and divided. He had put two men there to keep anyone from carrying more away. One of them was arguing with a guard who said a jar belonged to him by right of capture.

Jamil heard my request and ordered food for the child.

"From whose share?" the storekeeper asked.

"From mine," Jamil said.

I wanted to say that I would give my share instead. I had eaten from Ruma's stores since we left. I had no share to give.

The woman accepted the food after she watched it measured. She would not accept a guard to escort her. She took the boy with the help of two women from the settlement, one of whom had come down to the water carrying a pot and a bundle of cloth. They walked away from us along the edge of the houses.

I did not know whether they reached their kin.

The next day the people who remained at the landing sent three speakers to Jamil. They did not come together. One was an older man whose household had paid dues to Burné before. He said he wanted the boats gone and the market reopened. Another had lost a brother in the fighting and demanded that the men who had entered his house be named. The third was a woman who had kept a store of dried fish for several families. The store was empty now.

"Will the ruler of Burné feed them?" she asked.

Jamil said the goods taken by our men would be brought for judgment.

"They have eaten the fish," she answered.

Awan's receiver said the people of Laoe had withheld his master's cloth. The woman asked whether her fish had been in the cloth bundles. He said no.

"Then why did your men take it?"

He looked at Jamil. Jamil did not speak for him.

I could see the receiver's anger. He had waited many days for goods he was meant to deliver. He had watched a boatman return without them. He had come to a shore where men threw spears at his companions. He wanted someone to pay for all of it. I understood that. I also saw the woman's hands. She had cut her palm on something and wrapped it in a strip of cloth.

The older man said that the settlement's obligations had never been agreed by every household. Some had sent gifts to Burné, some to men who claimed to serve the ruler across the water, and some had refused both. When armed men arrived, those quarrels became reasons for neighbors to accuse one another.

"Who will speak for Laoe now?" Jamil asked.

The three looked at one another.

"Not one of us alone," the woman said.

Awan's receiver demanded a name he could take back to his employer. She told him to take back the names of the people who had spoken, and the words they had said. He did not like that answer.

Jamil asked me to record it. I wrote slowly, with the speakers watching. The older man corrected the order of two names. The woman made me mark that she had not agreed to pay tribute, only to meet again when the boats were gone. The man whose brother had died refused to put his name beside either agreement.

I left his space blank.

At the edge of the landing, guards were bringing in two men who had been captured with weapons. One was young enough that his beard had not filled in. He had a cut above his eye. The other had a broken wrist. The guard leader wanted them held until the ruler decided what to do with them.

The younger man said he had defended his father's house. The guard leader said he had thrown a spear at the boats.

"Both may be true," I said.

The guard leader turned on me.

"Will you let him go?"

"I have no right to let him go."

"Then do not tell me what is true."

He took the men away. I watched them pass the pile of goods. The younger one looked at the opened bundles and began to shout. The guards pulled him along until I could no longer hear him.

That evening Safi found me beside the boats. He had been counting the men who could still travel. His arm was bandaged, though he said the wound was shallow.

"Jamil wants the cargo separated before we sail," he said.

"How much is ours?"

"That is the question."

We went through the goods with Awan's receiver and two witnesses from the settlement. The receiver recognized several bundles by their bindings. One witness said the cloth inside was not the same as the cloth that had been withheld. The other said he had seen those bundles carried into a house before the fighting, but could not say who had put them there.

Safi set the disputed bundles aside. He did not let the receiver carry them away.

A guard came with a small chest and claimed it had been taken from a man who struck him. The chest contained ornaments and two folded pieces of cloth. He could not name the man. Safi told him to put it with the goods whose owners were unknown.

"Then who will reward us?" the guard asked.

Safi looked at his bandaged arm. "Ask the men who sent you."

The guard left the chest and went away angry.

I remembered the bracelet on another man's wrist. I had seen it and said nothing. I found the man near the cooking fire and asked where he had got it. He said it was payment from a house he had helped protect.

"Who gave it to you?"

"A woman."

"Will she say so?"

"She has gone."

I told him the goods had to be brought for judgment. He laughed at me, then saw Safi coming and took the bracelet off. He put it in my hand as though it were a dead insect.

I wanted to throw it at him. Instead I carried it to the pile. The woman who had lost her fish was standing there, watching the goods being sorted. She saw the bracelet.

"That was my sister's," she said.

"Where is she?"

"I do not know."

I gave her the bracelet. She closed her fingers around it and asked who had worn it. I told her the guard's name. She repeated it until she had it right.

I knew she might go to Jamil. She might go to the guard's household in Burné. She might do neither. I had given her a name. It was less than what she had lost.

When the goods had been divided into those claimed, those disputed, and those without known owners, the pile of unknown goods was still large. Some men complained that we were keeping their rewards from them. Others said they had already taken what they were owed. No one could say how much had left the landing before the count began.

Jamil came to see the piles.

"Will this answer Awan?" he asked.

"It will answer what we have," I said. "Not what his men say was taken."

"And the people here?"

"Some want the boats gone. Some want their goods back. Some want the men who entered their houses named."

"Can you give them all three?"

"No."

He looked at the water. The tide was rising, and the boats were moving against their ropes.

"Then do not promise it."

The settlement's speakers returned before we sailed. They agreed that a smaller group could later hear the disputed claims at a neutral landing. They would send their own witnesses. Jamil would send Hasan's men. No one signed away the settlement's loyalty. No one agreed that the ruler across the water had no claim. The older man accepted that Burné would send traders again if it could protect them. The woman with the fish store demanded payment before any such traders used her family's landing.

Awan's receiver said the terms were too little. He had expected a victory to settle the question of whose king was obeyed.

Jamil told him that the ruler would hear his complaint when they returned.

The receiver looked at the burned roofs and said, "Then what did we win?"

I had asked myself that question all night. I did not answer him.

We loaded the wounded first. The men who had been held at Laoe were given places beside their own boat owners when those owners could be found. One of them refused to sail until he knew whether his brother was among the dead. We waited while Safi sent men to look. They found no body and no witness. The man finally boarded with his brother's name still unaccounted for.

I thought of Nasir's sister in Burné. I had promised to ask for names before leaving. I had not found every name.

Ruma inspected her load and told me she would take no more jars. Banu wanted one more bundle of goods aboard his vessel. Tahir showed him how low it sat. Banu moved the bundle to a smaller boat and paid its owner to carry it.

The wind was steady when we left. From the water I could see the place where the first roof had burned. The blackened posts stood above the tide. A woman was climbing a ladder to a house farther inland. I could not tell whether it was the woman whose child we had carried away.

Dima's mother had a landing somewhere beyond the curve. I had never seen it. I did not know whether the fire had reached her house. I would have to tell Dima what I knew and let him ask what I did not.

We returned to Burné by stages. The men with wounds needed shelter, and the boats carrying goods could not all keep the same pace. At one landing a boat owner refused to sail in a rising wind. I did not ask him to go. I helped move the wounded into shade and waited.

When we reached the city, the ruler heard Jamil's account before the merchants were allowed to divide the goods. The men who had been held at Laoe were presented. The names of those missing were read aloud. Awan received some of his claimed cloth, but the disputed bundles remained under watch. The settlement's speakers had been promised another hearing, and Hasan was told to prepare for it.

The ruler asked how the force had taken the landing.

Jamil described the first failed embassy, the later seizure of a boat, the approach chosen by the owners, Safi's fighting party, and the movement of the supply vessels. He said I had kept the stores together and brought a rope to the stranded boat. He said I had also left a jar on the path to carry a child from a burning house.

"Did you disobey an order?" the ruler asked me.

"I left the water I was carrying."

"Why?"

"The child was trapped. The jar could be carried by someone else."

"Was it?"

"Yes. I saw it taken."

"Would you do the same if your men were dying for lack of water?"

I looked at Jamil. He did not help me.

"I do not know," I said. "I would have to see who needed it and who could carry it."

The ruler watched me for a long moment. Then he asked Safi whether the stores had reached the fighting men.

"They did," Safi said. "Later than I wanted. Ruma would not take her boat into the shallows. She was right."

The ruler turned to Jamil.

"Can he carry a greater responsibility?"

"He can," Jamil said. "If the owners know what he is allowed to ask of them, and if someone can remove him when he forgets."

I felt the room grow still. I had wanted Jamil to say that I was ready. He had said that I could be stopped.

The ruler assigned me responsibility for a group of vessels carrying goods and messengers between Burné and the routes toward Luzon. Their owners would agree to each voyage; their crews would answer to their own masters at sea. I would be answerable to Jamil for the work of keeping them together, for the goods they carried, and for the people placed in their care. Safi would continue to serve with the fighting men. I was not given authority over every boat in the harbor.

It was more than I had held before Hamzah's loss. It was less than the command I had imagined when I first came to Burné.

Awan bowed to me afterward. He had never bowed so low. Men who had ignored me at the lower store now asked whether I would carry their goods. One offered to bring me a boat if I would put his cargo first. I told him to speak to its owner. He smiled as though I had made a joke.

Safi found me on the steps outside the audience house. He was carrying a bundle of cloth for the wounded men.

"You have what you wanted," he said.

"Some of it."

He looked at me. "You will want more."

I wanted to deny it. I thought of the men calling the landing ours, of the ruler asking whether I could carry greater responsibility, of Awan bowing to me. I had felt pride at each of those things. I still did.

"Yes," I said.

Safi nodded once. "Then remember who carried you here."

He went down to the boats.

That evening I stood above the water with the new orders folded beneath my arm. The vessels below had owners, crews, and journeys they would not surrender to me. I knew more about how to ask them than I had known when I first came to Burné. I knew how much could be lost after a good decision, and how quickly a man could call a bad one necessary.

I also knew that I had helped take Laoe. Its burned houses would not disappear because I had carried one child away from them.

The next boats would go toward the routes that led home. I had begun to imagine how I would be received in Maynila. The thought came easily. I let it stay longer than I should have.

On the landing below, a boatman called to his crew to move a jar before the tide turned. I heard the call and thought I knew what he would do next. For a moment I was certain I could tell him.

I stayed where I was and watched him choose for himself.
